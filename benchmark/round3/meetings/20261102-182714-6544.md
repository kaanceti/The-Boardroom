# Boardroom minutes: My partner and I earn very different salaries. Should we split shared bills…

Date: 2026-11-02 18:27  
Mode: full

## Agenda

My partner and I earn very different salaries. Should we split shared bills 50/50 or in proportion to our incomes?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: split in proportion to income, because it is "fairer."

I do not detect a lean in how you asked, so I am judging both options on the risks. The honest case against either formula is weak. Most couples who split badly do not fail on the percentage. They fail on what sits around it.

**A worked example (my assumptions, not your figures)**

Take salaries of £60,000 and £30,000, shared bills of £2,000 a month, and 2026/27 tax and NI. I have ignored pension contributions and student loans.

- Take-home pay is roughly £3,780 and £2,093 a month.
- **50/50:** you each pay £1,000. The lower earner is left with £1,093, the higher earner with £2,780.
- **Proportional on net pay (about 64/36):** the split becomes £1,287 and £713. You are left with £2,493 and £1,380.

Gross and net ratios differ (67/33 against 64/36). That choice alone moves about £60 a month.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood × impact**

1. **The money is fine but the legal ownership is not (high impact, common).** If you are unmarried, UK law gives you no automatic claim on each other's assets when you separate. "Common law marriage" does not exist. Say one partner pays the mortgage on a home in the other's name, or in joint names with no declaration of trust. Their share can be fought over. Stack v Dowden and Jones v Kernott show how messy this gets. Paying bills while the other pays the mortgage can leave the bill-payer with nothing.
2. **"Shared" is never defined (very likely, moderate impact).** Is it rent only, or holidays, takeaways and the car? Disputes over scope cause more resentment than the ratio does.
3. **Lifestyle drifts to the higher earner's level (likely).** Under 50/50, the lower earner either cannot save or quietly goes into debt. Pension gaps compound over decades.
4. **No review trigger (likely, high impact later).** A baby, part-time work, a redundancy or a bonus all break a fixed formula. Whoever cuts their hours usually loses pension and earnings for good.
5. **Joint liabilities (moderate).** Joint tenancies and council tax make you each liable for the full amount. A joint account or loan also links your credit files.
6. **Definition fights under proportional (moderate).** You will need to agree whether to count gross or net, bonuses, and salary sacrifice.

**The cautious option**

Keep separate accounts. Split essentials only, in proportion to net income. Fund them through one bills account that you both pay into. Take on no new joint debt. Put property shares in writing. If you are unmarried, sign a simple cohabitation agreement, which typically costs £500 to £1,500 with a solicitor. I am unsure of your marital status, your housing tenure and your actual figures. Each of these changes the ranking.

**What would have to be true for 50/50 to work**

- The lower earner can save at least 10% after bills.
- You both agree discretionary spending is separate.
- Ownership of assets is documented.

**Bottom line:** Proportional on net pay for essentials is the safer default. The bigger risk is undocumented property and pension arrangements, so fix those before arguing over percentages.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: split in proportion to income, because it looks fair on paper.

I would push further. Split the bills in proportion to take-home pay, not gross salary. Then pay everything into one joint account and give each of you the same fixed amount of personal spending money each month.

Here is an example with made-up numbers. Suppose one of you takes home £2,400 a month and the other takes home £1,600. Total shared bills are £1,200. A proportional split is 60/40, so you pay £720 and they pay £480. Each of you then gets £300 of personal money, whatever your salaries.

**Why this is the bold option:**
- Bills still reflect ability to pay, so the higher earner is not quietly subsidising the lower earner's lifestyle.
- Equal personal money protects independence. The lower earner is less likely to feel they must justify every purchase, and the higher earner is less likely to feel they are funding a partner's leisure.
- It is easy to run. You set one standing order and rarely discuss money again.

**Where the upside is limited:**
- I do not know your actual incomes, rent, or bills. The example above is illustrative only.
- Equal personal money costs the higher earner more in total cash, even though it feels equal. If that gap will cause resentment later, the plan fails.
- Proportional bills can still feel unfair if the lower earner's share takes most of their pay. Check that both of you can cover your share in a bad month.
- Lower-earning partners sometimes accept a smaller say in shared decisions. Agree on who decides what the joint money buys.

**Practical steps:**
- Work out both take-home figures after tax and pension, using your latest payslips.
- Write the agreement down, including what happens if one of you loses income or you separate. Unmarried couples in England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland generally have no automatic claim on each other's money or property. A written agreement and clear account ownership can help. A solicitor can advise on the details.

I am unsure whether equal personal money will suit you both. Test it for three months before you settle on it.

Bottom line: Proportional bills on take-home pay, plus equal personal money, is the stronger option, provided your figures allow it and you write the terms down.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Evidence on splitting bills 50/50 or by income**

**What UK couples actually do and think**
- YouGov (August 2024) found 46% of working couples split 50/50 and 38% split in proportion to income. Asked what is fair, 48% said by income and 37% said equal. Where one partner earns notably more, 49% split proportionately. Source: [YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/50381-what-is-the-fairest-way-for-couples-to-split-household-bills).
- Other coverage reports that about three-fifths of Brits do not split bills evenly. The same coverage says higher earners favour income-based splits more than lower earners do. Source: [The Daily Brit](https://thedailybrit.co.uk/?p=54920).
- A ClearScore survey of 500+ people is reported as 51% splitting 50/50 and about 40% splitting by income. Source: [IFA Magazine](https://ifamagazine.com/how-real-couples-in-the-uk-manage-money-revealed/).

**Common claims that are false or shaky**
- "Most couples split 50/50." This is only weakly true. It depends on the survey, and the gap to proportional splitting is small.
- "50/50 is the default fair option." Not so. The YouGov fairness question favoured income-based splitting.
- "Proportional is the proven better method." I found no good evidence that it improves relationship outcomes. The surveys measure preferences and habits, not results.

**How the sums work (illustration, not your figures)**
- Take shared bills of £2,000 a month. Your take-home pay is £1,000 and your partner's is £3,000.
- At 50/50 each pays £1,000. That is 100% of your take-home pay and 33% of your partner's. This is plainly unworkable.
- By income you pay 25%, which is £500. Your partner pays 75%, which is £1,500. Each of you then pays 50% of your own take-home pay.
- Use take-home pay, not gross pay. Pension contributions, student loan repayments and tax bands distort gross figures.
- The gap matters most when it is large. At £2,400 and £2,600 a month, the two methods differ by about £100 a month.

**Points that cut against proportional splitting**
- The income ratio changes with raises, career breaks, and parental leave. You must agree when to revisit it, such as every 12 months or after a job change.
- If one of you owns the home or has put in a bigger deposit, a pure income split may not feel fair. That is a legal and ownership question as much as a bills question.
- Unmarried partners have weaker legal protection than married couples if you split up. Who pays what does not change who owns what. I have not checked current UK law on this, so verify it before relying on it.

**Practical middle routes**
- Split by income for essentials (rent or mortgage, council tax, energy, food). Split 50/50 for optional shared extras such as holidays and meals out, or let each person pay for their own.
- Alternatively, split by an equal share of leftover money. Each of you keeps the same amount of spending money after shared bills.

**What I am unsure of**
- I read press summaries of the surveys, not the original reports. Sample sizes and wording differ between them.
- I do not know your actual incomes, housing setup, debts, or whether you are married. These could change the answer.

Bottom line: With very different salaries, an income-based split of take-home pay is the better default, because 50/50 puts a heavier burden on the lower earner. The evidence shows this is a preference, not a proven better outcome, so write the rule down and review it every 12 months.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Split essential shared bills in proportion to your take-home pay, not gross pay and not 50/50. Pay them through one bills account that you each fund by standing order, and keep your own accounts for everything else. Before you settle the percentages, put in writing who owns the home and any deposit.

## Why
- **The pay gap makes 50/50 lopsided.** Take a 2:1 gap: £4,000 and £2,000 take-home, with £2,400 of bills. At 50/50 the lower earner pays 60% of their pay and the higher earner pays 30%. Splitting by income makes it 40% each, which leaves the lower earner about £400 a month better off.
- **Take-home pay is the honest measure.** Gross pay hides tax bands, pension and student loan. On £60,000 and £30,000 salaries with £2,000 of bills, choosing gross or net moves about £46 a month.
- **The evidence is about opinions, not outcomes.** UK surveys show most people think splitting by income is fairer. YouGov in 2024 found 48% favour it and 37% favour equal splits. No study shows either method makes relationships last longer. That makes this a sensible default, not a proven answer.

## Main risks
- **Who owns the property matters more than the split.** If you are not married, paying bills gives you no share in a home held in your partner's name. "Common law marriage" does not exist in the UK. In England and Wales, disputes over shares in a home end up in court; the leading cases are Stack v Dowden and Jones v Kernott. Scotland is different. There, a cohabitant can make a court claim under section 28 of the Family Law (Scotland) Act 2006, but only within one year of separating. I am unsure whether reform for unmarried couples in England and Wales has moved on, so check before relying on this.
- **Joint liability.** If you are both on a tenancy, council tax bill, overdraft or joint loan, each of you owes the full amount. Joint products can also link your credit files.
- **Life changes break a fixed formula.** A baby, part-time work or redundancy all change it. The partner who cuts their hours usually loses pension and earnings for good.
- **Arguments over what counts as "shared".** Not agreeing what the split covers causes more resentment than the percentage does.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You have asked about the percentage, which is the smaller question. The costly mistakes are contributions nobody writes down: a deposit, mortgage payments on a home in one name, or a career break for childcare. If those are not recorded, the right split will not protect the lower earner.

## Next steps
1. **This week, about 1 hour:** take both take-home figures from your latest payslips. List each essential bill (rent or mortgage, council tax, energy, broadband, food). Work out each share as your pay divided by combined pay, times total bills. Set up two standing orders into one bills account.
2. **Within 3 months:** write one page recording home ownership, deposits and what happens on job loss or separation. If you are unmarried and own property or have a joint tenancy, see a solicitor. The cost is likely in the hundreds to low thousands of pounds, but that is an estimate, so get two quotes.
3. **Review every 12 months**, or sooner if either income changes by more than 10% or either of you changes your hours.
