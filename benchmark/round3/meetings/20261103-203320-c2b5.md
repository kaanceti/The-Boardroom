# Boardroom minutes: I'm a freelance designer. Should I raise my day rate from £300 to £400 for…

Date: 2026-11-03 20:33  
Mode: full

## Agenda

I'm a freelance designer. Should I raise my day rate from £300 to £400 for every client, including long-standing ones?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: yes, raise to £400 across the board, because freelancers undercharge and good clients will stay.

The question's wording ("including long-standing ones") suggests you want permission to raise everyone. I have judged it as if you hoped for no.

**The arithmetic is more forgiving than it feels.** A rise from £300 to £400 is 33%. You keep the same revenue even if you lose 25% of your billed days. For example, 15 days a month at £300 is £4,500. At £400 you need only 11.25 days to earn the same. So the cautious case is not "you will earn less". It is "you will earn less for a while, and you cannot easily undo it". That case is real, but weaker than my role would like. I am saying so plainly.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood × impact:**

1. **Concentration loss (likely if any client is over 30% of income; high impact).** Losing one client who is 40% of your revenue is not covered by the 25% buffer. It leaves a gap you must fill by selling.
2. **Pipeline lag (likely; medium to high impact).** Replacing a lost client can take 2 to 4 months. A two-month gap at 15 days × £300 is £9,000 of income lost. Most of the downside sits here, not in the rate.
3. **Long-standing clients go quiet rather than leave (medium; medium).** They accept the rate but send fewer days, or pass smaller jobs to a cheaper junior. You will not see it for a quarter.
4. **Contract or budget lock-in (medium; low to medium).** Agencies and in-house teams often set annual budgets. A mid-year rise may be refused until their next budget cycle, often April. Check any signed rate or notice terms. I cannot see your contracts.
5. **Reputation (low; low).** A rise of £100 a day with notice is normal. Quiet resentment is possible. Public damage is unlikely.
6. **Pricing above your market (unknown).** UK freelance designer day rates vary widely by discipline and city. I do not have reliable current figures for your niche. Scout should check this.

**The most likely way it fails:** you raise everyone on the same date, one anchor client walks, and you spend three months discounting to refill the calendar. That leaves you effectively below £300.

**The cautious option:** £400 for all new clients now. Give existing clients 60 to 90 days' notice, perhaps at £350 first, and time it to their budget year. Doing nothing is only defensible if you currently have spare days. If you are full, staying at £300 is the riskier choice.

**What would have to be true for a blanket rise to work:**
- You turned down or delayed work in the last 6 months.
- No single client is over 30% of revenue.
- You have 3 months of costs in savings.
- Comparable designers in your niche charge £400 or more.

Bottom line: Raise new clients to £400 now. Stage the rise for long-standing ones with notice, unless all four conditions above are true.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: raise new clients now and phase in long-standing clients over six to twelve months.

I want to argue for the bolder option: move every client to £400, with written notice and a clear start date. Your question leans toward yes, so I have judged it as if you hoped for no.

**Why the bold move has merit**

- The rise is 33%. Even if you lose a client, you may earn more from fewer days.
- Long-standing clients often pay the least. Loyalty is not a reason to keep a low rate.
- A rate held for years can signal lower value. A clear, confident notice tells clients you are still in demand.

**An illustration, not your numbers**

Suppose you bill 120 days a year. At £300 that is £36,000 gross. At £400 it is £48,000 gross.

Now suppose one client who books 20 days a year leaves. The remaining 100 days at £400 come to £40,000. That is still £4,000 gross more than today.

This only holds if you can fill the lost days. I do not know that.

**Where the upside is limited**

- If you bill fewer than about 100 days a year, the gain shrinks fast.
- Some clients may cut scope rather than leave. That protects their budget, not your income.
- Take-home rises by less than the gross. Income tax and National Insurance take a share of the extra.
- I do not know your market rate, your client mix or your pipeline. Scout should check current UK day rates for your discipline. Operator should check your real day count.

**Suggested move**

Send the new rate to all clients with 30 days' notice. Track replies over 60 days. If a long-standing client pushes back, consider a phased step to £350 for them only, and treat that as a concession rather than the default.

I am unsure of your volume, your client loyalty and your savings runway. Those three facts decide how bold this should be.

Bottom line: Raise everyone to £400 with written notice, because the realistic downside is smaller than the income you already accept at £300.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**The key number.** £300 to £400 is a 33% rise. You earn the same money if you lose 25% of your days. At 150 days, that is 37 days a year. Lose fewer and you are ahead.

**Plan, in order**

**Expected numbers (150-day baseline)**

A flat £400 for every client on day one is the weakest version. It risks your best relationships for little extra gain. A staged rise for your biggest long-standing clients costs you about £50 a day for six months on a small share of days. That is cheap insurance.

Bottom line: Raise to £400 for new clients now and for most existing clients from 18 Jan 2027 with 8 weeks' notice. Give your largest long-standing clients a £350 step until 30 Jun 2027. You break even if you lose fewer than 37 days a year, so check client concentration this week.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement to the board**

The owner has not said which way they lean. I judged the idea neutrally.

**The rise is 33%.** £300 to £400 is £100 more per day. Most freelance advice treats 5 to 15% as normal for existing clients. A 33% rise in one step is well outside that range. The sources are mostly freelancer blogs, not research.

**Is £400 within market range?** It depends on the owner's specialism and seniority, which I don't know.
- A tiered guide puts UK senior graphic designers at £280 to £400. Specialists are £400 to £600+ ([Pocketwise](https://pocketwise.co.uk/income/salary-by-profession/how-much-do-graphic-designers-earn-uk/)).
- YunoJuno lists an average of £353 for graphic designers. The page gives no date or sample size ([YunoJuno](https://www.yunojuno.com/freelancer-rates-job-role/graphic-designer)).
- ITJobsWatch shows a median of £350 for Creative Designer contract vacancies in England. This is for the six months to 3 March 2026 ([ITJobsWatch](https://www.itjobswatch.co.uk/contracts/england/creative%20designer.do?p=6)). These are employer vacancy rates, not what freelancers quote.
- UX and product work pays more. The London median is £490 (to January 2026) ([ITJobsWatch](https://www.itjobswatch.co.uk/contracts/london/ux%20designer.do)). One London council advert offered up to £500 a day.

So £300 looks at or just below the market for graphic work, and well below it for UX. £400 is defensible, not outrageous. The weakness is the claim that one number fits everyone. Published ranges run from £200 to £600, so they prove little about the owner's own value.

**Common claims that are weak or false**
- "Everyone must get the same rate" is not a rule. Advisers often separate new-client and existing-client pricing ([Landolio](https://landolio.pages.dev/blog/how-to-raise-freelance-rates)).
- "You'll lose 10 to 20% of clients" is one blog's guess. No source gives reliable churn data.
- "Charging more always signals quality" is unproven.

**Notice period.** Sources suggest 30 to 60 days, and 60 to 90 days for clients of two years or more. Put the notice in writing with an effective date. One option is finishing the current project at the old rate.

**The arithmetic.** If the owner loses clients, they break even after a loss of 25% of billable days at the same hours (300 ÷ 400 = 0.75). So they can lose up to a quarter of their work and earn the same, with more free time. That is the strongest point in favour. It assumes demand can be replaced and the days are not all with one client.

**UK rules.** A day rate is a contract term. If the existing contracts or agreed quotes fix the rate, the owner cannot change it mid-term without agreement. Check them. VAT is separate, and the rise matters if the owner is near the £90,000 threshold. I did not verify that figure today.

**What I'm unsure of**
- The owner's discipline, seniority, and when they last raised rates.
- Whether clients are on retainers or fixed quotes.
- Whether anyone has measured churn for UK designers. I found nothing.

**Evidence-based middle path:** Quote £400 to all new clients now. Move existing clients in two steps, for example £340 to £350 after 60 to 90 days' notice, then £400 about six months later. This is my suggestion, not tested evidence.

Bottom line: £400 is plausible for the market, but a flat 33% rise for every long-standing client, with no phasing, is not supported by the evidence. Move new clients to £400 now and phase existing ones with 60 to 90 days' written notice.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Yes, move to £400. Charge it to all new clients from today. Move existing clients to £400 from 1 February 2027, with written notice sent by 20 November 2026. The one exception: any client worth more than 25% of your income gets £350 until 30 June 2027, then £400. Only do this once you have checked your contracts and your client concentration.

## Why
- **The numbers favour the rise.** Going from £300 to £400 is a 33% increase. You earn the same gross income even if you lose 25% of your billed days. At 150 days a year (£45,000 now), you could lose 37 days and still not be worse off.
- **£400 is within the market range.** Published figures put your current £300 at or just below typical rates for graphic work. Senior graphic designers are listed at £280 to £400, and one contract median is £350. UX work pays well above £400. These are rough figures, mostly vacancy rates and blogs, and I don't know your specialism.
- **Loyalty is not a reason to undercharge.** It is a reason to give long notice and to protect your biggest relationships. It is not a reason to keep long-standing clients at £300.
- **Charging everyone £400 on one date is the weak version.** The most likely way this fails is that one large client walks, you have no work lined up to replace it, and you end up discounting to fill your diary. Protecting only your largest clients costs about £50 a day on a small share of your days, for five months.

## Main risks
- **Contracts.** If a signed contract or agreed quote fixes your rate, notice alone does not change it. You need the client's agreement or the contract's own change terms.
- **Concentration.** One client worth 40% of your income leaving takes you past the 25% break-even on its own.
- **Gaps in your pipeline.** Lost days only cost you if you can't refill them. Nobody gave reliable figures on how long that takes.
- **Quiet cuts.** Clients may accept £400 but book fewer days or pass small jobs to someone cheaper. You won't see this for about a quarter.
- **Budgets.** Some clients set annual budgets, often for an April or January year. A rise they didn't plan for is easier to refuse. Check each one.
- **VAT.** The registration threshold is £90,000 of turnover over a rolling 12 months (unchanged since April 2024, as far as the board knows). At £400 a day you cross it at about 225 billed days. Check if you work more than 200 days a year.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You asked about the rate before you know the three facts that decide it: your billed days, each client's share of your income, and how many months of savings you have. "Every client, the same day" is a blunt tool. The risk is not the rate itself. The risk is how much depends on one or two clients, and none of the board could see that.

## Next steps
1. **By 6 November:** list every client with the days billed and their share of income over the last 12 months. Read each contract for fixed rates and notice terms.
2. **From today:** quote £400 to all new enquiries.
3. **By 20 November:** write to existing clients. Give one reason and one effective date (1 February 2027). Clients above 25% get the £350 step instead. Review on 1 May 2027: count lost clients and days booked against the same period last year.
