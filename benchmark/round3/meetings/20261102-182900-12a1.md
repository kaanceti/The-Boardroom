## Agenda

I'm self-employed and made £38,000 profit this year. Should I set up a limited company?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: "Yes, incorporate once profit passes about £30,000 to £40,000." On current rates I think that answer is wrong for you, and the cautious option, staying a sole trader, is the stronger one.

**The core numbers (2026/27, England/Wales/NI, all profit taken out)**

These are my calculations. Scout should check the rates.

- **Sole trader on £38,000:**
  - Income tax: £25,430 × 20% = £5,086.
  - Class 4 NI: £25,430 × 6% = £1,526.
  - Total: about **£6,612**.
- **Limited company, £12,570 salary, rest as dividends:**
  - Employer NI: (£12,570 − £5,000) × 15% = £1,136. You cannot claim the Employment Allowance as a sole director with no other employees.
  - Corporation tax: 19% on £24,295 = £4,616.
  - Dividend tax: 10.75% on £19,179 after the £500 allowance = £2,062.
  - Total: about **£7,813**.
- **Limited company, £5,000 salary:** about **£8,276**.

So incorporating costs you roughly **£1,200 to £1,650 more tax a year**. Add **£1,000 to £2,000** of extra accountancy, and you are £2,000 to £3,500 worse off. The 2025 rise in employer NI and the April 2026 rise in dividend tax removed most of the old advantage at your profit level.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood × impact**

1. **You pay more, not less** (high likelihood, medium impact). See above. This is the most likely way the idea fails.
2. **Ongoing admin and penalties** (high, low to medium). You would face:
   - annual accounts and a CT600 return
   - a confirmation statement (£50)
   - payroll RTI filings
   - identity verification at Companies House
   - a personal tax return as well.

   Late filing penalties start at £150 at Companies House and £100 at HMRC.
3. **Director's loan account trouble** (medium, medium). Taking company money casually triggers a 33.75% s455 charge and possible benefit-in-kind tax. Sole traders often slip here in year one.
4. **IR35** (unknown, potentially high). If most of your income comes from one client who controls your work, the tax saving can vanish. You also face retrospective liability risk.
5. **Mortgage and credit friction** (medium if you plan to borrow, medium). Lenders often want 2 years of company accounts. Incorporating restarts that clock.
6. **Hard to reverse** (low, low to medium). Closing a company costs money and adds paperwork.

**Where the honest case for caution weakens**

- **Retaining profit:** If you can leave £10,000 or more in the company each year, it is taxed at 19% instead of your 26% marginal rate. That defers tax, though you pay it later on extraction.
- **Pensions:** Employer pension contributions avoid NI and corporation tax.
- **Liability:** Limited liability matters if your work carries real claim risk. Insurance often covers this more cheaply.
- **Other income:** I do not know your other income, your location (Scottish rates differ), or your growth plans. Any of these could change the answer.

**What would have to be true for incorporating to work**

- Profit heading reliably towards £50,000 or more.
- Real profit you do not need to spend, which you can keep in the company or put into a pension.
- Low IR35 exposure.
- No mortgage application in the next 2 years.
- An accountant who confirms the saving in writing for your actual figures.

Bottom line: At £38,000, taking everything out, a limited company likely costs you about £2,000 to £3,500 a year more. Stay a sole trader unless you can keep substantial profit in the company or expect clear growth.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: Stay a sole trader until profits pass about £50,000.

I want to push further. Here is the strongest honest case for incorporating now, and where it stops. Figures use 2026/27 UK rules and are estimates to check.

**Sole trader, £38,000 profit, all taken out:**
- Income tax: about £5,086
- Class 4 NICs: about £1,526
- Total: about £6,612

**Limited company, same profit, £12,570 salary and the rest as dividends:**
- Employer NICs on salary: about £1,136
- Corporation tax at 19% on about £24,295: about £4,616
- Dividend tax on about £19,678: roughly £1,700 to £2,100, depending on the rate that applies
- Total: about £7,450 to £7,850

If you take every pound out, the company costs more. That is the honest starting point.

Now the upside. The case is not about this year's drawings. It is about what you keep.

1. **Retained profit is taxed at 19%.** Money left in the company avoids personal income tax at 20% or 40% until you draw it. If you reinvest, that is a real gain.
2. **Employer pension contributions.** These are deductible for corporation tax and carry no employer NICs. This can move a large sum out of reach of income tax. Check the annual allowance with an adviser.
3. **Limited liability.** This matters more once you take on staff, premises or large contracts.
4. **A cleaner base for growth.** Lenders, investors and larger clients often prefer a company.

Costs to weigh: accountant fees (often £800 to £1,500 a year for a small company, varying by provider), Companies House filings, and more admin.

**What I am unsure of:**
- Whether £38,000 is profit after allowable expenses.
- Whether you plan to reinvest or need the money to live on.
- Your pension position and any unused allowances.
- The dividend tax rates from April 2026. Verify them with HMRC or an accountant.
- Whether your income is steady enough to carry the extra cost.

My view: incorporate if you will keep a meaningful part of the profit in the business or plan to grow. If you need all £38,000 to live on, staying a sole trader is probably cheaper.

Bottom line: The case is real but modest at £38,000. Incorporate if you plan to reinvest or grow. Otherwise, stay put.

## Why

These figures use 2026/27 rates for England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and assume this is your only income.
- **As a sole trader:** £5,086 income tax plus £1,526 Class 4 National Insurance (NI) comes to about £6,612. You keep about £31,390.
- **As a company paying you a £12,570 salary, with the rest as dividends:**
  - Employer NI: £1,136
  - Corporation tax: £4,616
  - Dividend tax at the new 10.75% rate: £2,062
  - Total: about £7,813. You keep about £30,190.
  - A £5,000 salary is worse, at about £8,276.
- **The old "incorporate at £30,000 to £40,000" rule is out of date.** Employer NI went up in April 2025 and dividend tax went up in April 2026.
- **Leaving profit in the company mostly delays tax rather than saving it.** Money taken out later costs about 27.7% in total (19% corporation tax, then 10.75% dividend tax on what is left). As a sole trader you pay 26%.
- **Pensions are the one real gain.** Company pension payments avoid corporation tax and NI. You can already get tax relief on pension payments as a sole trader, so the extra benefit is small. On £10,000 a year it is a few hundred pounds. That is our estimate.

## Main risks

- **Paying more, not less.** This is the most likely outcome at your profit level.
- **Overdrawn director's loan.** If you take company money casually, as you can now, the company pays a 33.75% charge (section 455) on any amount not repaid within 9 months of the year end.
- **IR35.** If most of your work comes from one client who controls how you do it, working through a company can bring in these rules and wipe out any saving.
- **More admin and fines.** You would need payroll filings, company accounts, a corporation tax return and a £50 confirmation statement. Late-filing fines start at £150 at Companies House and £100 at HMRC.
- **Borrowing.** Mortgage lenders often want 2 years of company accounts, so switching can delay a mortgage. Limited liability is also weaker than it sounds: banks and landlords usually ask directors for personal guarantees.

- **Making Tax Digital.** It is based on turnover (your total sales), not profit. If your turnover is over £30,000, you will probably need to keep digital records and send quarterly updates from 6 April 2027. That is about 5 months away. Please confirm the date and threshold on gov.uk.
