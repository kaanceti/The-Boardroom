# Boardroom minutes: Should I buy an electric car now or wait two more years?

Date: 2026-11-03 20:38  
Mode: full

## Agenda

Should I buy an electric car now or wait two more years?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: buy now if you can charge at home, because running costs are low and grants and discounts are generous.

I argue for the cautious end. Keep your current car if it is reliable. If you must change, lease instead of buying outright. The case for caution is moderate, not overwhelming, and I say where it is weak.

**What we do not know.** Your annual mileage, whether you have a driveway, your current car's age and condition, and your budget. Each one can flip the answer.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Depreciation (high likelihood, high impact).** Used EV values in the UK fell sharply in 2023 and 2024. Many three-year-old EVs lost 50% or more of their value. Cheaper Chinese models (BYD, MG, Leapmotor) and better batteries keep pushing prices down. If you buy outright now, you carry that loss. Leasing passes it to the finance company.

2. **No home charging (medium likelihood, high impact).** On a home off-peak tariff you pay about 7p to 9p per kWh. Public rapid chargers cost about 70p to 85p per kWh. At those rates an EV can cost more per mile than an efficient petrol car. If you cannot charge at home, most of the savings disappear.

3. **Policy changes (high likelihood, medium impact).** EVs have paid Vehicle Excise Duty since April 2025. The 2025 Budget announced pay-per-mile charging for EVs from April 2028, at about 3p per mile. At 10,000 miles a year that adds roughly £300. Treat current incentives as temporary. Please have Scout confirm the exact rates.

4. **Replacing a working car too early (medium likelihood, medium impact).** If your current car is paid off and reliable, its running costs may be lower than any new car's total cost. Most of a car's lifetime carbon is produced when it is built, so an early swap also has weaker environmental logic.

5. **Technology regret (medium likelihood, low to medium impact).** Range and charging speed are improving. Solid-state batteries are unlikely to be in affordable UK cars by 2028, so do not wait for them specifically.

**The honest case against waiting.** The Electric Car Grant (up to £3,750 on eligible cars under £37,000) may shrink or end. The ZEV mandate is pushing manufacturers to discount heavily now. Waiting two years gives you no guaranteed gain. It only defers the decision.

**What would have to be true for buying now to work**
- You can charge at home.
- You drive more than about 7,000 miles a year.
- Your current car needs replacing within a year anyway.
- You will either keep the EV for 8 years or more, or you lease it.
- You check the grant eligibility of the specific model and the battery warranty (typically 8 years or 100,000 miles).

If any of the first three is false, the cautious option wins.

Bottom line: Do not buy an EV outright now unless your current car is failing and you can charge at home. Otherwise keep your car, or lease an EV, and decide again in 2028.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: wait, because prices will fall and the technology will improve.

I would argue for buying now, if your driving suits it.

The case for now:

- Waiting costs money you can count. If you drive 8,000 miles a year and an EV saves about 10p per mile on energy against petrol, that is roughly £800 a year. Check this against your own mileage and electricity tariff.
- A modern EV with a real-world range of 200 miles or more covers most UK daily driving. Two years of improvement will not make today's car useless.
- Used EV prices have fallen sharply. Depreciation is already priced in, so a nearly-new car now can be a strong deal.
- The UK Electric Car Grant opened in 2025. Funding is limited and the rules may change. If it is still open when you buy, that is a reason to act sooner.
- Home charging matters most. With off-street parking, you can charge overnight on a cheaper tariff. Without it, public charging costs much more and the saving shrinks.

The unusual move: buy a nearly-new or approved used EV now, not a new one. You capture most of the running-cost saving and avoid the steepest first-year depreciation.

Where the upside is limited:

- No off-street parking: the case weakens a lot.
- Under about 5,000 miles a year: fuel savings may not cover the price gap.
- The government has said EVs will pay a per-mile road charge from 2028, reported as 3p per mile. This cuts your savings. I am not certain of the final rate, so check the current Budget documents.

What I am unsure of: current EV and petrol prices, your mileage, whether you have home charging, and whether the grant is still open.

Bottom line: If you have home charging and drive over 8,000 miles a year, buy a nearly-new EV now. If you do not, wait and recheck the numbers.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Statement on buying an electric car now or waiting two years**

I can't give a firm answer. The owner gave me no mileage, budget, current car or home parking. I will use assumptions and say where they bite.

**What decides it**
1. Home charging. With a driveway and an overnight tariff of about 8p per kWh, running costs are low. Without it, public charging at 65p to 80p per kWh costs more than petrol. If there is no home charger, wait or don't buy.
2. Mileage. I assume 8,000 miles a year.

**Running cost at 8,000 miles a year (my estimates)**
- Petrol at 45 mpg and about £1.40 a litre: roughly £1,130 a year.
- EV on home overnight rate: roughly £200 a year.
- EV on standard home rate (25p): roughly £600 a year.
- EV on public rapid charging: roughly £1,700 a year.

So the saving is £500 to £900 a year at home. A home charger costs about £800 to £1,200 fitted.

**Policy points (I am fairly sure, but Scout should verify)**
- The Electric Car Grant offers up to £3,750 on eligible new cars priced under £37,000. It is available now. I am unsure how long the funding lasts.
- Pay-per-mile tax on EVs of about 3p a mile is due from April 2028. That is about £240 a year at 8,000 miles. Waiting two years does not avoid it.

**Cost of buying now versus waiting**
- New EVs typically lose around 45% to 55% of value in three years. A two-year-old used EV is much cheaper per year of ownership. I'd expect savings of £5,000 to £8,000 on the purchase price, though that varies by model.
- Waiting means you keep paying for your current car's fuel and repairs. If it is cheap and reliable, waiting wins on cash.
- Battery and charging improvements are real but gradual. Today's cars are not obsolete.

**Plan if the owner proceeds (assuming a £25,000 budget)**
1. By 17 Nov: confirm home charging is possible and get two installer quotes (2 hours, £0).
2. By 30 Nov: get a trade-in value for the current car (1 hour, £0).
3. By 15 Dec: shortlist three cars and take test drives (a weekend).
4. By 31 Jan 2027: order, with the grant applied by the dealer.
5. Spring 2027: delivery and charger fitted.

**Realistic figures if bought now (mid-case)**
- 3 months: £1,000 charger cost spent; running savings about £150.
- 6 months: savings about £300 to £400, so still well behind on cash.
- 12 months: savings about £500 to £900. Depreciation of £5,000 to £7,000 swamps that unless the old car was being replaced anyway.

**My view:** Buy now only if the current car needs replacing within 12 months and home charging is available. Otherwise waiting two years and buying used is the cheaper path. The saving from running costs alone never justifies replacing a working car.

I am unsure about tax rates, grant duration and used values in 2028. Please don't treat my figures as quotes.

Bottom line: Wait and buy used in late 2028 unless your current car is failing within a year and you have home charging; if both are true, buy now.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Evidence on buying an electric car now or waiting two years**

The owner has not said which answer they want. I judged this on the evidence alone.

**Reasons to buy now**
- The Electric Car Grant gives up to £3,750 off a new car. It is reported to run to March 2030, so waiting two years should not lose it. The funding pot is £650m, enough for about 173,000 cars at the full rate. The price cap is about £37,000, and one source says some higher trims go to £42,000. The lists conflict, so check the official Department for Transport list. ([Auto Express](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/news/363390/ev-grant-returns-labour-announces-new-ps3750-electric-car-discount), [MoneyWeek](https://www.moneyweek.com/personal-finance/electric-car-grant-uk-government-scheme), [Lloyd Motor Group](https://www.lloydmotorgroup.com/News/Electric-Car-Grant-Save-upto--3750/2666))
- Used EVs are cheap and getting cheaper. The AA says the 20 most-searched used EVs averaged £13,940 in Q2, down 10% year on year. ([AA via am-online](https://www.am-online.com/news/tesla-leads-sharpest-used-ev-price-falls-as-values-drop-10))
- Petrol and diesel have no waiting benefit. Electric cars cost less per mile to run, and I did not compare the figures here.

**Reasons to wait**
- A pay-per-mile tax starts in April 2028. It is 3p per mile for electric cars and 1.5p for plug-in hybrids. It rises with CPI and sits on top of annual road tax. A typical 8,500 miles costs about £255 a year. Waiting two years does not avoid it, because the tax applies to all electric cars. ([Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/ev-tax-announced-everything-you-need-to-know-aUbgq3z4kYEc), [TransportXtra](https://www.transportxtra.com/publications/transit/news/79605/electric-vehicle-owners-face-pay-per-mile-tax))
- Depreciation is steep, and the sources disagree. One says 61% lost over three years for an EV against 47% for petrol. Another says only about 10% worse than petrol. The Electric Car Scheme estimates about 13% a year for used EVs. Falling used prices are good for a used buyer and bad for a new buyer who plans to sell. ([Warrantywise](https://www.warrantywise.co.uk/blog/advice/buying-and-selling/do-evs-depreciate-faster-than-petrol-and-diesel-vehicles/), [Electric Car Scheme](https://www.electriccarscheme.com/blog/pay-per-mile-road-tax-ev-update-2026))
- The used-price gap to petrol has reportedly halved in two years. It may stabilise in 2026 to 2027, so waiting may not bring much further saving. This is weakly sourced.

**Common claims that are false or outdated**
- "The grant is ending soon." Not on current reporting. It runs to about 2030.
- "Waiting dodges the pay-per-mile tax." False. It applies to all electric cars from April 2028.
- "Electric cars are always dearer than petrol." Outdated for used cars.

**What I could not establish**
- The average new EV price today.
- Whether new models in 2028 will be much better or cheaper. Claims of big battery gains are mostly marketing, and I did not test them.
- The owner's mileage, home charging access, budget and current car. These change the answer more than anything above.

**My reading**
The grant lasts, the tax is unavoidable, and used prices are already low. Little evidence shows a big gain from waiting. The case to wait is mainly that you can drive your current car for free and prices may fall a bit more.

Bottom line: The evidence does not favour waiting for policy reasons, so buy now if your current car is due for replacement and you can charge at home, ideally a used or grant-eligible new EV. Otherwise keep your car, because waiting gains little.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Don't time this around the EV market. Time it around your current car. If it is reliable, keep it and buy an EV when it actually needs replacing, whether that is in 2026 or 2029. If it needs replacing within about 12 months and you can charge at home, buy now. A one to three year old used EV usually gives the best value; a grant-eligible new car is the alternative.

## Why
- **Waiting gains you little.** The 3p per mile road charge starts in April 2028 and applies to every EV, so waiting does not avoid it. The Electric Car Grant (up to £3,750 on new cars under about £37,000) is reported to run to March 2030. Used EV prices have already fallen a lot.
- **Running costs can save real money.** At 8,000 miles a year, petrol costs about £1,130. An EV charged on a home overnight tariff (about 8p per kWh) costs about £200. After the roughly £240 a year road charge from 2028, the net saving is about £700 a year. On a standard 25p tariff it is about £300.
- **Depreciation is larger than those savings.** A new EV can lose £5,000 to £7,000 in its first years. That is why swapping a working car early does not pay. It is also why used is usually the better buy.

## Main risks
- **No home charging.** Public rapid charging costs 65p to 85p per kWh. At that price an EV costs more per mile than petrol. Without a driveway or a workable charger setup, don't buy.
- **Values are still falling.** Used EV prices were down about 10% year on year. Whoever owns the car takes the next drop. A lease is not free protection either: its price already includes expected depreciation, plus mileage caps and damage charges.
- **The grant could run out early.** "Runs to 2030" is not the same as "funded to 2030". The £650m pot covers about 173,000 cars. This only matters if you buy new.
- **Battery health on used cars.** Get a battery health report and check how much of the manufacturer's battery warranty is left. It is typically 8 years or 100,000 miles.
- **Insurance** is often higher for EVs. Get quotes before you commit.

## Honest criticism for the owner
"Now or in two years" is the wrong question. You left out the four facts that decide it: your annual mileage, whether you can charge at home, the age and condition of your current car, and your budget. Each one can flip the answer. Waiting two years on purpose, while a car that needs replacing costs you in repairs, would be a mistake. So would replacing a sound car now.

## Next steps
1. This week, write down four things: your annual mileage, whether you have off-street parking, your current car's age and likely repairs over the next 12 months, and your budget.
2. If you have off-street parking, get two charger installation quotes (about £800 to £1,200) and an overnight tariff quote.
3. If your car is due for replacement, check the official GOV.UK list of grant-eligible cars. Price two or three used EVs with a battery health report, and get insurance quotes for each.
