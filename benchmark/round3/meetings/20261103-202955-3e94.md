# Boardroom minutes: My dog-walking business has a waiting list. Should I hire my first employee or…

Date: 2026-11-03 20:29  
Mode: full

## Agenda

My dog-walking business has a waiting list. Should I hire my first employee or raise my prices?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: hire, because a waiting list means demand and you should grow into it.

I argue for the cautious end: raise prices first, and do not hire yet. The owner gave no hint of a preferred answer, so I judge both options equally.

**What the waiting list really tells you**
A waiting list is the clearest sign you are underpriced. It does not prove demand will hold at a price that also covers an employee. Those are different tests. A price rise runs the first test cheaply and can be reversed. A hire is hard to reverse.

**Risks of hiring, ranked by likelihood × impact**

1. **Idle paid hours (high, high).** NMW for 21+ is £12.71/hour from April 2026. Travel time between walks counts as working time for NMW. Cancellations, rain days and school holidays still cost you wages. A 25-hour contract is about £16,500 a year before anything else. If utilisation drops below roughly 70%, a group-walk margin can disappear. I don't know your prices, so treat that threshold as an estimate.
2. **Reputation from one incident (medium, very high).** A lost or injured dog under someone else's lead lands on your name. Most of your work likely comes from local word of mouth, so one bad Facebook post can undo the waiting list.
3. **Client poaching (medium, high).** Your employee meets your clients and holds their keys. Non-solicitation clauses are hard to enforce against a low-paid worker. They can leave with your best routes.
4. **Your time moves to management (high, medium).** Rotas, sick cover, DBS checks, payroll and training come out of your walking hours.
5. **Compliance costs (certain, medium).** Employer's liability insurance is compulsory, with fines up to £2,500 per day without it. Employer NICs are 15% above £5,000, which may be covered by the £10,500 Employment Allowance if you are eligible. Auto-enrolment pension is 3%. Statutory sick pay is due from day one from April 2026. Some councils and the Royal Parks cap dogs per walker or require a commercial permit, so check yours.
6. **Misclassification (medium, medium).** Calling them "self-employed" to dodge the above is risky if you set their hours and routes.

**Risks of raising prices (honestly weaker)**
- You lose some existing clients. The waiting list should refill those slots.
- Local competitors undercut you. That is only a real risk if your clients chose you on price, and a waiting list suggests they did not.

**Most likely way the hire dies:** within 6 to 12 months, the employee's diary is not full enough, or they leave and take clients. You are then left with costs, gaps, and less of your own walking time.

**What would have to be true for hiring to work**
- The waiting list is still 15 or more dogs after a 10 to 15% price rise.
- You have enough committed recurring bookings to fill 80% of the hire's hours before they start.
- You have written procedures, insurance and a trial period in place.

**Suggested sequence**
1. Raise prices for new clients now.
2. Raise prices for existing clients with 30 days' notice in January.
3. Watch churn for 8 weeks.
4. Revisit hiring in spring 2027.

Bottom line: Raise prices first, because it is cheap and reversible, and only hire if the waiting list survives the rise.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: hire first, because a waiting list means you have more demand than hands.

I would push the other way. Raise prices first, then hire.

A waiting list is a price signal. People want your service more than you charge for it. Test this now. Raise prices for new clients by 10% to 15% for one month. If the list stays full, you were underpriced. If a few people leave, you have learned the true level of demand, and you have cash to spare.

Hiring is expensive before it pays. In the UK, a first employee costs more than the wage:

- Employer National Insurance, currently 15% above the secondary threshold. Check current rates on gov.uk.
- Workplace pension auto-enrolment, with at least 3% employer contribution.
- Holiday pay, roughly 12% on top of hours worked.
- Employers' liability insurance, which is a legal requirement once you have an employee.
- Payroll software or an accountant's fee.

If your walker costs you £14 an hour all-in and you charge clients £15, the margin is thin. The same hire at £20 an hour pricing leaves room to grow.

The upside of hiring is real. You break the ceiling of your own hours. You can serve more clients per week. But that upside only lasts if the margin supports it. Pricing up first gives you the money to hire without strain.

The unusual move: add a priority tier. Waiting-list clients could pay a set fee to skip the queue, or a higher fee for same-day or fixed-time slots. This turns the queue into revenue. Use the extra income to fund the first hire from month two, once you have seen how clients react.

What I am unsure of:

- How long the waiting list is. Ten people and fifty people are very different situations.
- How price-sensitive your clients are. Some dog owners will pay more for reliability. Others will leave.
- What local walkers charge. If you are already above the going rate, the case for a rise is weaker.
- How many walks you do per hour, and how many hours you can work each week. I do not have these figures, so I cannot say how much a hire would add.
- Whether a walker would be an employee or self-employed. That choice changes your legal duties a great deal. Check with HMRC or an accountant.

If the list is long, the price rise is likely to hold. If it is short, the answer may be to fill it first, then decide.

Bottom line: Test a price rise for new clients now, and let the response tell you whether to hire.

**What the evidence says**

- Typical UK dog walking is about £16 to £25 per hour. Insured, experienced walkers often charge £18 to £30. Source: [Protectivity 2026 guide](https://www.protectivity.com/?p=12657). That is an insurance blog, so treat it as a rough guide.

- The National Living Wage is £12.71 per hour for ages 21 and over from April 2026. For ages 18 to 20 it is £10.85. Source: [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/national-minimum-wage-rates).
- Employer National Insurance is 15% above £5,000 a year of pay. The Employment Allowance is £10,500. Sources: [GOV.UK rates](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/rates-and-thresholds-for-employers-2026-to-2027) and [Kreston Reeves](https://www.krestonreeves.com/news/payroll-changes-from-april-2026-what-employers-need-to-know/). One source disagreed on the £100,000 eligibility cap. Most say it was removed in April 2025. Check this.
- Employer's liability insurance is legally required once you have an employee who is not family. I found no reliable price. Sole-trader public liability starts around £65 to £81 a year ([NimbleFins](https://www.nimblefins.co.uk/node/782), [Moneysupermarket](https://www.moneysupermarket.com/business-insurance/dog-walker-insurance/)). Employer's liability is not included in basic quotes.

**Rough cost of a first hire (my arithmetic)**

- A walker paid £12.71 per hour for 25 paid hours a week costs about £16,500 a year in wages.
- Employer NI on that is about £1,700 a year. The Employment Allowance would probably cover it if you claim it.
- Holiday pay of 5.6 weeks adds about 12%. Add pension auto-enrolment at 3% employer minimum, insurance, and paid travel time between walks. Paid travel time often catches owners out.
- Net, a hire only pays if each paid hour earns well above about £15 in fees. Group walks at £12 to £15 per dog-hour can make that easy or impossible, depending on dogs per group. Check this for your own rounds.

**Claims to correct**

- "Raising prices will lose clients" is not shown by this evidence. A waiting list is itself evidence that demand exceeds supply at your current price. I found no UK study on price rises in dog walking, so this is inference.
- "Employing someone is just their wage" is false. Add 20% or more on top of wages.
- Check whether a self-employed walker would be a safer option. HMRC judges status by control and substitution. Walkers you direct and schedule are likely employees.

**What I'm unsure of**

- Your current price against your local market.
- How long the waiting list is, and whether those people would still wait after a rise.
- Local rates. Rover's averages are listings, not what people actually pay.

**Practical order**

1. Compare your price with local rivals. If you are under about £18 per hour, you are likely underpriced.
2. Raise prices for new clients first. This is a cheap test with little downside.
3. Hire once the higher price still leaves a waiting list.

Bottom line: The evidence favours raising prices first, because a waiting list signals underpricing and a hire adds roughly 20% or more on top of wages. Hire second, funded by the higher margin.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Raise your prices now, and don't hire yet. Put the rise on new and waiting-list clients this week, and on existing clients from 1 January 2027. Decide on a part-time hire in February 2027. Only hire if the list, at the new price, could fill about three-quarters of the hours you would pay that person for.

## Why
- **A waiting list is the clearest sign you charge too little.** It tells you demand beats supply at today's price. It does not tell you whether demand would cover a wage. A price rise answers the first question at no cost, and you can reverse it.
- **A hire is a fixed cost that is hard to undo.** At the £12.71 an hour minimum wage for ages 21 and over, a 25-hour contract costs about £16,500 a year in wages. Then add the 3% employer pension, insurance and payroll costs. Travel time between walks counts as paid working time.

- **No member's profit forecast holds up.** The board has none of your real figures. The one 12-month estimate (about £13,000 extra) assumed the hire bills only about 8 of 20 paid hours, so treat it as a guess.
- **Waiting too long has a cost.** People on the list will not wait months. Testing until spring 2027 lets the list fade.

## Main risks
- **Paying for empty hours.** This is the most likely way a hire fails. Rain, cancellations and school holidays still cost you wages. On a 25-hour contract, fewer than about 18 billed hours a week loses money.
- **Legal duties from day one.** Employer's liability insurance is compulsory, with fines of up to £2,500 a day without it. You also need PAYE registration, auto-enrolment, a right-to-work check and a written contract. Statutory sick pay is now due from day one.
