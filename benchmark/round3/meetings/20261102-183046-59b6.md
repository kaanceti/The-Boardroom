# Boardroom minutes: Someone has offered me £25,000 for my small website, which earns about £800 a…

Date: 2026-11-02 18:30  
Mode: full

## Agenda

Someone has offered me £25,000 for my small website, which earns about £800 a month. Should I sell it?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: £25,000 is about 31 times monthly earnings, close to market rate, so take it or haggle a little.

I argue the cautious end: do not accept this offer as it stands. Doing nothing for now is a real option. The honest caveat is that keeping the site also carries risk, so this is closer than my role suggests.

**Assumptions that may be weak**
- "Earns £800 a month" may be revenue, not profit. Buyers value net profit. If hosting, tools and writers cost £150 a month, the true multiple is about 38x. That favours you, but the buyer will find it and may reprice.
- The offer was made to you; you did not ask for it. Unsolicited offers are a common route for fraud and for lowball bids from people who know more about your site's value than you do.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**
1. **Retrade after due diligence (likely, medium impact).** Buyers often agree a headline price and then cut it, for example to £18,000, once they see a traffic dip. You will have spent weeks and emotional commitment by then.
2. **Underpricing (likely, medium).** Broker listings often quote 30 to 45 times monthly profit for stable sites. I am not certain of current 2026 ranges; the Scout should check. At 40x you leave £7,000 on the table.
3. **Tax surprise (moderate, medium).** Capital Gains Tax applies above the £3,000 annual exempt amount, at 18% or 24% depending on your income band. If HMRC treats the site as a trade, or it sits in a limited company, the treatment changes. Your take home could be nearer £20,000 to £21,000. Get an accountant's view first; I am unsure of your setup.
4. **Payment or transfer fraud (low to moderate, high impact).** Typical patterns are handing over domain or account access before the funds clear, PayPal chargebacks, or bank transfers that get reversed. Use a recognised escrow service only.
5. **Warranty or non compete liability (low, medium).** A badly drafted contract can make you liable later if the traffic falls.

**The case for keeping it**
£800 a month is £9,600 a year. The site pays back the £25,000 in about 2.6 years. If it lasts three years or more, keeping it wins. The counter risk is real, though. Google core updates and AI answers in search have cut traffic to many small sites. If your traffic is already falling, selling now may be the cautious choice. I do not know your trend, and that one fact decides most of this.

**What would have to be true for selling to be right**
- Traffic and income have been flat or falling for six to 12 months.
- The buyer is verified and pays through escrow.
- After tax and fees you net at least about £21,000, and you have a better use for the cash than 38% a year.
- The contract has no earnout and only limited warranties.

**Most likely way it goes wrong**
You accept quickly, the price is cut during due diligence, and you sell for less than the site was worth.

Bottom line: Do not accept yet. Check 12 months of traffic, get a tax view, and get one independent valuation. Sell only through escrow, and only if traffic is falling or you net over £21,000.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: take the £25,000 and move on.

I want to argue the other end. Keep the site, or refuse this price as it stands.

**Why the offer may be low**
- £25,000 is about 31 months of gross revenue (£800 a month, £9,600 a year). If the £800 is profit, the price is roughly 2.6 years of profit. That is a fair rule-of-thumb price for a small site. It is not a windfall.
- The buyer expects to earn more from it. Buyers pay for upside they can capture. You may be able to capture some of that yourself.
- The site already earns money. Most small sites never do.

**Growth you could test first**
- Raise prices, or add a paid tier.
- Add an email list or affiliate links, if traffic supports them.
- Target search terms that bring in buyers, not just readers.

**The unusual move**
Do not sell outright. Counter with a higher upfront price, or a deal where you keep a share of future revenue for 12 to 24 months. You could also ask the buyer what they plan to do with the site. Their plan shows what they think it is worth.

**Limits, stated plainly**
- I do not know whether £800 is profit or revenue. Check this first.
- I do not know whether traffic is rising, flat or falling. A falling site makes this offer look better than it is.
- I do not know your niche, how much time you can give it, or your tax position. A sale may trigger Capital Gains Tax, depending on how the sale is structured. Check with an accountant before agreeing anything.
- Growth is not guaranteed. Tests like these take months, not weeks.

Bottom line: Do not accept £25,000 yet. Confirm the profit and the traffic trend. If both hold, counter with a higher price or a revenue-share deal. Sell at £25,000 only if the buyer will not move and you do not want the extra work.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**The number first.** £25,000 is 31 times monthly earnings, or 2.6 years of income. That is a normal price for a small site. It is not a bargain. Keeping the site only beats the offer if it still pays well after about 2.6 to 3 years.

5. Weeks 4 to 6 (10 to 15 hours): Hand over the domain, accounts, content and logins. Limit your support to 2 to 4 weeks.
6. Tax: A sale in Nov or Dec 2026 falls in tax year 2026/27. Report it and pay by 31 January 2028.

**Tax estimate (please check with an accountant):**
Assume your cost base is near zero.
- The taxable gain is about £22,000 after the £3,000 annual exempt amount.

- At 18%, tax is about £4,000.
- You may only get the lower rate if Business Asset Disposal Relief applies, and I am unsure that a hobby-sized site qualifies.

**Numbers if you keep it (flat at £800 a month, before tax):**
- 3 months: £2,400
- 6 months: £4,800
- 12 months: £9,600, or about £7,000 to £7,700 after tax

**Numbers if you sell (net about £20,500 after tax):**
- 3 months: about £20,500 in hand, plus about £170 interest at 4%
- 6 months: about £20,700 including interest
- 12 months: about £21,300 including interest

On those figures, selling stays ahead for about 2.7 to 3 years. Keeping only wins if the site grows or holds up for that long.

**Flags:**
- If £800 is revenue, the price is too low, and I would not sell below 30 times real profit.
- If most traffic comes from one search engine, one update could halve the income. That favours selling.
- If the site is growing 5% or more a month, that favours keeping.
- If your time on it exceeds 10 hours a week, your effective pay is under £19 an hour. That favours selling.

Bottom line: Do not accept £25,000 as offered. Counter at £30,000 and sell at £25,000 to £28,000 only if £800 is true profit and the income is flat, falling or reliant on one traffic source. Keep it if it is growing.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement to the board**

**The offer in numbers.** £25,000 on £800 a month is 31.25 times monthly profit. That is 2.6 years of profit. It is also 2.6 times annual profit (£9,600).

**What the evidence says about fair price.**
- Common guidance is 30 to 45 times monthly net profit for small sites with steady traffic (summarised on [Flippa's valuation page](https://flippa.com/blog/website-valuation/)). On that range, your site is worth £24,000 to £36,000, if £800 is true profit.
- One secondary blog says Empire Flippers content sites averaged 33.2 times in 2025 and Flippa averaged 24.6 times ([source](https://organicarbitrage.pages.dev/articles/revenue-multiple-valuation-seo-sites)). Other sources give 25 to 29 times. They conflict, and I could not check them against the primary reports.
- Since 8 September 2026, [Empire Flippers shows annual multiples](https://empireflippers.com/switching-to-annual-multiples/). Divide older monthly figures by 12 to compare. Mixing the two is a common error.
- So £25,000 is at the low-to-middle end of the range. It is not a ripoff, and it is not generous.

**Claims that are false or outdated.**
- "Sites sell for 3 to 5 years of profit." Only the top quality sites do. I found a 42x average claim on a page that looks like spam. Ignore it.
- "A direct offer equals market value." Not necessarily. Listed sites compete for buyers. A single private offer does not.

**What I could not check.**
- Whether £800 is profit or revenue. If it is revenue, £25,000 may be a high price.
- Your niche, traffic source, age, and how much work you do. These move the multiple more than anything else.
- Whether the buyer is real. Offers for small sites are often scams or lowball tests.

**Things that cut against selling.**
- If you keep the site, £800 a month pays back £25,000 in about 31 months, assuming the income holds. Search traffic can fall after Google updates, which is the main risk for the buyer and for you.
- Tax: in the UK, a sale is usually Capital Gains Tax, not income tax. I did not verify current rates or allowances, so check HMRC. You may also qualify for Business Asset Disposal Relief, but I have not confirmed that for a website.

**Things that favour selling.**
- A lump sum removes the risk that income drops.
- It is fair to sell if the site needs work you no longer want to do.

**Practical checks before saying yes.**
1. Ask for proof of funds. Use an escrow service such as Escrow.com, not direct payment.
2. Get a counteroffer in at about 36 times (£28,800). Test their reaction.
3. Get a free valuation from Flippa or Empire Flippers to compare.
4. Use a written sale contract. Limit handover support to a fixed period.
5. Do not give analytics or login access before payment is secured.

Bottom line: £25,000 is a fair but not strong price, so do not accept it yet. Counter at about £28,000 to £30,000, confirm the £800 is net profit, and sell only through escrow.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Don't accept £25,000 as it stands, but don't reject the idea of selling either. First confirm that the £800 a month is net profit and check the 12-month traffic trend. Then counter at £28,000 to £30,000. If the income is flat, falling or relies mostly on Google, sell at £25,000 to £28,000, through escrow only.

## Why
- **The price is fair but low.** £25,000 is about 31 times monthly earnings, or 2.6 years of income. Common guidance for small, steady sites is 30 to 45 times monthly profit, which puts the low-to-middle value at £24,000 to £36,000. We could not confirm 2026 market figures, so treat that range as rough.
- **The trend decides it.** If income holds, keeping the site matches the sale proceeds in about 3 years. If a Google update cuts traffic, selling now is clearly better.
- **Keeping the site is taxed too.** Profit is taxed as income: 20% for a basic-rate taxpayer, 40% plus 6% National Insurance for a higher-rate taxpayer. That cuts £9,600 a year to roughly £5,200 to £7,700. A sale nets about £20,000 to £21,000 after Capital Gains Tax. Once both sides are taxed, selling looks better than it first appears.

## Main risks
1. **A fake or haggling buyer.** The offer came to you unasked. Ask for proof of funds and use an escrow service such as Escrow.com. Don't give anyone logins or domain access until the money is secured.
2. **Price cut after checks.** Buyers often agree a price, then cut it during due diligence. Have your figures ready before you negotiate, so there is less to find.
3. **Money clawed back after the sale.** Broad promises in the sale contract about traffic or revenue can let the buyer claim money back later. Escrow does not protect you from this. Pay a solicitor to review the contract. I'd guess £500 to £1,500, but I'm not sure.
4. **Tax.** Capital Gains Tax is 18% or 24% on gains above £3,000. Business Asset Disposal Relief is now 18%, so it only helps higher-rate taxpayers, and only if the site is a genuine trade you've held for 2 years or more. If the site sits in a limited company, the tax works differently.
5. **Your counter ends the deal.** With only one buyer, this can happen. Decide your walk-away figure before you counter.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You quoted "about £800 a month" without saying whether that is revenue or profit, or which way it is trending. Those facts decide the answer, and a buyer will ask for them on day one. Also, at £9,600 a year, you should already be filing Self Assessment for this income. If you haven't, fix that before you sell, because the buyer's checks may bring it to light.

## Next steps
1. **This week:** pull 12 to 24 months of income, costs and traffic, including where the traffic comes from. If you can't produce this, you aren't ready to sell.
2. **Next week:** ask an accountant how the sale would be taxed. Ask the buyer for proof of funds and a written offer. Get a free comparison valuation from Flippa or Empire Flippers.
3. **Week 3:** counter at £28,000 to £30,000. Agree only to a written contract, escrow, no deferred or revenue-share payments, and 2 to 4 weeks of handover support at most.
