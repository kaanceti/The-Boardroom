# Boardroom minutes: Our family restaurant gets complaints about slow service on Saturdays. Should…

Date: 2026-11-03 20:34  
Mode: full

## Agenda

Our family restaurant gets complaints about slow service on Saturdays. Should we cut the menu in half?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: yes, simplify the menu, because fewer dishes means a faster kitchen.

The question is framed as "should we cut," which suggests you lean yes. I have judged it as if you hoped for no.

**My position: do not cut the menu in half yet.** The problem is on one day. The fix would hit all seven. And nobody has yet shown that the menu causes the delay.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Misdiagnosis (high likelihood, high impact).** On a busy Saturday, slow service often comes from somewhere other than the menu. Common causes are too few front-of-house staff, a single till, slow drinks, tables seated all at once, or one cook running the pass. If the bottleneck is any of these, halving the menu costs you dishes and fixes nothing. This is the most likely way the idea fails. I do not know your setup, so I cannot say which applies.

2. **Losing regulars (medium likelihood, high impact).** Family restaurants live on repeat customers who order "their" dish. Cut half the menu and some of those dishes go. A few complaints about waiting may turn into quiet non-return, which you will not see in reviews.

3. **Weekday revenue loss (medium likelihood, medium impact).** Monday to Friday has no stated speed problem. A permanent 50% cut reduces choice on those days for no benefit.

4. **Arbitrary size of cut (high likelihood, medium impact).** "Half" is a round number, not a measured one. The right cut might be 3 slow dishes, or 15. Cutting by the wrong amount either fails to fix speed or removes good sellers.

5. **Hidden costs (high likelihood, low impact).** Reprinting menus, updating Google, booking sites and any delivery apps, writing off stock for dropped dishes, and updating your allergen information, which you must keep accurate under the Food Information Regulations 2014. These run to hundreds of pounds, not thousands, but they are real.

6. **Family friction (unknown likelihood, medium impact).** In a family business, dishes often carry history. A forced cut can cause disagreement that outlasts the speed problem.

**The honest limit of my case:** menu reduction does often speed up kitchens. If your kitchen really is the bottleneck, the cautious case weakens a lot.

**What would have to be true for a 50% cut to work**
- Timed tickets on 2 or 3 Saturdays show the kitchen, not the floor or bar, is where orders stall.
- The dishes you drop are the slow, low-selling ones, checked against till data, not gut feel.
- Regular customers' top orders survive the cut.
- Weekday sales do not drop, or the drop is smaller than the Saturday gain.

**Cheaper first steps:** time orders from seating to food for 3 Saturdays. Then trial a Saturday-only short menu for 4 weeks and compare ticket times, covers and complaints.

Bottom line: Do not halve the menu now. Measure where Saturday orders actually stall, then test a Saturday-only shorter menu before changing anything permanently.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: cut the menu, because fewer dishes means a faster kitchen.

I'd argue for something bolder. Don't cut the whole menu. Build a separate, tight Saturday menu of about 8 to 10 dishes, run only at peak hours, and keep the full menu on quieter days. You get the speed gain where the complaints happen, and regulars still get their favourites on weekdays.

The bolder move goes further. Offer a fixed-price Saturday set, with two or three courses chosen from a short list, and charge a little more than the à la carte average. Fixed sets let the kitchen batch prep, cut ingredient waste, and give the table a clear, quick order. That can lift the average spend per cover as well as speed. A higher spend per head also gives you room to hire one extra server at peak, which may fix the front-of-house side too.

Your advantages are real. You know your regulars. You are a family business, so you can change the menu quickly without a head office. And a shorter Saturday menu is easy to test for four weeks and reverse if it fails.

Where the upside is limited:
- I don't know how many covers you do on a Saturday, or how long tickets take now. Without those numbers, a 50 per cent cut is a guess.
- The slowness may come from the kitchen, the front of house, or the bookings pattern. A smaller menu fixes only the first.
- Regulars may leave if their favourite dish disappears. Keep two or three of their most-ordered dishes on the Saturday list.
- A fixed-price set is only worth it if your food cost holds at the new price. I'd need your current cost of sales to say so.

What I'd want before committing: average ticket time on Saturdays, covers per Saturday, the wording of the complaints, and your top ten dishes by sales. With those, the Operator can check the numbers and the Scout can check the claims about speed.

Bottom line: Don't halve the whole menu. Test a short Saturday-only menu or fixed-price set for four weeks, and measure ticket times and covers against your current Saturdays before deciding.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

You seem to want a yes on halving the menu. I have judged it as if you hoped for no.

**My view:** Do not cut in half yet. You have not said where the delay is. Menu size only fixes it if the kitchen is the bottleneck. It could be the pass, the single fryer, table turn, drinks, or too few staff on Saturday. Cutting 50% blind risks lost regulars and lost revenue, and the delay may stay.

**Plan, in order**

1. **Sat 7 and Sat 14 Nov: measure.** Time 3 points on every table: order to food, food to bill, and sit to leave. Use the till or a notebook. Cost: £0. About 2 hours of your time per Saturday.
2. **Mon 16 Nov: find the cause.** List the 10 slowest dishes and the 10 best sellers. Typically 20% of dishes make about 60-70% of sales (an assumption, check your till data). Cut dishes that are slow and low-selling first.
3. **Sat 21 and 28 Nov: trial a Saturday-only short menu.** Cut 30-40%, not 50%. Keep every top seller. Reprint a simple sheet: £50-150. Brief staff for 30 minutes on Friday.
4. **Mon 30 Nov: review.** Compare average wait, covers, spend per head and complaints against the 2 baseline Saturdays.
5. **December: do not change anything.** It is peak season and a bad time to experiment. Keep whichever version won.

**Costs**
- Upfront: £50-300 (printing, staff briefing time).
- Running: roughly zero. Food waste and prep may fall.

**Numbers (illustrative, I do not have your figures)**
- **3 months (Feb 2027):** Target 15-20% shorter order-to-food time. Complaints down by half. Revenue flat to +5% if table turns improve.
- **6 months (May 2027):** Short menu settled. Target one extra table turn in the busy window. If you seat 60 covers per turn at £25 a head, one extra turn is about £1,500 per Saturday. That is only valid if demand is there.
- **12 months (Nov 2027):** About 45 Saturdays of trading. If the gain is even £500 per Saturday, that is about £22,000 a year. If it is £0, you have lost nothing but a reprint.

**What does not fit**
- If you have no table timing data, you cannot judge success. Do the measuring.
- Do not start changes in December.
- If you have one chef alone on Saturday, a menu cut will not replace a second pair of hands. A casual prep or pass hire at about £12.71 per hour (check the current National Living Wage, rates change each April) for 8 hours is about £100 per Saturday.

**Unsure about:** your covers, average spend, kitchen layout, staff numbers, and whether complaints come from food delay or from booking and seating delay.

Bottom line: Do not halve the menu blind. Time two Saturdays, then trial a 30-40% Saturday-only cut on 21 Nov, judge it on 30 Nov, and hold all changes in December.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

Menu cut evidence (statement for the board)

I can't tell yet whether the menu is the cause. The evidence says to find the bottleneck first, then decide.

**Hint check:** The question sounds like you want a yes or no on halving the menu. I judged it as if you hoped for the opposite.

**What the evidence shows**
- The best example is Chili's. It cut about 40% to 50% of its menu in 2017. It reported ticket times fell 12% within six weeks. [NRN: Chili's streamlined menu speeds up service times](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-finance/chili-s-streamlined-menu-speeds-up-service-times)
- That figure is the company's own. It has no control group. It also comes from a large chain with a big kitchen and head-office support. A family restaurant is not the same case.
- Sales at Chili's did not clearly rise from the speed gain. So a faster kitchen does not guarantee more money.
- I found no independent or academic study linking menu size to speed. Claims like "25 to 40 items is optimal" come from consultancy blogs with no source. Treat them as unproven.

**Common claims that are weak or misleading**
- "A smaller menu always speeds service." Not proven. It depends on which items you remove.
- "Slow service means a slow kitchen." Often false. Several trade sources say time is lost in other places: seating gaps, order entry, bussing and payment. [Sunday: seven overlooked reasons](https://sundayapp.com/whats-causing-service-delays-and-how-to-fix-them/) and [Restaurant Online: kitchen bottlenecks](https://www.restaurantonline.co.uk/Article/2020/02/28/Five-ways-to-solve-common-kitchen-bottlenecks/)
- Most of these sources are vendor blogs that sell software. Their figures are illustrative, not audited.

**What I'd check before cutting anything**
1. Split a Saturday into stages: wait to be seated, wait to order, kitchen time, wait for the bill. Time each one for two or three Saturdays.
2. Look at the complaints. Do they say "food took ages" or "nobody came to the table"? These need different fixes.
3. Find the slowest 10 dishes by cooking time and the few that clog one station, such as the fryer or grill. Removing those may give most of the benefit.
4. Check how many staff you roll out on Saturday compared with Friday.

**A cheaper test than halving**
Trim the slowest or most complex 20% to 30% of dishes on Saturdays only, for 3 to 4 weeks. Compare ticket times and complaints with the earlier Saturdays. This is reversible. Cutting half the menu permanently is not, because regulars may lose their favourite dish.

**What I'm unsure of**
- I don't know your menu size, kitchen layout, covers per Saturday, or staffing.
- I did not find UK-specific data on menu cuts and service speed.
- Chili's might have gained from other changes made at the same time.

Bottom line: Don't halve the menu yet. First time each stage of a Saturday service to find the real bottleneck. Then trial a Saturday-only cut of the slowest 20% to 30% of dishes for a month.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Don't cut the menu in half. First, time every stage of three Saturday services and read what the complaints actually say. If the kitchen turns out to be the bottleneck, trial a shorter menu on Saturdays only, dropping just the slow, low-selling dishes, and keep the full menu Monday to Friday.

## Why
- **The cause is unknown.** Slow Saturdays often start outside the kitchen: seating everyone at once, too few floor staff, drinks, or the bill. A smaller menu only fixes a kitchen bottleneck.
- **A full cut is out of proportion.** The problem is on one day, but halving would change all seven.
- **"Half" is a guess.** The right cut could be 3 dishes or 15. Only timings and till data can tell you which.
- **The evidence is thin.** The best example is Chili's, which reported ticket times 12% faster after a big menu cut. That is the company's own figure, with nothing to compare against, from a large chain. There is no independent research for a family restaurant.
- **A trial can be undone. A permanent cut is hard to undo.** Regulars who lose their favourite dish may not come back.

## Main risks
- **Misdiagnosis.** You lose dishes and Saturdays stay slow. This is the most likely way it fails.
- **Losing regulars quietly.** People who stop coming rarely leave a review, so you won't see it.
- **Allergen records.** The Food Information Regulations 2014 require accurate allergen information for every dish. A second Saturday menu means a second record to keep correct, and more chance of staff mistakes.
- **Money.** Reprinting, updating booking sites and writing off stock should cost about £50 to £300, though nobody has itemised this. An extra Saturday hire at the £12.71 per hour National Living Wage, plus employer National Insurance and holiday pay, costs about £115 to £125 for an 8-hour shift.
- **Waiting too long.** Every slow Saturday adds more bad reviews, and those take a long time to recover.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You jumped to a fix before checking the cause. You are treating "slow service" as "slow kitchen" without evidence. You also chose "half" because it is a round number, not because anything measured it. A family restaurant runs on its regulars, so a cut you can't easily reverse is the wrong first move.

## Next steps
1. **Saturdays 7, 14 and 21 Nov: time every table.** Have one family member with a phone stopwatch record four times: seated, ordered, food out and bill paid. Ask the cooks which dishes are slowest or clog one station, such as the fryer or grill. Pull your top sellers from the till.
2. **Sat 28 Nov, if seating or the floor is the problem: stagger bookings.** Space arrivals 15 minutes apart. It costs £0 and changes no dishes. Make no menu changes in December, because Christmas parties will distort any results.
3. **Saturdays 9 to 30 Jan 2027, only if the kitchen is the bottleneck: run a 4-week trial.** Use a Saturday-only menu that drops the slow, low-selling dishes and keeps every top seller and regulars' favourites. Write down the pass mark before you start: time from order to food down at least 15%, and Saturday covers and spend per head not down.
