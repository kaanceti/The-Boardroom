## Agenda

I have a job offer from another company. Should I tell my manager to try for a counter-offer?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: yes, use the offer as leverage, because the worst case is a "no" and you still have the new job.

Your question suggests you want a yes. So I have judged it as if you hoped for no.

**The cautious case**

A counter-offer play only works if you would truly be happy staying for more money. If your reasons for looking are the manager, the work, progression or culture, a pay rise does not touch them. You would be back to looking within a year, with less trust at your current employer.

The "worst case is a no" logic is wrong. Telling your manager can cost you more than a "no".

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Root cause unfixed (high likelihood, high impact).** You stay for money, the real problem remains, and you leave anyway later. You have lost the new offer and some goodwill. The often-quoted figure that "80% of people who accept counters leave within a year" is poorly sourced. I would not rely on it. The logic holds without it.
2. **Seen as a flight risk (medium likelihood, high impact).** You may be quietly passed over for promotion, key projects or training. UK redundancy selection must be fair. But scoring often includes subjective criteria, and you cannot easily prove bias.
3. **The new offer is lost (medium likelihood, high impact).** Offers are often "subject to references and checks". If you tell your employer before the offer is unconditional and in writing, a reference request could go badly. A slow negotiation can also lead the new company to withdraw or cool.
4. **The counter is weaker than it looks (medium likelihood, medium impact).**
   - It may be a one-off retention bonus with a clawback clause, not a base rise.
   - A £5,000 rise at the 40% tax band nets about £2,900 after tax and 2% NI.
   - Between £100,000 and £125,140 the effective rate is around 62%, so you would keep roughly £1,900.
5. **Your bluff is called (lower likelihood, high impact).** Your manager says "congratulations". If you did not really want the new job, you are now stuck with it.
6. **Reputation with the new employer (low to medium likelihood, medium impact).** Accepting and then reneging, or being visibly used as leverage, is remembered in small industries.

**Doing nothing is a real option.** If you prefer the new job, simply resign and take it. If you prefer staying, decline it quietly. You can then ask for a pay review later, using market data rather than a threat.

**I am unsure of:** your sector, your pay level, why you looked, and how replaceable you are. Each of these changes the odds.

**What would have to be true for the counter-offer play to work**
- Money is the only reason you looked.
- The new offer is unconditional and in writing, with a start date.
- You would genuinely take the new job if no counter comes.
- Your employer has a record of countering without punishing people later.
- You are hard to replace in under 3 to 6 months.
- Any counter is a base salary rise, in writing, not a bonus with clawback.

Bottom line: Only ask for a counter if all six conditions above hold. Otherwise decide which job you want and act on that, without using the offer as leverage.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: No. Keep quiet, resign, and do not bluff with your manager.

I would argue the bold end. Tell your manager, but ask for something specific, not a vague "counter."

The case for boldness:
- The outside offer is real information. It shows the market values you. You can use it to reset your pay, title or scope.
- Managers often prefer to pay now rather than hire and train someone new. Replacing a person can take months, and the costs are real, though I cannot give you exact figures for your sector.
- Asking gives your current employer a chance to invest in you before you leave.

Where the upside is limited:
- A counter-offer often fixes pay. It may not fix the reasons you started looking, such as workload, your manager or the direction of the work. A bigger salary may not last if those stay the same.
- Some managers say yes now and then quietly rank you as a flight risk. That can hurt your standing.
- If you do not really want to stay, a bluff will show. Only ask if you would accept a good enough counter.

The unusual move: before you speak to your manager, write down three things. Name your walk-away pay figure, the two or three changes that would make you stay for a year or more, and a date for a review. Then ask for those. If they agree, get it in writing. If they refuse, you have a clear answer and a clean exit.

What I am unsure of:
- Your current pay, the value of the new offer, and whether the new role is firm and in writing.
- Why you are looking. The answer changes the advice.
- Your manager's record on counter-offers.
- Whether any figure about how often counter-offers last is accurate. I have no verified data here, so Scout should check any number before you rely on it.

Bottom line: Ask for a counter only if you know your walk-away number and the specific changes you need, and you would accept them. Otherwise, take the new job.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Statement to the board**

Missing facts: your salary, the offer, your notice period and your real reason for leaving. I use example numbers below. Swap in yours.

**The order of work**

1. **Today, 30 minutes.** Write down why you want to leave. Write your walk-away figure. If money is not the main reason, a counter-offer will not fix it.
2. **Days 1 to 3.** Get the offer in writing. Check salary, bonus, pension, start date, notice, probation length and any clawback. Do not tell your manager until it is signed.
3. **Decide before you talk.** Would you stay if they matched it? If the answer is no, do not ask. You would burn trust for nothing.
4. **Days 3 to 5, one 30 minute meeting.** Say you have an offer and are leaning towards it. Ask what they can change. Do not bluff. Do not name a number you would not accept.
5. **Set a deadline.** Ask for a written answer within 3 working days. Most offers expire in 5 to 10 days.
6. **Day 8 to 10.** Decide. Resign in writing or accept in writing. Check your contract for notice, often 1 to 3 months.

**Cost**

Upfront cost is nil. The cost is risk and about 3 to 4 hours of your time. Do not hand in notice before the new contract is signed.

**Example numbers**

Say you earn £50,000 and the offer is £55,000. The gain is £5,000 gross. Above about £50,270 you pay 40% tax plus 2% National Insurance, so the take-home gain is roughly £2,900 a year, or £240 a month. This is approximate.

**If you stay on a counter-offer**
- 3 months: raise on your payslip. Little else changes.
- 6 months: if your reason for leaving was not fixed, you will probably be looking again.
- 12 months: I am unsure of the odds. Commonly quoted figures say many people who accept counter-offers leave within a year. I have not checked the source. Scout should.

**If you take the new job**
- 3 months: likely still in probation, often 3 to 6 months. Notice rights are weaker. I am unsure how the planned UK employment law changes will affect this, so check.
- 6 months: probation done, and you are earning the extra £240 a month.
- 12 months: about £2,900 extra take-home banked, plus whatever the role adds to your CV.

**What does not fit**

If you want a counter-offer only to feel valued, that is vague, and I will not build a plan on it. Also, if you tell your manager without being ready to leave, you lose your position either way. Managers often remember who threatened to go.

**Bias check:** your wording suggests you may want leverage more than a move. I judged this as if you wanted to leave cleanly.

Bottom line: Only ask for a counter-offer if you would truly stay for a match, and only after the new offer is in writing. Give a 3 working day deadline. If money is not your real reason, take the new job.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

Statement to the board

The owner's wording ("should I tell my manager to try for a counter-offer?") suggests they lean towards doing it. I have judged it as if they hoped for the opposite.

**What the evidence says**

- The popular claim that "80 to 90% of people who accept a counter-offer leave within a year" is weak. I could not trace a real study behind it. Treat it as false or unproven.
- The better-documented figure is about 40 to 52%.
  - Robert Half UK (Opinium survey of 2,000 UK workers, Dec 2021) found 40.8% of acceptors left within 12 months. [Robert Half UK](https://www.roberthalf.com/gb/en/about/press/half-counteroffers-successful-hiring-crisis-continues)
  - A CEB/Gartner figure of roughly 50% within 12 months is often quoted. Its method is debated.
  - A Robert Half UK page reports about 34% left within 6 months. Another summary of the same release says 74% left within a year. These conflict, so I am unsure which is right.
- Randstad found 59% of employees see counter-offers as "too little, too late". It also found 37% would still plan to leave within a year after accepting one. [Randstad UK](https://www.randstad.co.uk/banking-style-counter-offers-spread-over-uk-jobs-scene/)
- Randstad says the average rise needed to turn down a new job is about 18% of current salary. A 2017 survey put it nearer 25%. That one is old.
- Employers do not always respond. One UK survey found most employers have never made a counter-offer, even though over half said they would. [HR Review](https://hrreview.co.uk/hr-news/strategy-news/the-majority-of-employers-have-never-made-a-counter-offer-despite-over-half-saying-they-would/43622)

**What this means**

- Most acceptors do stay. Roughly half to 60% were still there after a year. So a counter-offer is not a trap by default.
- The surveys cannot say why the leavers left. They also do not separate forced exits from chosen ones. I found no UK data on whether acceptors were later passed over for promotion or let go.
- Asking for a counter-offer is different from receiving one. Saying "I have an offer, can you match it?" signals you are leaving. Your manager may then doubt your loyalty even if you stay. That risk is real, but I found no hard numbers on it.

**Questions the owner should answer first**

1. Do they actually want the new job? If yes, a counter-offer is unnecessary. If they only want leverage, that is a bluff, and the new employer may withdraw if it gets out.
2. What is the main reason for looking? Pay can be fixed by a counter-offer. Poor management, a dead-end role or culture usually cannot.
3. Is the gap above about 18%? If the new offer is much higher, a match is unlikely.
4. Is the new offer in writing, with a start date and a probation period? In the UK, a new employee usually has little protection against unfair dismissal until 2 years of service. I have not rechecked that threshold, and recent law changes may affect it. Check before relying on it.

**Practical points**

- Do not resign first and hope. Do not accept the new job verbally and then bargain with it.
- If they do ask, they should name one clear request, such as a salary figure or a title, and a deadline.
- If they accept a counter-offer, they should get it in writing.

Bottom line: Do not ask for a counter-offer as a tactic. Only raise it if you would honestly stay for the right pay and the problem is money. Even then, expect about a 40 to 50% chance of leaving within a year, not the 90% that people quote.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Only tell your manager if pay is the main reason you looked and you would really stay for a specific package you have already written down. If something else drives you, such as your manager, the work or a lack of progression, don't ask for a counter-offer. Decide which job you want and act on it. Before you speak to anyone, get the new offer unconditional and in writing, but do not sign it yet.

## Why
- **A counter-offer only fixes pay.** If you looked for other reasons, staying for money just delays the move, and you will have lost some trust.
- **The numbers are less bleak than people say, but still not good.** The "80 to 90% leave within a year" claim has no traceable source. Better evidence is a Robert Half UK survey of 2,000 workers in 2021, which found 40.8% of people who accepted a counter-offer left within 12 months. Other figures are around 50%, and the sources conflict. So it is not a trap by default, but it is close to a coin flip.
- **The prize shrinks after tax.** Say you earn £50,000 and get a £5,000 rise. You keep about £2,900 a year, roughly £240 a month, at 40% tax plus 2% NI. Between £100,000 and £125,140 the marginal rate is about 62%, so you would keep roughly £1,900.
- **You may get nothing.** One UK survey found most employers have never made a counter-offer.

## Main risks
- **Flight-risk label.** Once you say you have an offer, you may be quietly passed over for promotions or projects, even if you stay. You cannot undo this, and it is hard to prove.
- **Losing the new offer.** Slow talks, or signing and then backing out, can make the new employer withdraw and damage your name with them and any recruiter.
- **A weak counter.** It may be a one-off retention bonus with a clawback clause rather than a base pay rise. Check your current contract for clawback on bonuses or relocation, and for restrictive covenants.
- **"Congratulations, go."** Your employer may accept your resignation, bring your leaving date forward or put you on garden leave.
- **Weaker legal protection in a new job.** You currently need 2 years' service before you can claim unfair dismissal. The Employment Rights Act 2025 is reported to cut this to 6 months from January 2027. I am not certain of the date, so check gov.uk.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You asked how to get a counter-offer, not which job you want. That suggests you may want the offer as leverage rather than as a real choice. A bluff shows, and if your manager calls it you are stuck with whatever you can get. Also, you can ask for a pay review based on market data without mentioning the offer at all, and you have not considered that.

## Next steps
1. **Today, 1 hour.** Write down your main reason for leaving, your walk-away salary figure and two or three changes that would keep you for 12 months or more. If you would not stay for those, stop here and take the new job.
2. **Within 3 days.** Get the new offer unconditional and in writing, and check the salary, pension, start date, probation and notice period. Ask the new employer if they can improve the package. That costs you nothing.
3. **If you go ahead.** Hold one 30-minute meeting with your manager. Make one specific request and ask for a written answer within 3 working days. Accept only a base pay rise in writing. Otherwise, resign in writing.
