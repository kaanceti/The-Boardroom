# Boardroom minutes: I run a small online clothing shop. Should I start offering free returns?

Date: 2026-11-03 20:31  
Mode: full

## Agenda

I run a small online clothing shop. Should I start offering free returns?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: yes, free returns lift conversion, so offer them and absorb the cost.

I argue for the cautious end: do not offer free returns yet. Keep the legal minimum and test anything more on a small scale first.

**What the law already requires**

Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, customers can cancel within 14 days of delivery. You must refund within 14 days of getting the goods back. You can make them pay return postage if you state this clearly before purchase. So free returns are a voluntary cost, not a legal duty.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood x impact**

1. **Your return rate rises, not just your conversion. (High likelihood, high impact.)** Free returns invite "bracketing": ordering two sizes and sending one back. Online clothing return rates are often quoted at 20 to 30%, sometimes higher. I cannot verify your category's figure. The danger is that conversion gains are smaller than the extra returns.

2. **Cost per order is larger than it looks. (High, medium.)** This is an illustrative sum, not your data:
   - Average order £40, gross margin 50%, so £20 profit.
   - Return rate 25%.
   - Return label about £3 to £4 (Evri or Royal Mail rates vary). Handling about £2.
   - 10% of returns are unsellable.
   - Result: roughly £2 per order, which is about 10% of gross profit.

   If the return rate climbs to 35%, the cost is nearer £3. On thin margins that can wipe out net profit.

3. **It is hard to reverse. (Medium, medium.)** Withdrawing a perk annoys repeat buyers and draws bad reviews. The big players went the other way. Boohoo started charging £1.99 in 2022. Zara charges £1.95 for postal returns. ASOS added fees for low-value orders in 2023. They have far better data than you. That they retreated is a warning sign.

4. **Fraud and wardrobing. (Medium, low to medium.)** Worn-once returns, swapped items and empty parcels are more common when returning costs the customer nothing.

5. **Cash flow and admin time. (Medium, low.)** In a small shop, returns processing is usually your own unpaid hours.

**Most likely reason it fails:** the return rate rises faster than sales. Margin quietly erodes, and you only notice after a quarter.

**What would have to be true for it to work**
- Your margin per order comfortably covers about £3 to £4 of return cost.
- Your current return rate is known and low, ideally under 15%.
- Competitors in your niche already offer free returns and you are losing sales to them, with evidence such as abandoned-basket feedback.
- You can cap it, for example free returns only above a £50 spend, or as exchange-only or store credit.

**Where I am unsure:** if your margins are high (60%+) and returns are rare, the case for caution is weak. The figures above are assumptions to replace with your own.

Bottom line: Do not switch on blanket free returns. Measure your real return rate and cost per return, then trial free exchanges or a spend threshold for 8 to 12 weeks before committing.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: yes, free returns lift sales, so match the big shops.

I'd go further. Make free returns your headline promise, not just a checkout line. Small UK fashion shops compete on trust, and a clear, generous returns policy is one of the few things a small shop can out-promise a large one on.

The upside:
- Shoppers hesitate most over fit. A free return removes the main reason to hold back, so more people buy.
- Customers who feel safe often order larger baskets and come back.
- Each return also gives you size and fit data. Use it to fix product pages and size charts, which cuts future returns.

The upside is limited if:
- Your current return rate is low. Then the cost is small, but so is the gain.
- Return postage is large relative to your average order. Get a real quote from Royal Mail or your courier. I do not have current UK rates, so do not rely on my guess.
- Your margins are thin. Returns cost you postage, handling, and sometimes resale value.

Legal point, to check: under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, you must give customers a 14-day cancellation right on online sales. If you do not clearly say the customer pays return postage, the law generally puts that cost on you. So free returns is a choice, and it is a clean one. Confirm the detail with a source or adviser, since I am not a lawyer.

Options that keep most of the upside with less risk:
- Free returns on full-price items, with a small fee on sale items.
- Free exchanges for a different size, with a fee for refunds.
- Free returns for a set period, such as 30 days, reviewed after 90 days.

What I would want before deciding:
1. Your current return rate, by product type.
2. Your average order value.
3. Quoted return postage per parcel.
4. Your gross margin per item.
5. Whether your main rivals offer free returns. Check their sites.

With those figures, I can say how large the upside really is. Without them, it is an informed guess.

Bottom line: Offer free returns, make it a visible promise, and use the return data to fix fit. Confirm the cost per return before you commit.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

Not enough numbers to say yes yet. I will use assumptions, and you must replace them with your own by Friday 7 November.

**Assumptions (guesses, not your data):** 1,000 orders a month, £45 average order, 20% return rate, 55% gross margin (£24.75 per order), £3.50 per return label, 10 minutes handling per return.

**Running cost of free returns:**
- Labels: 200 returns x £3.50 = £700 a month.
- Handling: 200 x 10 minutes = about 33 hours a month. At £12 an hour that is £400, or your own time.
- Total: about £1,100 a month.
- Break-even: about 44 extra orders a month, a 4.4% sales lift, if return rates stay flat.
- If free returns push the rate to 25%, cost rises to about £1,400 a month. You then need about 57 extra orders, a 5.7% lift.

**Timeline at those numbers (cost only):**
- 3 months: about £3,300.
- 6 months: about £6,600.
- 12 months: about £13,200.
- Upside depends on the lift. A 10% lift is 100 extra orders, worth about £2,475 a month in margin. That would net about £1,000 a month. A 3% lift loses about £350 a month.

**Steps:**
1. By 7 November (2 hours): pull 12 months of data. You need orders, average order, return rate, current label cost, and return reasons.
2. By 14 November (3 hours): get quotes from Royal Mail, Evri and DPD for prepaid return labels. Check whether a QR code drop-off option is cheaper.
3. By 21 November (4 hours): set up a returns portal or app. Upfront cost is probably £0 to £200, and apps are often £0 to £50 a month. I am unsure of current pricing.
4. 24 November to 19 January: run a test. Offer free returns to half of visitors if your platform allows it. Otherwise compare the 8 weeks before and after. This window covers Black Friday and January sales, so results will be noisy. Keep a clean comparison.
5. By 26 January: decide using three numbers: conversion rate, return rate, margin after returns.

**Cheaper options to test first:**
- Free exchanges, paid refunds.
- Free returns over £50, which also lifts the average order.
- Free returns as store credit.
- Free returns for a members list.

**Legal point:** under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, customers have 14 days to cancel. You must refund the standard delivery cost. If you did not clearly state before purchase that the customer pays return postage, you may already owe it. Check your terms.

**Does not fit:** if you handle returns alone and have under 300 orders a month, the labour and cash drag will not pay back quickly. Returned stock that cannot be resold, such as worn or damaged items, adds further loss. I have not costed that.

**What I am unsure of:** the real return rate effect, the sales lift, and your margin. Industry figures vary widely, and I cannot verify them.

Bottom line: Do not launch free returns permanently yet. Send me your four numbers, then run an 8-week test from 24 November, and go ahead only if the sales lift beats about 5% with returns held near 20%.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

Here is what the evidence says. The owner's question leans towards "yes", so I judged it as if they hoped for "no".

**What the evidence supports**
- Free returns are no longer the norm. [Ingrid's 2026 report](https://www.ingrid.com/blog/paid-e-commerce-returns-strategy) says 35% of the UK's top 100 fashion brands charge for returns, up from 23% in 2023. [Sendcloud's count](https://www.retail-insight-network.com/news/uk-fashion-retailers-return-fees/) is far higher, at 79%. The samples differ, so treat it as a range. Both come from vendors.
- Shoppers say they care. A [Narvar and Reshop survey](https://redo.com/blogs/how-free-returns-can-positively-impact-conversion-rate) found 82% say free returns make them more likely to buy, and 47% avoid brands that charge. These are US, self-reported answers. They show what people say, not what they do.
- The best behavioural evidence is a study by Bower and colleagues. It covered two retailers over 49 months. As [summarised secondhand](https://chainstoreage.com/how-do-returns-affect-online-purchase-decisions), customers who paid for returns cut their spending by 75% to 100% over two years. Customers with free returns spent more. I could not read the paper, so the exact figures are unverified. Also, those retailers were large. A small shop may see a different effect.
- A [Danish fashion retailer study](https://hhs.se/contentassets/dc8f9122647141bebe035a7bf838bfc6/early-insight-32_product-returns.pdf) found a mostly positive effect from free returns, through loyalty. It also reported that returns rise.

**What cuts against the idea**
- Fashion return rates are high. Estimates run from [23.6% (ZigZag, UK clothing)](https://www.zigzag.global/es/zigzags-annual-returns-report) to 25% to 40% (Ingrid). Free returns usually push the rate up.
- Return label costs are real. I found only undated reseller rates: about £2.24 + VAT (Evri via a reseller), £3.95 + VAT (DPD Shop) and £5.22 + VAT (Collect+), as [listed by returns platforms](https://help.zigswag.global/en/articles/1619199-yodel-and-evri-return-label-pricing). I could not confirm 2026 carrier prices. Check them yourself.
- Return costs are more than the label. They also cover handling, repackaging and unsellable stock.

**Rough worked example (my arithmetic, not sourced)**
- Take a £40 order with a 30% gross margin, so £12 of margin.
- A £4 label on a 30% return rate costs £1.20 per order. That is 10% of margin.
- If the return rate rises to 40%, the cost is £1.60. That is 13% of margin.
- Add £2 handling per return, and it reaches about £2.40 per order, or 20% of margin.
- Free returns pay off only if they lift sales or repeat orders by more than that.

**Common claims that are false or outdated**
- "Everyone offers free returns." Outdated. Paying for returns is now common among large UK fashion retailers.
- "Charging cuts returns a lot." Not shown. Ingrid argues fees shift cost more than they change why people return items.
- "Free returns always lift sales." Not proven for small shops. The strong evidence comes from large retailers.

**Unsure**
- I don't know your return rate, margin or order value. These decide the answer.
- Whether the UK evidence applies to a small shop is unclear.

**Cheaper options to test:** free returns above a basket value, free exchanges, or free returns for loyal customers. Run it as a 8 to 12 week test and track conversion, return rate and margin.

Bottom line: The evidence does not show that free returns pay off for a small shop, so test a limited version first and don't commit until you know your own return rate and margin.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Do not switch on blanket free returns now, and do not make them a headline promise. First measure your real return rate, margin and label cost. Then trial free size exchanges (refunds stay paid) for 12 weeks from Monday 1 February 2027. Keep the exchanges only if margin per order holds and orders rise clearly.

## Why
- **The law doesn't require free returns.** Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, a customer can charge back return postage to you only if you didn't tell them before purchase that they pay it. Free returns are a choice you'd be paying for.
- **The cost is bigger than the label.** You pay postage (roughly £2.24 to £5.22 plus VAT per label, undated reseller rates), handling time and the loss on stock you can't resell. Free returns also invite customers to order two sizes and send one back, so your return rate rises. Each extra return loses the margin on that sale.
- **You need a big sales lift to break even.** At a £40 order and about £1.80 of return cost per order, you need roughly 10% more orders at a 50% margin. At a 30% margin you need roughly 18%. No source the board found shows a lift that big for a small shop.
- **Big UK fashion retailers are moving the other way.** Between 35% and 79% of top UK fashion brands now charge for returns. Both figures come from vendors and their samples differ.
- **Exchanges get most of the benefit for less risk.** The main thing stopping people buying clothes online is worry about fit. A free size swap removes that worry without paying for people who just change their minds.

## Main risks
- **Margin loss.** Your return rate rises faster than your sales, and you only see it after a quarter.
- **Hard to undo.** Withdrawing a public promise looks like a downgrade and draws bad reviews.
- **Misleading results.** Holiday returns arrive 2 to 4 weeks late, so a test that ends in January would make free returns look better than they are. A small shop may also not get enough orders to tell a real change from noise. Aim for at least a few hundred orders in each period.
- **Legal.** You must always refund the original standard delivery charge. If your terms don't clearly say the customer pays return postage, you may already owe it. Showing different return terms to different visitors could be a problem under the DMCC Act 2024. The board is unsure, so compare before and after instead.
- **Fraud.** Some customers will return worn items or swap in different ones.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You're asking about a policy whose answer depends entirely on three numbers you didn't give: your return rate, your margin and your cost per return. If you don't know them offhand, that is the first problem to fix, whatever you decide. Also, don't count your own hours as free. Value them at least at the National Living Wage.

2. **By 28 November:** get return label quotes from Royal Mail, Evri and DPD. Check the returns policies of your five closest rivals.
3. **1 February to 25 April 2027:** run free exchanges, or free returns over £50. Measure returns until 23 May. Continue only if orders rise at least 10%, the return rate stays within 3 points of today, and margin per order holds.
