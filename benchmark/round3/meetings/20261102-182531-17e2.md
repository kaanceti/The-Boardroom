# Boardroom minutes: I'm 29 with £8,000 on a credit card at 24% interest and £3,000 in savings.…

Mode: full

## Agenda

I'm 29 with £8,000 on a credit card at 24% interest and £3,000 in savings. Should I use my savings to pay off part of the card?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: yes, put most or all of the £3,000 on the card, because 24% beats any savings rate.

**What the numbers say**

- £3,000 on the card at 24% APR costs about £720 a year, roughly £60 a month.
- £3,000 in an easy-access account at about 4% earns about £120 a year. It is likely tax-free under the £1,000 Personal Savings Allowance.
- So keeping the cash costs you about £600 a year, or £50 a month. That is the price of the cautious option. It is real, and I will not pretend it is small.

**Risks of paying it off, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **You re-spend the freed credit.** This is the most likely failure. The balance drops to £5,000, the space fills back up, and you end with £8,000 owed and £0 saved. Likelihood: high if the debt came from regular overspending. Impact: high.
2. **An emergency hits with no cash.** Rent, a deposit, a car repair at a garage that wants a bank transfer. Cash withdrawals on a card usually cost a fee of about 3% plus a higher rate, often near 30%, charged from day one. Likelihood: moderate over 12 months. Impact: medium.
3. **The card stops being your backup.** Lenders can cut limits, and one missed payment can mean fees and a credit file mark. If you are relying on the card as your emergency fund, you have no fund you control. Likelihood: low to moderate. Impact: high if income drops at the same time.
4. **You skip a cheaper fix.** A 0% balance transfer card, if you qualify, might cost a 3% fee (about £240) for 20 months or more at 0%. That saves more than the £3,000 payment does, and keeps the cash. Likelihood you qualify: unknown. It depends on your credit file and income.

**What I do not know**

Your income, whether it is stable, your monthly essentials, whether you pay more than the minimum, and why the debt built up. Each of these changes the answer.

**The cautious case, honestly**

It is moderate, not strong. Spending your savings is not reckless. Leaving yourself with £0 is the risk.

**What would have to be true for paying it off to work**

- Your income is stable for the next 6 to 12 months.
- The card is closed to new spending, or you have a written budget that clears the rest by a set date.
- You keep at least £1,000, or one month of essential costs, in cash.
- You have already checked eligibility for a 0% transfer using a soft-search tool, which does not mark your file.

**Order of actions**

First, soft-search for a balance transfer this week. If you qualify, transfer, keep the savings, and pay the card off within the 0% period. If you do not qualify, pay £2,000 and keep £1,000. Stop all new spending on the card.

Bottom line: Do not empty your savings; try a 0% balance transfer first, and if that fails, pay about £2,000 and keep at least £1,000 as a cash buffer.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: Yes, use the savings to cut the 24% debt. That is the safe and sensible choice.

I'd go further. Use the full £3,000 now, and treat the card as the first target, not a bill to manage.

Why this is strong:
- At 24% APR, £3,000 costs about £60 a month in interest. Paying it off saves roughly £720 a year before any compounding. Very few savings accounts pay anywhere near 24%, so the return on paying the card is far higher than any safe return on cash.
- After paying £3,000, you still owe about £5,000, which costs about £100 a month. That is the real target.
- The bold move: check for a 0% balance transfer card. UK deals often charge a transfer fee of around 3%. Moving £5,000 would cost about £150 in fees. If you then paid roughly £420 a month for 12 months, you would clear the balance with almost no interest. Compare that with about £1,200 of interest at 24% over the same year. Offers and fees change often, so check current terms before you apply.
- Stop adding new spending to the card. Otherwise any gain is lost.

Where the upside is limited:
- I do not know your income, bills or job security. If you have no other emergency cover, using all your savings could leave you relying on the card again, which is the worst outcome.
- A balance transfer needs a credit check. Approval is not guaranteed, and a new application can dent your score for a short time.
- I do not know your savings rate. If it is above 24%, which is very unlikely, keep the savings.

I am unsure of current UK balance transfer offers, your credit score and your monthly cash flow. Confirm those before acting.

Bottom line: Pay the card down now, and probably use all £3,000, but only if you can rebuild a small cash buffer soon and avoid new card spending. A 0% balance transfer could be the stronger next step, if approved.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

Interest maths (my own calculation):
- £8,000 at 24% APR costs about £160 a month in interest at the start. That is roughly £1,920 a year.
- Putting £3,000 on the card saves about £60 a month. That is about £720 a year.
- The same £3,000 in savings at about 4.5% to 5% earns roughly £135 to £150 a year before tax. Most people pay no tax on it, because of the Personal Savings Allowance.
- So the card debt costs about five times what the savings earn. On numbers alone, paying it down wins clearly.

**Common claims that are false or out of date**
- "Always keep six months of savings before touching debt." Several guides now suggest a small starter buffer, then debt, then a bigger fund. Those guides are mixed and not official. I could not find MoneyHelper's own wording, so check it yourself. [Sources found](https://www.sofi.com/article/money-life/which-comes-first-saving-or-paying-down-credit-card-debt/), [Raisin UK](https://raisin.co.uk/savings/pay-off-debts-or-save)
- "Savings pay enough to offset card interest." Best easy-access rates are about 4.5% to 5%, and some of those include bonuses or have withdrawal limits. They are nowhere near 24%. [Moneyfacts](https://moneyfactscompare.co.uk/savings-accounts/easy-access-savings-accounts/)
- "0% cards only last 12 to 18 months." Offers of 34 to 38 months exist, with fees of about 3% to 3.5%. Terms change often, and the sources I found disagree. [Nationwide](https://www.nationwide.co.uk/media/news/nationwide-launches-its-longest-ever-0-percent-interest-balance-transfer-credit-card), [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/money/credit-cards/balance-transfer-credit-cards)

**A better option than choosing between the two**
A 0% balance transfer could cut the interest to nothing. The fee on £8,000 at 3.3% is about £264. That is far less than £1,920 a year of interest. Approval depends on your credit score and the limit you are offered. It may not cover the full £8,000.

**What I'd weigh**
- Keep a small buffer. A common rule of thumb is one month of essential spending, perhaps £1,000 to £1,500. I don't know your costs, so treat that as a guess.
- Paying £1,500 to £2,000 off the card saves about £30 to £40 a month.
- If an emergency hits and the card is near its limit, you may have to borrow again, possibly at the same 24%.
- Paying the card down does not lock the money away. On many cards you can borrow it again, but that depends on your limit and the issuer.
- Job security matters. A stable job and family backup make a thin buffer safer.

**Unsure about**
- Your income, monthly outgoings and credit score.
- Your card's minimum payment and whether you are still adding spending.
- Current live rates and offers. My search found nothing dated November 2026.

Bottom line: Yes, use most of the savings on the card, and keep about £1,000 to £1,500 as a buffer. The card costs roughly five times what the savings earn. Check at the same time whether a 0% balance transfer could move the whole £8,000 and save more.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Yes, use your savings on the card, but not all of them. Put £2,000 on the card now and keep £1,000 in cash as a buffer. Before then, use soft-search tools to check whether you could get a 0% balance transfer card for the rest. If you can, moving the balance to it is a bigger saving than anything you do with your savings.

## Why
- The card charges 24%. Savings pay about 4% to 5%. Keeping £3,000 in savings while you owe on the card costs you about £600 a year.
- Here is how the amount you pay in changes things, assuming you pay £300 a month at 24% and spend nothing new on the card:
  - Pay in nothing: about 37 months to clear, about £3,000 interest.
  - Pay in £2,000: about 25 months, about £1,500 interest.
  - Pay in all £3,000: about 20 months, about £1,000 interest.
- That last £1,000 saves only about £500, and it leaves you with no cash at all. One car or boiler bill would then go back on the card at 24%, or cost even more as a cash withdrawal (often about 3% fee plus around 30% interest).
- A 0% transfer card usually charges a fee of about 3%. On £6,000 that is about £180. Paying the card at 24% instead costs over £1,000 in interest.

## Main risks
- **Running the card back up.** This is the most likely way the plan fails. If the balance drops to £6,000 and then fills up again, you end up owing £8,000 with no savings.
- **Not getting a transfer card.** You may be refused, or offered a limit too low to move everything. You won't know until you check.
- **Breaking the 0% deal's terms.** One missed payment can cancel the 0% rate. Many deals also make you transfer within 60 to 90 days. Any balance left when the 0% period ends goes back to a high rate.
- **Using the card as your emergency fund.** The card company can cut your limit without warning. Cash you hold yourself is safer.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You asked how to split your money, but the real question is why £8,000 built up on the card. If you regularly spend more than you earn, paying it down only treats the symptom and the card will fill up again within a year. Work out your actual monthly surplus. If it is under about £200, this is an income or spending problem, and no way of splitting your savings will fix it.

## Next steps
1. **By 9 November:** Write down your monthly income, bills, and what you can really pay the card. Run soft-search eligibility checks for 0% balance transfer cards (MoneySavingExpert or a card provider's checker). These do not affect your credit file. Don't make full applications yet.
2. **By 16 November:** Pay £2,000 from savings onto the card. If a transfer looks likely, apply after that payment clears, so the fee is charged on about £6,000, not £8,000. Stop all new spending on the card.
3. **Set a direct debit for more than the minimum payment, for the day after payday.** Aim to clear the balance before any 0% period ends. Once the card is cleared, rebuild savings to £1,500 to £2,000 or more.
