## Agenda

Should I take a six-month unpaid career break to travel, using about £12,000 of my savings?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: "Go, you'll never regret it, money comes back." I argue the cautious end: do not go yet, or go shorter, until four conditions are met. No clear hint from the owner on which answer they want.

**The real cost is not £12,000.** For someone on £35,000 gross, six months unpaid is about £14,000 of take-home pay lost. Add roughly £900 to £1,500 of employer pension contributions you will not receive (at a typical 3% to 5%), plus any growth on that over decades. Add UK costs that keep running: rent or mortgage, phone, storage, subscriptions. A realistic all-in cost is £25,000 to £35,000, not £12,000. I do not know your salary, so check this with your own figures.

**Ranked risks (likelihood, impact):**

1. **No guaranteed job on return (high if quitting, low if formal sabbatical; impact high).** This is the most likely way it goes wrong. UK job searches often take 3 to 6 months. That could turn a 6-month break into 9 to 12 months without income. Get any sabbatical agreement in writing, including role, salary and return date.
2. **Savings left too thin (medium, high).** If £12,000 leaves you with less than 3 to 6 months of expenses, one bad event wipes you out. Examples are a boiler, a medical bill abroad, or a slow job search.
3. **Budget overrun (high, medium).** Six months for £12,000 is about £66 a day, all in. That works in South East Asia or South America. It does not work in Australia, Japan or Western Europe. Flights, visas and insurance eat 15% to 25% before you arrive.
4. **Housing (medium, medium).** Subletting without your landlord's written consent can breach your tenancy. Paying empty rent on £1,000 a month adds £6,000.
5. **Health and insurance (low, high).** Long-stay cover costs roughly £300 to £800 for six months. Many policies exclude pre-existing conditions or adventure sports. Without cover, one hospital stay abroad can cost five figures.
6. **National Insurance gap (low, low).** You probably still get a qualifying year if you earn above roughly £6,500 in that tax year. I am unsure of the 2026/27 threshold, so check your record on GOV.UK.

**The honest limit of my case:** if this is a formal sabbatical, the risk is modest and mostly reversible. Caution is weak in that situation. The strong case for caution applies only if you are resigning or your buffer is small.

**What would have to be true for it to work:**
- A return date and job are agreed in writing, or you have a credible plan to find work within 3 months.
- After spending £12,000, you still hold at least 6 months of essential costs.
- Your daily budget is tested against your actual destinations, with a 20% contingency.
- Housing is legally sorted, and insurance covers your health and activities.

Bottom line: Go only if your job is guaranteed in writing and you keep 6 months of savings after spending the £12,000. Otherwise wait, save more, or take 6 to 8 weeks instead.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: keep the savings, build a bigger buffer first, and take a shorter trip later.

I think the bold case is stronger than most advisers will admit.

The best version is to take the six months now, while a career gap costs the least. A gap at 25 to 35 is easier to explain and recover from than one later in life. Travel can also build skills that many employers value: adapting to new places, handling unfamiliar problems, working across cultures, and sometimes learning a language. If you can tell a clear story about why you stepped away, the gap becomes a point in your favour.

Your advantages:
- You have £12,000 saved. Spread over six months, that is about £2,000 a month. Many routes cost less than that, but prices vary a lot, so check current costs for your planned countries before you rely on this figure.
- You can plan the budget in advance and set a firm daily spending limit.
- You can choose lower-cost destinations to stretch the money, or add a few weeks of paid remote or freelance work along the way.

The unusual move: do not treat this as a break from work. Ask your employer about a sabbatical or an unpaid leave agreement with a guaranteed return date. Some UK employers will agree to this, and it removes most of the career risk. If that is not possible, look for a remote or freelance contract you could do from abroad, even for a few hours a week.

Where the upside is limited:
- The £12,000 does not grow. Once spent, it is gone.
- Six months without income can delay pension contributions, a house deposit, or other goals.
- If your field is slow to hire, a gap could cost more than expected.
- The money may not cover an emergency at home or a medical problem abroad, so check your travel insurance and whether you keep any cash reserve.

I am unsure of several things. I do not know your job, your age, your debts, your rent or mortgage, whether you have other savings, or how strong your job market is. Those facts would change the answer a lot.

Bottom line: The upside is real but modest. Go if the £12,000 is not your only safety net and you have a credible plan to return to work, ideally with a sabbatical agreement or remote income. If either condition is missing, delay until you have a bigger buffer.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Go only if you get unpaid leave in writing with a guaranteed return date, or if you have savings beyond this £12,000. If you would have to resign and £12,000 is your whole safety net, don't go for six months. Take a trip of about three months instead, or wait until you have saved more.

## Why
- **The return is the real risk, not the trip.** With an agreed return date, the downside is small and you can undo it. If you resign, you could come home with almost nothing left and spend months looking for work.
- **£12,000 is less than it looks.**
  - Over 182 days it is £66 a day.
  - Flights, insurance, vaccines and visas probably take £2,200 to £4,500 before you leave. These are estimates, not quotes.
  - Bills at home keep running. Rent of £800 a month comes to £4,800.
  - With both of those taken off, you have roughly £21 to £40 a day. That is only enough for budget travel in Southeast Asia or Latin America.
- **Your full cost is bigger than the cash you spend.** You also give up six months of salary, plus roughly £430 to £875 of employer pension contributions. Interest you lose on the savings is minor, under £300.

## Main risks
1. **No job to come back to.** UK employees have no legal right to unpaid leave or a sabbatical. Your employer can say no. Resigning also resets your continuous employment, which affects redundancy pay and unfair dismissal protection.
2. **Coming home with no buffer.** If you spend all £12,000, one slow job search or one emergency at home leaves you overdrawn.
3. **Housing.** Subletting without your landlord's written consent can breach your tenancy. Paying rent on an empty home may eat 30% to 40% of your money.
4. **Working illegally.** Remote work while on a tourist entry usually breaks visa rules, even for a UK client. It can also raise tax residence questions. Working holiday visas are the legal route for Australia, New Zealand and Canada (usually up to age 35) and Japan (up to 30). Check that you qualify.
5. **Insurance gaps.** Policies often exclude pre-existing conditions and adventure sports. A hospital stay that isn't covered can cost five figures.
6. **National Insurance.** This is a small risk. If you earned above about £6,500 earlier in the tax year, you probably still get a qualifying year. Rules on paying voluntary contributions from abroad changed in April 2026. Check yours at gov.uk/check-national-insurance-record.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You have framed this as "£12,000 of savings". The real questions are whether you have a job to return to and what you will have left when you land. Neither is answered yet. Also, "six months of travel" is not a budget. Without a route and a daily spending cap, trips commonly run 15% to 25% over.

## Next steps
1. **By 30 Nov 2026:** ask HR for unpaid leave in writing. Ask for your role, salary, return date, and whether pension contributions continue. Do not resign before you have an answer.
2. **By 17 Nov 2026:** write down your monthly costs at home. Check whether your lease allows subletting or ending it early, then set a daily budget for your chosen countries with 20% added for overruns.
3. **By 15 Dec 2026:** make the go or no-go decision.
   - **With written leave:** go, and keep at least £3,000 back, more if your rent is high.
   - **Without it:** plan a trip of about three months, or keep saving.
