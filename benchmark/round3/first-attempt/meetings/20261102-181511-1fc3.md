## Agenda

I have a job offer from another company. Should I tell my manager to try for a counter-offer?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic: statement to the board**

The usual answer: use the offer as leverage, because the worst case is a "no" and you still have the new job.

Your question already leans towards asking. So I judge it as if you hoped to stay quiet.

I start by assuming the move fails. Here is how it most likely fails.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood × impact**

1. **Your standing drops even if they match. (Likely, medium to high impact.)** Your manager now knows you were job hunting. You can be marked as a flight risk. That can quietly cost you promotions, key projects and trust. If redundancies come within 12 to 24 months, you may be easier to cut. I cannot put a number on this. It is common enough that recruiters warn about it routinely.

2. **The counter fixes pay, not the reason you looked. (Likely, medium impact.)** A one-off rise rarely changes the manager, the workload or the career path. Many people who accept a counter leave within a year or two anyway. You will often see a figure of around 80%. I cannot find a solid source for it, so treat it as folklore, not fact.

3. **The rise is smaller than it looks. (Certain, low to medium impact.)** A £5,000 gross rise is about £3,600 net at basic rate (20% tax plus 8% NI). At higher rate it is about £2,900 net (40% plus 2%). Was it worth the risk in point 1?

4. **The new offer slips away. (Possible, high impact.)** Delays look like bargaining. The new employer can withdraw an offer that is still conditional, for example on references or checks. If you have already accepted it and then renege, that is technically a breach of contract. It is rarely pursued, but it burns the bridge and the recruiter.

5. **They say "congratulations".** **(Possible, high impact if you didn't really want to leave.)** Your bluff is called. You must go, on their timeline.

**What would have to be true for asking to work**

- You would genuinely take the new job if they refuse. If not, do not ask.
- Your only real problem is pay or title, not the manager or the culture.
- You are hard to replace, and they cannot hire your skills quickly.
- The new offer is unconditional, in writing, and has a clear deadline.

**The honest weak point in my case**

If all four conditions hold, a counter-offer can work, and my case for caution is only moderate. The cautious options are to take the new job cleanly, or to decline it and raise pay at your next review without mentioning it.

**What I do not know**

Why you started looking. Your notice period (often 1 to 3 months in the UK). Whether the new offer is conditional. The size of the gap in pay.

Bottom line: Do not use the offer as a bargaining chip unless you would happily walk and pay is your only complaint. Otherwise, either leave cleanly or stay quietly.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: Only ask for a counter if you would really stay. Otherwise, do not bluff.

I think you should ask, and I would go further than most advisers suggest. A live offer is the strongest leverage you will get this year. Managers can often approve a pay rise or a new title faster than a new hire can be onboarded, so the cost of saying yes to you is lower than it looks to them.

The bold version:

1. **Get the offer in writing first.** Check the salary, bonus, start date, and notice terms. A counter is only as good as the offer behind it.
2. **Decide your walk-away point before you talk.** Write down the lowest package you would accept to stay. If your manager can't reach it, you will know without guessing.
3. **Ask for a specific thing.** Do not say "I want more." Say "The offer is £X plus Y. Can you match that, or offer Z?" Specific numbers are easier to approve.
4. **Ask about things beyond pay.** Title, scope, flexible working, and bonus timing can sometimes move when pay can't.
5. **Tell your manager you want an answer by a set date.** Two weeks is common. Without a deadline, the request can drift.

Where the upside is limited:

- Counters often buy time rather than fix the reason you were looking. If the problem was workload, progression, or your boss, a pay rise will not solve it.
- Some managers will see the request as a signal you are halfway out. Your relationship may change even if you stay.
- If you accept a counter, you will need to decline the new offer cleanly. Reneging on an accepted offer can damage your reputation in a small market.
- I have no verified figures on how many people who accept counters stay long term. Scout should check that before you rely on any number.

What I am unsure of: your current pay, the size of the gap, your tenure, your notice period, and whether you actually want to stay. Those answers change the advice a lot.

Bottom line: Yes, ask, but only with a written offer, a specific number, a deadline, and a clear walk-away point. If you would be relieved to leave, use the offer as your exit, not as a bargaining chip.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Operator statement**

I don't know your salary, the offer, your notice period or whether you want to stay. Those four facts decide this. Without them, here is the plan.

**Do not ask for a counter-offer as a bargaining chip.** Only raise the offer if you would take the new job over your current one at equal pay. If not, you are bluffing, and a manager who calls it leaves you stuck.

**Steps, in order**
1. Day 0, 30 minutes: write down what you actually want. Pay figure, title, hours, remote days, training. Rank them.
2. Day 1, 1 hour: get the new offer in writing. Check salary, bonus, pension, start date, probation length and notice period.
3. Day 1 to 2: ask the new company for 5 to 7 working days to reply. Most will give it.
4. Day 3, 30 minutes: meet your manager. Say: "I have a written offer I'm seriously considering. I'd rather stay if we can close the gap. Here is what would do it."
5. Day 3 to 8: give them 3 to 5 working days. Ask for the answer in writing.
6. Day 8: decide. If the counter is vague or "after the next review", resign.

**Cost**
- Upfront: £0.
- Running: your notice period. Typically 1 to 3 months. Check your contract.

**Illustrative numbers (assumed, not yours).** Current £50,000, offer £58,000, so the gap is £8,000 a year, about £500 a month take-home.
- 3 months: if you stay on a £55,000 counter, you are £3,000 a year behind the offer but have no move risk. If you leave, you are in probation, usually 3 to 6 months.
- 6 months: the counter should be paid by now. If the rise is not in your payslip by month 2, treat it as a broken promise.
- 12 months: the new job is worth about £8,000 more, plus whatever the next rise adds. A counter-offer only wins if the non-pay gains are real.

**Flags**
- Commonly quoted claims say most people who accept counters leave within 12 months. I am unsure of the source quality. Scout should check.
- Your manager may agree now and quietly treat you as a flight risk. I cannot measure that.
- If you have 2 or more years of service, leaving resets your unfair dismissal protection. UK qualifying rules are changing, so check the current period.
- If the counter needs approval above your manager, add 1 to 2 weeks. That may breach the offer deadline.

Bottom line: Ask for a counter-offer only if you would genuinely take the new job, do it within a week of having the offer in writing, set a 5 working day deadline, and resign if you do not get a written, dated answer.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

I can't tell from your question whether you want to stay or go. I've judged this as if you hoped for the opposite of whatever you lean towards.

**One point about the question.** You asked whether to tell your manager so they will counter. That is a different act from resigning and then being offered a counter. Hinting that you hold an offer to get a raise is a bluff or a negotiation. It can work, but it can also mark you as a flight risk. I found no good data on that specific tactic.

**What the evidence says about counter-offers**
- Robert Half's UK survey of 2,000 employees (Opinium, December 2021) reported that 34% of those who accepted a counter-offer left within six months. 74% left within a year. See [The HR Director](https://www.thehrdirector.com/business-news/staff-retention/half-of-counteroffers-accepted-as-hiring-crisis-continues/) and [Robert Half UK](https://www.roberthalf.com/gb/en/about/press/half-counteroffers-successful-hiring-crisis-continues). I did not read the original report, so check the 74% figure before you rely on it.
- Other countries show lower numbers. [Australia](https://ontarget.cmaaustralia.edu.au/bosses-beware-counteroffers-dont-achieve-retention/): 52% left within a year. [Japan (Robert Walters)](https://www.ccifj.or.jp/actus/n/news/robert-walters-why-counter-offers-dont-work.html): 47% within a year.
- These come from recruiters, who earn money when you move jobs. They are surveys, not peer-reviewed studies.
- A contrary result: [one vendor survey](https://rossclennett.com/2023/08/the-evidence-is-clear-more-employers-are-using-counter-offers-because-they-work/) found 94% of acceptors said their relationship with the employer was the same or better. It is a weak source too.

**Common claims that are false or shaky**
- "9 in 10 who accept a counter-offer leave within a year." I found this only on unsourced blogs. Don't repeat it.
- "Counter-offers never work." Not true. Many people stay. The data is mixed, and no source I found tracks real people over time.
- "Counter-offers are rare." Also false. The [CIPD Labour Market Outlook](https://www.cipd.org/en/about/news/employers-turning-to-counteroffers-reports-cipd-labour-market-outlook/) found 40% of UK employers made one in the past 12 months. That is employer behaviour, not proof that they work.

**What this means for you**
- The usual reason people leave after accepting is that the cause of their unhappiness (role, boss, progress) stays the same. A pay rise fixes only pay.
- If pay is your only issue, a counter-offer is more likely to hold.
- Whatever you do, get any counter-offer in writing, with the new salary and title.

**Questions only you can answer**
1. Would you take the new job at its current terms? If not, you are shopping for leverage, not leaving.
2. Is the new employer's offer firm and in writing?
3. Would you be happy to stay if your employer matched the pay?

**What I'm unsure of:** I have no figures on how managers react to being told in advance. I also don't know your industry or the size of the gap between the two offers.

Bottom line: The evidence does not support relying on a counter-offer to fix a deeper problem. Ask for a raise only if you would be content to stay at the matched pay, and be ready to take the new job if they refuse.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Yes, you can tell your manager, but only if two things are true. First, you would really take the new job if they say no. Second, pay or title is your main complaint. If either is false, don't use the offer as a bargaining chip. Take the new job cleanly, or turn it down quietly and ask for a raise at your next review.

## Why
- **Your manager can say "congratulations".** Once you tell them, you can't take it back. If you wouldn't actually leave, you are bluffing and you have no fallback.
- **The evidence on counter-offers is weak and mixed.** A Robert Half UK survey from December 2021 reported that 74% of people who accepted a counter-offer left within a year. Nobody has checked that figure, it comes from a recruiter that profits when people move, and the 2021 job market was unusually tight. Surveys from Australia (52%) and Japan (47%) show lower figures. The pattern behind all of them is the same: a pay rise only fixes pay.
- **Counter-offers are normal in the UK.** The CIPD found 40% of employers made one in the last 12 months. So asking for one is not reckless in itself.
- **The rise is worth less than it looks.** An £8,000 gap on a £50,000 salary is about £4,640 a year after tax and National Insurance. That is roughly £390 a month, not £500. A student loan or pension changes would reduce it further.

## Main risks
- **The new offer runs out while you wait.** Many employers expect an answer within 5 to 10 working days. A pay rise that needs HR or finance sign-off can take 1 to 4 weeks.
- **You stay with a vague promise.** The counter is verbal or "after the next review", the new offer has lapsed, and you have little extra pay and a flight-risk label. This is the most likely way it goes wrong.
- **You get quietly marked as a flight risk.** This is plausible but no one has measured it. Nobody knows how big the effect is.
- **Legal risk.** If you accept the new offer and then back out, that may breach the contract, depending on its terms. Employers rarely enforce it, but it can damage your reputation. If you move, your unfair dismissal protection starts again. Today you need 2 years' service for it. Under the Employment Rights Act 2025 this is expected to drop to 6 months from January 2027. Check the current position with Acas.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You asked how to get a counter-offer, not whether you want to leave. That suggests you may be shopping for leverage. If you started looking because of your manager, your workload or your career path, a pay match won't fix it. The evidence suggests you would probably be looking again within a year.

## Next steps
1. **Today:** Get the new offer in writing. Check the salary, pension, bonus, conditions (references and checks), start date and the deadline to reply. Write down the lowest package that would make you stay, and ask yourself honestly whether you would leave if they refused.
2. **Within 3 working days:** If you pass that test, have one meeting with your manager. Give a specific figure ("£X, or £Y plus this title") and ask for a written answer by a fixed date that falls before the new offer expires.
3. **On that date:** If you have a written, dated counter that meets your figure, accept it and turn down the new job politely. If not, sign the new offer and resign. If the rise isn't on your payslip within 2 months, treat the promise as broken.
