## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

# Skeptic: Free returns for an online clothing shop

The usual answer: yes, free returns lift conversion and big retailers do it, so you should too.

The owner gave no hint of a preferred answer, so no bias correction is needed. I argue for the cautious end: do not offer blanket free returns now, and possibly never.

**The trend has turned against free returns.** Large UK fashion retailers that could afford free returns started charging for them:

- Zara added a £1.95 return fee in 2022.
- Boohoo added £1.99 in 2022.
- ASOS began charging on lower-value orders in 2023.

Scout should verify those figures. The direction is clear, though. Firms with far more scale than you found free returns unprofitable.

**You already owe customers a lot by law.** Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, UK customers can cancel within 14 days of delivery and get a refund, including standard outbound delivery. They pay return postage only if you told them so beforehand. Free returns go beyond the law. They are a cost you choose to carry, not a legal duty.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Return rate rises and eats margin. Likely, high impact.** Online clothing return rates are often cited at 20% to 30%, and higher for dresses and fit-sensitive items. I am unsure of your current rate. Here is an illustration only:
   - Average order: £45.
   - Return rate rises from 15% to 25%.
   - Cost per return: about £4 postage plus £2 handling.
   - The extra returns cost about £0.60 per order sold, before any lost stock.
   - On a 10% net margin (£4.50), that is roughly 13% of profit gone.
2. **Bracketing and wardrobing. Likely, medium to high impact.** Customers order three sizes and keep one, or wear an item once and send it back. Free returns invite this.
3. **Unsellable returns. Moderate likelihood, medium impact.** Items come back creased, perfumed, tag-less or out of season. Each written-off item loses its full cost price.
4. **Hard to reverse. Moderate likelihood, medium impact.** Withdrawing free returns later draws complaints and bad reviews. Big brands absorbed the backlash. A small shop may not.
5. **Cash flow and admin time. Moderate likelihood, medium impact.** Refunds go out fast while resale is slow. Processing returns takes your own hours.
6. **Competitive gain smaller than hoped. Uncertain likelihood.** The conversion uplift is real in studies but varies widely. I cannot say it will cover the costs above.

**Most likely way this dies:** returns climb, margin quietly erodes over 3 to 6 months, and you notice only at year-end accounts.

**What would have to be true for it to work**

- You know your current return rate and cost per return.
- Your gross margin can absorb at least a 10 percentage point rise in returns.
- Conversion or order value rises enough to cover that. Operator should model the break-even.
- Returned stock is mostly resellable at full price.
- You can cap abuse, for example by flagging serial returners or offering free exchanges only.

If you want to test it, run a time-limited trial on one product line, with a free-exchange-only option as the fallback.

Bottom line: Do not launch blanket free returns until you know your return rate and break-even. At most, trial free exchanges or a minimum-spend threshold for 8 to 12 weeks.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: no, free returns cost too much for a small shop, so keep charging.

I want to argue for the bold end: make free returns a core promise and fund it on purpose.

**Why this could work**
- Fear of a bad fit stops many online clothes buyers from ordering. Free returns removes that reason.
- It can raise order value. Shoppers often order two sizes to try at home, and free returns makes that feel normal.
- A small shop can stand out. "Free returns" is a short message people remember, and it costs you nothing to say it up front.
- Return reasons are useful data. If many returns say "too small," your size guide or product pages need work.

**The unusual move**
Do not offer it on everything at once. Try free returns on exchanges for a different size, or on orders above a set basket value. Pay for it with a small price change or a minimum order. Start with one product line.

**Where the upside is limited**
- Cost per return is the main risk. It includes postage both ways, handling, repacking, and any stock you cannot resell. Fashion return rates can be high. I do not know yours.
- Your margin decides everything. If margins are thin, one extra return can wipe out the profit from a sale.
- Larger retailers can match your offer more cheaply because of their scale.
- Free returns does not fix poor sizing or weak photos. It can hide those problems.

**A legal point**
Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, online buyers have 14 days to cancel most orders. If you do not clearly tell them they must pay return postage, you have to pay it. So you may already carry part of this cost.

**What I am unsure of**
I do not know your current return rate, gross margin, average order value, or typical return cost. Give me those four numbers and I can work out your break-even point.

Bottom line: test free returns on one range for about 60 days, with a written break-even figure set in advance, before switching it on for the whole shop.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

I can't tell which answer you hope for. I've judged this as if you wanted the opposite of whatever you lean towards.

**What the evidence shows**

- **Free returns are no longer the norm in UK fashion.** Retail Week says about three-quarters of major UK fashion retailers charge for returns. Sendcloud's Top 100 analysis puts it at 79% ([IADS summary](https://www.iads.org/member/news/majority-of-uk-fashion-retailers-now-charging-for-returns), [Retail Gazette](https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2026/06/returns-reform-how-retailers-are-rewriting-the-rules-without-losing-customers/)). A claim that "everyone offers free returns" is out of date.
- **Big names charge.** H&M charges £2.95. Next charges £2.50 on Evri returns. Zara deducts £1.95 ([Retail Gazette on H&M](https://www.retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2025/01/hm-return-fees/)).
- **Clothing returns a lot.** About 23.6% of online clothing orders come back. Other sources say 25% to 40% ([ZigZag benchmark](https://www.retaileconomics.co.uk/retail-insights/thought-leadership-reports/zigzag-uk-returns-benchmark-2025), [Retail Gazette](https://retailgazette.co.uk/blog/2026/02/returns-crippling-supply-chain/)). Free returns tend to push that rate up.
- **Each return is costly.** Estimates run from £10 to £25 per parcel in reverse logistics, before the refund. The figures are mostly from vendor blogs and don't reconcile ([search summary of sources](https://eightx.co/blog/uk-ecommerce-return-rate-benchmark)).
- **Fees seem to lower returns.** H&M says its fee reduced returns. Retail Economics links the forecast fall in the UK returns rate, from 21% to 19.5%, to fees and tighter policies.

**What is false or unproven**

- "Free returns always lift sales" is unproven for a small UK shop. I found no controlled UK study of conversion with free versus paid returns. The claims I found are vendor opinion.
- "You must offer free returns" is false. For a change of mind, the customer can be made to pay return postage. You must say so before they buy. You can't charge more than the direct cost. You must refund the original standard delivery charge within 14 days ([Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/consumer-rights/advice/i-want-to-return-something-bought-online), [legislation.gov.uk, reg. 35](https://legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2013/3134/regulation/35)).
- For faulty items, you always pay return postage.

**Rough sums for you to check**

- Take 100 orders at £40 average. About 25 come back.
- A £4 return label costs £100 a year per 100 orders if you cover all of them.
- The refunds and handling are extra. Resale of returned stock is also at risk.
- If free returns lift sales by 5%, that is £200 of extra revenue. At a 40% gross margin, that is £80 of margin. This would not cover the label cost.
- These are my illustrative figures, not market data. Use your real return rate and margin.

**Middle options with some support**

- Free exchanges, with a small fee for refunds. The Kolonell case study suggests a fee of about £3 plus free exchanges can hold conversion. It is anecdotal.
- Free returns above a basket threshold, or for loyal customers.
- A clear sizing guide to cut returns at the source.

**Uncertain**

- I don't know your margin, return rate or carrier price.
- I found no UK data on how a fee changes conversion for small shops.

Bottom line: The evidence doesn't support blanket free returns for a small shop. Most UK fashion rivals charge, and each return costs roughly £10 to £25. Try free exchanges with a small refund fee, or free returns above a spend threshold. Track conversion and return rate for 8 weeks before you commit.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Do not offer blanket free returns. Keep charging for refund returns, and from 4 January 2027 run an 8 to 12 week pilot of **free size exchanges**, with a break-even figure written down before you start. Do not launch anything before Christmas.

## Why
- **Most of your rivals charge.** About 75% to 79% of major UK fashion retailers charge for returns. H&M charges £2.95, Next £2.50 and Zara £1.95. Offering free returns would be a choice you pay for, not something customers expect.
- **The law does not require it.** Under the Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013, customers can cancel within 14 days of delivery and then have 14 more days to send the goods back. You must refund the original standard delivery charge. The customer pays return postage only if you told them so before they bought.
- **The sums are against it.** Online clothing return rates are about 24%, and other sources say 25% to 40%. Take an illustrative £45 order at a 40% gross margin (£18), a 25% return rate and £6 per return. Free returns then cost about £1.50 per order. You would need about 8% more orders to break even, or nearer 10% once you allow for the extra orders also coming back. No UK evidence shows a small shop reliably gets that lift.
- **Free exchanges work differently.** When a customer swaps a size, you keep the sale. It removes the fear of a bad fit without paying people to leave.

## Main risks
- **Legal:** if your terms do not clearly say the customer pays return postage, you are already paying it. Faulty items are always your cost. Be careful with any flat "returns fee" taken off refunds. The rules limit you to the direct cost of the return. The board has not checked how a flat fee fits with that, so check before you use one.
- **Margin:** returns rise, and profit falls quietly over 3 to 6 months. You may only see it in the year-end accounts.
- **Abuse:** customers ordering several sizes and keeping one, or wearing items once and sending them back.
- **Hard to undo:** once you advertise free returns, taking the offer away costs trust and reviews.
- **No clear answer:** at small volumes, an 8 week test cannot reliably tell a 3% to 5% sales lift from normal ups and downs.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You asked "should I?" without giving your return rate, margin or cost per return. Those three numbers decide the answer. If you don't already track them, that gap is a bigger problem than your returns policy.

## Next steps

3. **From 4 January to about 1 March 2027:** run free exchanges on one range. Stop if the return rate rises by more than 5 points or sales do not rise. Use the return reasons to fix your size guide.
