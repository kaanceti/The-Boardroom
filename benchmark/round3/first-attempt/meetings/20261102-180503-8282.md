# Boardroom minutes: My partner and I earn very different salaries. Should we split shared bills…

Date: 2026-11-02 18:05  
Mode: full

## Agenda

My partner and I earn very different salaries. Should we split shared bills 50/50 or in proportion to our incomes?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

# Skeptic: Splitting shared bills

**The usual answer:** split in proportion to income, because it is "fairer." I will argue the cautious end: do not change anything until you know what you are protecting, and pick the split that fails least badly.

The owner has not hinted at a preferred answer, so I have no bias to correct for.

## Illustration (my assumptions, not your figures)

Suppose you earn £60,000 and £30,000 gross, take home about £3,700 and £2,100 a month, and share £2,000 of bills.

- **50/50:** £1,000 each. That is 27% versus 48% of take-home pay.
- **Proportional (64/36):** £1,280 and £720. That is about 35% each.

## Risks, ranked by likelihood × impact

1. **The formula is not the real problem (high likelihood, medium impact).** Most splits fail on definitions, not ratios. Is a takeaway "shared"? A holiday? The car the lower earner needs for work? Childcare? Without a written list, either method breeds quiet resentment.

2. **Property and separation (medium likelihood, high impact).** If you are unmarried in England or Wales, there is no automatic financial claim on separation. Who owns the home depends on legal title and, in disputes, on evidence of intention and contributions (Stack v Dowden, Jones v Kernott).
   - Under a proportional split, the lower earner often pays bills while the higher earner pays the mortgage. That can leave the lower earner with little visible stake.
   - Scotland differs, so check where you live.
   - If married, courts look at needs and sharing anyway. Then the split matters less legally but still matters day to day.

3. **50/50 squeezes the lower earner (high likelihood if the gap is large, medium impact).** In my example they lose nearly half their take-home pay. That means no savings, a pension gap, and dependence when something breaks. This is the strongest case against 50/50.

4. **Proportional can breed resentment the other way (medium likelihood, low to medium impact).** The higher earner may push spending up to their own comfort level, which inflates the lower earner's 36% too. Ratios also go stale when one of you gets a raise, goes part-time or goes on parental leave.

5. **Joint liability (low likelihood, high impact).** Joint accounts, joint tenancies and council tax at a shared address can make each of you liable for the whole bill, whatever you agreed between you.

## What would have to be true for a change to work

- You have a written list of what counts as shared.
- If you own or plan to buy property, you have a declaration of trust or a cohabitation agreement. Expect roughly £500 to £1,500 in solicitor fees. I am unsure of current rates.
- You review the ratio yearly, or at any change in income.
- Both of you can still save something each month.

## Honest note

The case for doing nothing is weak if the current split leaves one of you unable to save. It is strong if things work and nobody is resentful.

**Bottom line:** Proportional is usually safer for the lower earner. But before you change anything, write down what counts as shared, and if a home is involved, get a declaration of trust first.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: split bills in proportion to take-home pay, because it feels fair and most couples land there.

I want to argue for the bolder move: pool all income into one joint account and pay every shared bill and savings goal from it. Each of you adds a fixed amount or a proportional share. Then decide together what the pot is for.

Why this has upside:

- **One view of the money.** Bills, savings and spending sit in one place, so you both see the same picture.
- **Faster saving.** A shared goal, such as a house deposit or a six-month emergency fund, is easier to reach when money is not split across two accounts.
- **No small debts.** Pooling ends the "you owe me £14" tally.
- **Fair to both.** You can still agree a proportional contribution, so the lower earner keeps more personal spending money.

A worked example with made-up numbers:

- Partner A takes home £3,000 a month. Partner B takes home £1,500.
- Shared bills total £2,400 a month.
- 50/50 means £1,200 each. B's share is 80% of their take-home pay.
- Proportional means A pays two thirds (£1,600) and B pays one third (£800). B keeps £400 more each month, or £4,800 a year.

Use take-home pay, not gross. Income tax and National Insurance take a larger share of the higher salary, so the true ratio is slightly different from the gross ratio.

Where the upside is limited:

- Pooling only works if you both trust each other and agree how the pot is spent. If one of you resents it, the gain turns into friction.
- Proportional splits need regular updating. If salaries change, the split should change too.
- I do not know your incomes, debts, mortgage, children, or how much you save now. Each of these could change the answer.
- If you earn similar amounts, a plain 50/50 split may be right for simplicity.

What I am unsure of: whether the salary gap is stable, and whether either of you would welcome a shared pot.

Bottom line: Proportional contributions into a joint account, with a shared savings goal, offer the most upside. Keep 50/50 only if your incomes are close or you both prefer simplicity.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Operator statement**

**My view:** Split in proportion to take-home pay, unless the gap is small. It is fairer and it costs nothing. The only hard part is doing it on a fixed date, not "sometime".

**Worked example.** I do not know your real figures, so I used these. Replace them.
- Partner A takes home £4,000 a month. Partner B takes home £2,000.
- Shared bills (rent or mortgage, council tax, energy, broadband, food) are £2,400 a month.

| Rule | A pays | B pays | A has left | B has left |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50/50 | £1,200 | £1,200 | £2,800 | £800 |
| Proportional (67/33) | £1,600 | £800 | £2,400 | £1,200 |

Under 50/50, B keeps 40% of their pay and A keeps 70%. Under proportional, both keep 60%. That is the whole case.

**Steps, in order**
1. **This week, 30 minutes:** each of you writes down monthly take-home pay. Use net pay, not gross. Tax bands and student loan repayments distort gross figures.
2. **This week, 1 hour:** list every shared bill with its amount. Agree what counts as shared. Holidays and gifts are the usual fight.
3. **By Sunday 8 November, 15 minutes:** work out each person's percentage. Divide own take-home by combined take-home.
4. **By Sunday 15 November, 1 hour:** set up standing orders into a joint account, timed for the day after you are both paid. Cost: £0. Most UK banks do not charge for a joint current account.
5. **Reviews, 20 minutes each:** 2 February 2027 (3 months), 2 May 2027 (6 months), 2 November 2027 (12 months). Also review whenever either of you gets a pay change. April is the likely date, because of tax year changes.

**Running cost:** £0. Time is about 3 hours to start and 1 hour a year.

**What it means in the example**
- 3 months: B has kept £1,200 more than under 50/50.
- 6 months: £2,400 more.
- 12 months: £4,800 more. A pays that extra.

**What does not fit**
- If you skip the review dates, the percentages go stale after the first pay rise. That is the usual failure.
- If A resents paying £400 more a month, proportional will not last. Say so now.

**Things I am unsure of**
- Your real incomes and bills.
- Whether either of you has children, debts or a much larger savings pot. These can change what is fair.
- Whether you are married. Unmarried couples in the UK have no "common-law" protection. If one of you pays more towards a home you buy, put the shares in writing, for example in a declaration of trust. A solicitor typically charges a few hundred pounds. I have not checked current fees.

**A middle option:** if the gap is under about 15%, 50/50 is simpler and the difference is small. Above that, use proportional.

Bottom line: Split shared bills in proportion to take-home pay, set it up by 15 November 2026, and review on 2 February, 2 May and 2 November 2027.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

The owner has not hinted at a preferred answer, so I have judged this neutrally.

**What the evidence says**

I found UK survey figures through search. I saw summaries only, not the full reports, so treat the numbers as approximate.

- 50/50 is the most common split, but not by a wide margin. YouGov reports 46% of cohabiting working couples split 50/50 and 38% split by income. Where one partner earns notably more, 49% split by income. ([YouGov](https://yougov.com/en-gb/articles/50381-what-is-the-fairest-way-for-couples-to-split-household-bills))
- Other surveys give different figures. One reports 51% split 50/50 and 40% split by income. Another reports 57% split 50/50 and 23% split by income. The differences come from sample size and question wording. ([Daily Brit](https://thedailybrit.co.uk/?p=54920), [Confused.com](https://www.confused.com/press/releases/2026/almost-half-of-brits-admit-lying-to-their-partner-about-money))
- Higher earners tend to favour income-based splits more than lower earners do. One summary says 27% of lower earners favour them. I could not verify which survey that figure comes from.

**Claims that are false or shaky**

- "Everyone does 50/50." Not true. Roughly four in ten couples split by income.
- "Proportional is the fair default." Also not proven. These surveys measure what couples do or prefer. They do not show which method leads to better relationships. I found no strong evidence either way.

**Worked example (illustrative figures, not yours)**

Take-home pay is £3,900 a month for Partner A and £2,200 for Partner B. That is £6,100 combined. Shared bills are £2,000 a month.

- 50/50: each pays £1,000. That is 26% of A's take-home but 45% of B's.
- Proportional: A pays £1,279 (64%) and B pays £721 (36%). Each then pays about 33% of their take-home.

Under 50/50, B has £1,200 left and A has £2,900. The gap in spare money grows.

**Practical points**

- Use take-home pay, not gross. Pension contributions, student loan repayments and tax can distort gross figures.
- Agree what counts as shared. Rent or mortgage, council tax, energy, food and broadband are usual. Holidays and hobbies are often separate.
- Review the split when pay changes, or once a year.
- If you have children or one partner cuts their hours, a pure income split may still be unfair. I have not researched that case.

**What I am unsure of**

- I do not know your incomes, debts or whether you are married. Those change the answer.
- I have not checked the legal position on joint mortgages or tenancies. Each person is usually liable for the whole debt, whatever split you agree between you.

Bottom line: The evidence does not prove either method is better. Where incomes differ a lot, splitting by take-home pay leaves both partners with a similar share of spare money, and 50/50 does not. Pick the method you both think is fair, and review it yearly.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision

Split shared bills in proportion to your take-home pay, not 50/50. Pay both shares into a joint bills account by standing order, and keep the rest of your money separate. Before you start, write down which costs count as shared. If you are unmarried and own or are buying a home, put your ownership shares in writing.

## Why

- **50/50 hits the lower earner hardest when pay differs a lot.** Example figures, not yours: you take home £4,000 and £2,000 a month and share £2,400 of bills. Under 50/50, one of you keeps 70% of their pay and the other keeps 40%. Under a proportional split, you each keep 60%. That moves £400 a month, or £4,800 a year, to the lower earner.
- **The cost is close to zero.** It takes a few hours to set up and about an hour a year to review.
- **The evidence does not settle the fairness question.** UK surveys show 46% to 57% of couples split 50/50 and 23% to 40% split by income. Those surveys show what couples do, not what works better. So the decision rests on the arithmetic above, plus whether you both accept it.

## Main risks

- **The home, if you are unmarried.** "Common-law marriage" does not exist in England and Wales. Who owns the home depends on legal title and on evidence of what you intended. A common pattern is that the lower earner pays the bills while the higher earner pays the mortgage, and the lower earner ends up with no visible stake. Scotland gives limited claims under the Family Law (Scotland) Act 2006, so check where you live.
- **Joint liability.** On a joint tenancy, joint mortgage or joint account, each of you can be pursued for the whole amount, whatever you agreed between you. A joint account also links your credit files.
- **Arguments over what counts as shared.** Holidays, takeaways, a car one of you needs for work. Most disputes start here, not over the ratio.
- **Out-of-date ratios.** After a pay rise, a move to part-time work or parental leave, the split stops being fair.
- **Resentment.** The higher earner may feel used, or may push spending up, which raises the lower earner's share too. Say so now if either of you feels this.

## Honest criticism for the owner

You have framed this as a choice of formula. That is the smaller question. The bigger money risks are an unclear list of shared costs and, if you own property, unwritten ownership shares. You also mention salaries. Use take-home pay instead, and add back any salary-sacrifice pension contributions, or the ratio will be skewed.
