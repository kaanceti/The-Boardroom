# Boardroom minutes: Someone has offered me £25,000 for my small website, which earns about £800 a…

Date: 2026-11-02 18:08  
Mode: full

## Agenda

Someone has offered me £25,000 for my small website, which earns about £800 a month. Should I sell it?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: take it. £25,000 is about 31 times monthly earnings, which sits inside the normal range for small sites.

My position is the cautious end. Do not accept this offer yet, and do not treat "sell" as the safe choice by default. An unsolicited offer is where most of the risk sits. You have not hinted at a preference, so I judge both paths equally.

**Ranked risks (likelihood, impact)**

1. **The buyer is not genuine (medium likelihood, high impact).** Unsolicited website offers are a known scam route. Typical patterns are asking for logins or domain transfer before cleared funds, "verification fees," cheques that bounce, or overpayment refunds. If the domain moves before money clears, you lose the site and the income. Only proceed through escrow, such as Escrow.com, and transfer nothing until funds are held.

2. **The price is below value (medium likelihood, medium impact).** Brokers like Empire Flippers and Flippa have often quoted 25 to 45 times monthly *net profit*. I am unsure how far those multiples have fallen since Google's 2024 updates and AI Overviews. A buyer who approaches you first usually expects a discount. You have no competing bid to test against.

3. **£800 may be revenue, not profit (unknown likelihood, high impact).** If hosting, tools and content cost £150 a month, real profit is £650. Then £25,000 is about 38 times, a strong price. This one fact changes the answer.

4. **Tax eats more than expected (high likelihood, medium impact).** As an individual, the gain is likely subject to Capital Gains Tax at 18% or 24% after the £3,000 allowance. Business Asset Disposal Relief, now 18%, may or may not apply. If the site sits in a limited company, the rules differ. Rough worst case: around £5,300 in tax on a £25,000 gain. Get an accountant's view before signing.

5. **Keeping it fails (likelihood depends on traffic source, high impact).** This is the honest weakness in my cautious case. If most traffic comes from Google search, decline is a real risk. At steady income, keeping beats selling after 31 months. At 30% decline a year, three years earns only about £21,000 before tax. Then doing nothing loses.

**Most likely way this goes wrong:** you rush a private deal, skip escrow, or accept a lowball because the offer felt flattering.

**What must be true for selling to be right**
- The buyer pays through escrow and passes basic checks.
- £800 is close to net profit, or the price still holds after costs.
- Traffic is search-dependent or trending down over the last 12 months.
- After tax, you have a better use for roughly £20,000 to £22,000.

**What must be true for keeping to be right**
- Traffic is stable or growing, from diverse sources like email or direct visits.
- The site takes little of your time.

Bottom line: Do not accept yet. Check profit, the 12-month traffic trend and the tax position, and get one comparison valuation. If you then sell, use escrow only. If traffic is steady and not search-dependent, keeping it is a defensible choice.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: take the £25,000 and move on.

I would not accept yet. Here is the bold case.

**What the numbers say**
- £800 a month is £9,600 a year. That is revenue, not profit. I do not know your costs.
- £25,000 is about 2.6 times annual revenue. For a small site, that is a reasonable price. It is not obviously a bargain.

**Why it may be worth more to you**
- Someone is willing to pay this. Ask them what they plan to do with the site. Their answer shows how much it is worth to them, and what they expect it to earn.
- Small sites often grow when someone gives them steady time and attention. I cannot confirm that this site would grow.
- You can ask for more. Counter at a higher figure, or propose a split: part now and part as an earn-out based on income over the next 12 months. An earn-out moves the risk to the buyer if they believe in the numbers.

**Where the upside is limited**
- If most of the £800 comes from one traffic source or one ad network, the income may not last. If the buyer cannot explain why it earns, treat that as a warning.
- Tax: sale proceeds may be subject to UK Capital Gains Tax. Business Asset Disposal Relief may apply. I believe its rate rose to 18% from April 2026, but please check this. The annual exempt amount is £3,000. Ask an accountant before you agree.

**What I am unsure of**
- Your profit margin.
- How stable the traffic and income are.
- Whether the offer includes the domain, content, email list, and accounts.

**Next steps**
1. Gather 12 months of income and cost records.
2. Ask the buyer why they want it and what they plan to change.
3. Ask for a counter-offer or an earn-out structure.

Bottom line: Do not sell at £25,000 until you know the profit and have pushed for more. The bold case is a higher price or an earn-out, not a quick yes.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Don't accept £25,000 yet, and don't turn it down either. If £800 a month is close to your real profit and the buyer checks out, sell. Counter once at about £28,000, but accept £25,000 rather than lose a genuine cash buyer over a few thousand pounds. Only sell through escrow.

## Why
- **The price is fair but not generous.** £25,000 is 31 times £800 a month, or 2.6 years of income. Small sites usually sell for very roughly 25 to 35 times monthly net profit. Those figures are secondhand and not audited.
- **The answer depends on whether £800 is profit or revenue.** If your costs are £150 a month, your profit is £650 and the offer is about 38 times profit, which is a strong price. If £800 is already profit, the offer is mid-range.
- **Keeping the site only wins if income holds.** After income tax at 20%, £800 a month is about £7,680 a year. A sale leaves you about £19,700 to £21,000 after Capital Gains Tax. So keeping only comes out ahead after about 2.6 years, and only if income stays flat. If income falls 30% a year, three years earns about £21,000 before tax, and selling wins.
- **Pushing hard has a cost.** An extra £2,000 is worth about £1,500 after tax. Losing the buyer leaves you holding all the traffic risk.

## Main risks
- **Fraud.** Never hand over logins or transfer the domain before the money is held in escrow, for example on Escrow.com. Refuse any "verification fees", cheques or overpayment refunds.
- **Tax.** Capital Gains Tax is 18% or 24% on the gain above the £3,000 allowance. With a cost base near zero, that is about £3,960 to £5,280. Business Asset Disposal Relief is now 18%, so it only helps if you pay higher-rate tax. It also needs a trading business you have owned for 2 years, and a site earning passive ad or affiliate income may not count. If the site is owned by a limited company, different rules apply.
- **Traffic dependence.** If most visitors come from Google or most income from one affiliate programme, one algorithm change could cut the site's value.
- **Due diligence as a fishing trip.** A competitor posing as a buyer could learn your traffic sources and affiliate deals. Share details only after you have checked the buyer and both of you have signed a non-disclosure agreement.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You quoted "earns about £800" without saying whether that is before or after costs. That one gap decides whether this offer is strong or ordinary. If you don't have 12 months of clean income and cost records, any buyer will cut the price or walk away.
