## Agenda

I'm 29 with £8,000 on a credit card at 24% interest and £3,000 in savings. Should I use my savings to pay off part of the card?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic: statement to the board**

The usual answer: yes, throw all £3,000 at the card, because 24% beats any savings rate.

You have not hinted at the answer you want, so I am judging this straight.

I will be honest first. **The case for doing nothing is weak.** At 24% APR, £3,000 of card debt costs roughly £720 a year. The same £3,000 in a good easy-access account earns about £120 to £135 at 4 to 4.5%. Keeping the savings loses you around £600 a year. I cannot argue that away. My job is to find how paying it off goes wrong, and to argue for the most cautious version of "pay it."

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **An emergency lands on the card at 24%.** This is likely and has medium impact. With £0 in savings, a £600 car repair or boiler fix goes straight back on the card. You are then back where you started, with no cushion. This is the most likely way the plan fails.
2. **You re-spend the freed credit.** Likelihood depends on you, and I cannot judge it. The impact is high. Paying £3,000 opens £3,000 of headroom. If the debt came from spending habits rather than a one-off shock, the balance can drift back up within months. Then you have £8,000 owed and no savings.
3. **The issuer cuts or freezes your limit.** This is low likelihood but high impact. You might be relying on the card as your "emergency fund." Lenders can reduce limits at short notice, often when you need them most, such as after a job loss.
4. **You miss a cheaper route.** This is medium likelihood with modest impact. A 0% balance transfer, typically with a fee of about 3%, could cut interest on the remaining £5,000 to £8,000 far more than the £3,000 payment does. I do not know your credit score, so I cannot say if you would be accepted.
5. **Income shock.** I do not know how secure your job is. If it is precarious, cash in hand matters more than the maths suggests.

**The cautious option I would back**

- Pay about £2,000 now.
- Keep about £1,000, or one month of essential costs if that is higher, in instant access.
- Before paying, check eligibility for a 0% balance transfer using a soft-search tool such as MoneySavingExpert's eligibility checker or Experian.
- Stop using the card for new spending.

Keeping £1,000 back costs you roughly £200 a year more than paying the full £3,000. That is a cheap insurance premium against Risk 1.

**What would have to be true for the full £3,000 payment to work**

- Your income is stable.
- You have no foreseeable large costs in the next 6 months.
- The debt came from a one-off event, not ongoing overspending.
- You will rebuild savings quickly from income.

If all four are true, pay the full £3,000. If you are unsure about any of them, keep a buffer.

Bottom line: Pay down the card, but keep roughly £1,000 back as a buffer and check for a 0% balance transfer first. Emptying your savings completely is the riskier version of a good idea.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: yes, use some savings to cut the card, but keep a cushion.

My case goes further. Clear as much of the card as you can, then look for a cheaper way to deal with what remains.

The upside is simple. Your card charges 24% a year. On £3,000, that avoids about £720 a year in interest. Savings usually earn much less. If your easy-access account pays around 4%, you keep roughly £600 a year more. I don't know your savings rate, so check it. Paying down debt at 24% is a guaranteed return that few investments can match.

Cutting the balance also helps. £5,000 at 24% costs about £100 a month in interest, compared with about £160 a month on £8,000. Lower card balances relative to your credit limit can also help your credit score over time.

The unusual move is a 0% balance transfer card. Some UK lenders offer 0% on transferred balances for a set period, often with a fee of about 3% of the amount moved. On £5,000, a 3% fee is £150. If you can clear the balance inside the promotional period, the saving could be much larger than that fee. Check current offers, because approval depends on your credit file.

Where the upside is limited:
- It disappears if you need that cash later. Many people keep three months of essential spending in reserve. I don't know your outgoings, so I can't give you that figure.
- A transfer only helps if you stop adding new card spending. Otherwise you end up with more debt.
- I don't know your income, job security, or whether this £3,000 is all your cash.
- Rates and deal terms change often. Verify every figure above before acting.

Bottom line: Pay down a large part of the £8,000 now, keep enough cash for emergencies, and look at a 0% transfer for the rest. The gain is real but modest, roughly £600 a year. It only works if the card spending stops.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Yes. Pay part of the card off with your savings, and keep about £1,000 in instant access. Keep £1,500 instead if your job is insecure or someone depends on you. First spend one week checking whether you can get a 0% balance transfer card. If you can move most of the balance to 0%, the case for emptying your savings gets much weaker.

## Why
- Your card costs about £145 to £160 a month in interest on £8,000. £3,000 in a good easy-access account earns about £90 to £140 a year. Keeping the cash instead of paying the card loses you roughly £600 a year.
- Clearing a 24% debt is a better return than any savings account, as long as you don't borrow on the card again.
- Keeping £1,000 back costs only about £200 a year. That is cheap protection against a boiler or car bill going straight back on the card at 24%, or into an overdraft at around 35% to 40%.
- A 0% balance transfer usually charges a fee of about 3%, which is £180 on £6,000. That fee is far smaller than the roughly £1,200 of interest you would pay clearing £6,000 at 24% with £400 a month. I could not confirm current deal lengths or fees for November 2026.
- Waiting about 2 weeks to check transfer options costs you about £30 in extra interest. That is worth paying to get the order right.

## Main risks
- **You spend on the card again.** Paying £2,000 off gives you £2,000 to spend again. A transfer also leaves your old card empty. If it fills up again, your debt doubles. This is the likeliest way the plan fails.
- **An emergency drains your buffer.** £1,000 is thin. The cost then lands back on the card at 24%.
- **The transfer has conditions.** Missing one payment can end the 0% rate. The rate usually goes back above 20% when the deal ends. Most deals only cover transfers made within 60 to 90 days of opening the card. The real application is a hard search on your credit file, and a soft-search result is not a guarantee.
- **You might not be accepted, or get a lower limit.** Then you save less, but paying down the card still helps.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You haven't said how the £8,000 built up or what you have left each month after bills. Those two facts matter more than anything else here. If the card covers a regular monthly shortfall, this plan only buys time and the debt will come back. You need to fix the gap between what comes in and what goes out, or no payoff date will hold.

## Next steps
1. **By 9 November 2026:** Write down your exact balance, whether the 24% is the APR, your minimum payment and your real monthly surplus. Then run soft-search eligibility checks for 0% balance transfer cards on MoneySavingExpert or Experian.
2. **By 16 November 2026:** Pay £2,000 from savings onto the card and keep £1,000. If you're accepted for a transfer, apply for it and move the rest within the deal's time window. Keep paying at least the minimum until the transfer goes through.
3. **Once the transfer is done:** Stop using the old card and ask the provider to lower its limit. Set up a fixed direct debit that clears the balance before the 0% period ends. For example, £6,180 over 16 months needs about £390 a month.
