# Boardroom minutes: My dog-walking business has a waiting list. Should I hire my first employee or…

Date: 2026-11-02 18:18  
Mode: full

## Agenda

My dog-walking business has a waiting list. Should I hire my first employee or raise my prices?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**The usual answer:** you have a waiting list, so hire someone and grow. I argue the cautious end: raise prices first and hire no one yet. You have not signalled a preference, so I am judging both options on their merits.

**Risks of hiring, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Paid hours with no work (high likelihood, high impact).** Dog walking demand bunches around 10am to 3pm on weekdays. An employee needs paid hours, and you pay for gaps. Travel between clients counts as working time for minimum wage purposes. The National Living Wage is £12.71 an hour from April 2026. Twenty-five hours a week costs about £16,500 a year in wages alone.

2. **Hidden employer costs (certain, medium impact).** These add perhaps £2,000 to £3,500 a year:
   - Employer National Insurance at 15% above £5,000, possibly offset by Employment Allowance. Check your eligibility with an accountant.
   - Auto-enrolment pension at 3% on qualifying earnings.
   - Employer's liability insurance, which is compulsory. Fines run up to £2,500 per day without it.
   - Payroll software and holiday pay.

3. **The waiting list is softer than it looks (medium likelihood, high impact).** People on a list are not paying clients. Some will have found another walker by the time you call them. I do not know your list's age or how many would convert. That is the single biggest unknown.

4. **A lost or injured dog under someone else's care (low likelihood, severe impact).** Your reputation is the business. One escaped dog posted on a local Facebook group can cost more clients than a price rise ever would.

5. **The employee takes your clients (medium likelihood, medium impact).** Non-compete clauses are hard to enforce in the UK. Walkers often leave and take the dogs they bonded with.

6. **Calling them "self-employed" to dodge costs.** If you control their hours and routes, HMRC and a tribunal may treat them as an employee. Do not do this.

7. **Local limits on dog numbers.** Some councils use Public Space Protection Orders to cap dogs per walker, often at 4 to 6. The Royal Parks require a licence and cap walkers at 4 dogs. These caps limit how much one extra walker can earn you. Check your area.

**Risks of raising prices**

These are smaller and more reversible. You may lose some existing clients. But the waiting list can backfill them, and a modest rise of 10 to 15% is normal. If clients leave in large numbers, you have learned that the list was weak. You learned it for free, without a payroll commitment.

**What would have to be true for hiring to work**

- At least 20 to 25 confirmed, paying walks a week beyond your own capacity, sustained for two to three months.
- After a price rise, the list still holds.
- Routes are tight enough that paid travel time stays low.
- You can afford about £20,000 a year in total employment cost before the new revenue fully arrives.

**The honest limit of my case**

If your list is long, recent and local, hiring may well be right. Delay could then send those clients to a competitor. The case for caution here is moderate, not overwhelming.

**Bottom line:** Raise prices by 10 to 15% now and watch whether the waiting list survives for two to three months. Hire only if it does.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: hire first, because a waiting list means you are turning paying customers away.

I'd go the other way. Raise your prices first.

A waiting list tells you demand is higher than your supply. It also suggests your current price may be too low. Raising prices costs almost nothing to start. You need no payroll, no extra insurance, and no training.

Here is a test I'd run:
- Raise prices by 15 per cent for new clients only. Leave existing clients alone for now.
- Watch how many people on the waiting list still sign up at the higher rate.
- If most still do, raise again in two months. If few do, you have learned the ceiling for little cost.

The unusual move: create a premium slot. Offer a higher-priced service, such as a fixed-time walk, a pick-up and drop-off, or a small-group walk with a limited number of places each week. Scarcity supports a higher price, and a premium tier lets you charge more without raising the price of everything.

The case for hiring is real. A second person lets you take on more clients, and group walks can raise your income per hour. But hiring brings costs you have not mentioned:
- Employer's liability insurance, which is a legal requirement in the UK once you have an employee. Check the cost with a broker.
- PAYE and workplace pension auto-enrolment.
- The National Minimum Wage or National Living Wage. Check the current rate on gov.uk, because it changes each April.
- Your reputation. If a walker mishandles a dog, you carry the blame.

I am unsure of several things. I don't know your current prices, your margins, how long the waiting list is, whether your clients are price sensitive, or how many hours you can still work yourself. Those figures would change my view. If the waiting list is long and your margins are thin, a hire may come sooner. If you have spare hours and loyal clients, a price rise is the better first step.

My suggested path: raise prices for new clients now. Hire once the higher price has held for about two months and the waiting list still exists. That way the extra income pays for the hire, and you do not take on the risk before the demand is proven.

Bottom line: Raise prices first, test it on new clients, and treat a hire as the second step once the higher price holds.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Raise your prices now and do not hire yet. Start with new clients at about 15% above your current rate, and give existing clients about 10% with four weeks' notice. Hire one part-time walker only when enough people have firmly agreed to pay the new price to cover a wage, and you have about £4,000 in cash to carry the first three months.

## Why
- A waiting list means demand is higher than you can serve, and that usually means your price is too low. A price rise costs £0, takes about 3 hours to set up and can be reversed.
- A part-time employee costs about £16,600 to £17,500 a year. That is 20 to 25 hours a week at the £12.71 National Living Wage (age 21+), plus holiday pay, pension and insurance. Employer National Insurance would add about £1,700, but the £10,500 Employment Allowance probably covers it if you are eligible.
- The hire only pays if your walker runs group walks. Solo walks at about £18 an hour cannot cover £12.71 plus unpaid travel and gaps. Groups of 3 dogs at £12 each bring in about £36 an hour.
- Break-even is roughly 21 extra dog-walks a week. "10 dogs waiting" is not enough.

## Main risks
- **The list may not be real.** Names on a list are not paying clients, and older names will already have found another walker. This is the biggest unknown.
- **Legal costs of employing.** Employers' liability insurance is compulsory, with fines up to £2,500 a day without it. You also need PAYE, pension auto-enrolment and a right-to-work check. Do not call the walker self-employed to avoid these costs. If you set their hours and routes, HMRC can treat them as an employee.
- **A dog lost or hurt on the employee's watch.** Check that your public liability policy covers dogs in an employee's care.
- **Dog limits.** Council Public Space Protection Orders and insurers may cap dogs per walker, often at 4 to 6. This sets the ceiling on what one walker can earn.
- **Two price levels.** Clients compare notes. Bring existing clients up within a few months.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You framed this as hire or raise prices, but you did not give your price, the length or age of your list, or your spare hours. Those figures decide the answer, so gather them first. Also, never hiring is a valid result. If you raise prices until the list is just a few names, you may earn more per hour with no payroll at all.

## Next steps
1. By Fri 6 Nov 2026: check 3 to 5 local competitors' prices. Then phone everyone on your list and quote the new rate. Count the firm yeses in dog-walks per week.
2. By Mon 9 Nov: email existing clients a 10% rise, effective Mon 7 Dec. Check whether you are a sole trader or a company, and ask an accountant whether you qualify for the Employment Allowance.
3. By mid-January 2027: hire part-time if firm demand is 25 or more dog-walks a week beyond what you can cover, your area allows groups of 3 or more, and you have £4,000 in cash. If not, stay solo at the higher price.
