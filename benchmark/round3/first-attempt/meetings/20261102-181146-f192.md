# Boardroom minutes: My company wants everyone in the office four days a week instead of two. Should…

## Agenda

My company wants everyone in the office four days a week instead of two. Should I accept it, push back, or start looking for another job?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

The usual answer: push back politely, ask for three days, and quietly look around.

**My position:** the cautious path is to comply for now and resign nothing. Check your contract. Make a formal request only if you have a strong, specific reason. Search quietly with no deadline. The option most likely to hurt you is acting fast.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Quitting or committing to leave before you have an offer.** High likelihood, high impact. Many UK employers have moved to 3 to 5 days in the office over 2024 to 2026. A new job may carry the same rule, or adopt it within a year. You would also reset your service clock. That matters for unfair dismissal rights and statutory redundancy pay, which needs 2 years' service. The Employment Rights Act 2025 is shortening the unfair dismissal qualifying period, reportedly to 6 months from 2027. I am not certain of the final date, so check it.

2. **Pushing back informally and being marked as resistant.** Medium likelihood, medium to high impact. Some return-to-office mandates are a quiet way to reduce headcount. People who visibly refuse can become easier targets in the next restructure. If you simply do not turn up, it can become a conduct issue.

3. **Accepting and absorbing the real cost.** High likelihood, usually moderate impact. Two extra days over about 46 working weeks is about 92 more commutes a year. At an illustrative £20 a day for travel and lunch, that is about £1,840 a year, after tax. Add 90 to 180 hours of travel time. Your real figure could be much higher with childcare or a long rail season ticket. Work it out before deciding.

4. **Assuming your current two-day pattern is protected.** Uncertain. If hybrid working is written into your contract, or has become an implied term, a one-sided change may need your agreement. If it is only a policy, they can probably change it. I do not know which applies to you. Read your contract and the policy wording first.

**What I do not know:** your commute, any caring duties, any disability (Equality Act reasonable adjustments may apply), your sector's job market, and your savings buffer. Each one could change this advice.

**The honest limit of caution:** if the commute is genuinely unworkable, staying silent is not the safe option either. In that case, use a formal flexible working request under the statutory scheme. You can make up to 2 requests in 12 months. They must decide within 2 months and consult you before refusing. A specific written request is safer than an informal complaint.

**What would have to be true for leaving to work:** a signed offer in hand, a written hybrid or remote term in the new contract, and at least 3 months' expenses saved.

Bottom line: Comply for now, check your contract this week, make a formal request only with a concrete reason, and do not resign until a written offer guarantees better terms.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: push back, or start looking for another job. I think that is too fast.

The upside is real. Four office days can mean more time with managers, more chances on live projects, and more people who know your work. In most firms, the people in the room get the best work. If you want to grow here, this is a cheap way to buy visibility.

The bold move is to say yes, and make it a deal. Accept the four days in writing, and ask for three things in return:

1. A review or promotion discussion within six months.
2. A travel allowance if your commute is long.
3. A written review at six months to check whether the arrangement still works for you.

Under UK law, you have a statutory right to request flexible working from your first day in the job. This comes from the Employment Relations (Flexible Working) Act 2023, in force since 6 April 2024. The employer must respond within two months. You can ask for a different pattern, such as three office days, or a fixed set of days. Treat that as your fallback, not your opening move. Also check your contract, because a change to your place of work may need your agreement.

The limits are clear. I do not know your commute time, your travel costs, your pay, or why the firm is making this change. If the reason is cost cutting or quiet headcount reduction, the upside shrinks. I have not checked any prices or company facts. Operator should check the numbers, and Scout should check any claims about the policy.

The unusual move is to volunteer to run the pilot. Offer to lead one team's four-day plan and report the results. That makes you the person the policy depends on, which is rare leverage.

I am unsure of three things: what your commute costs, whether your role needs in-person work, and whether the firm is still hiring. If those answers are good, accepting with written terms is the strongest play.

Bottom line: accept with written conditions and a six-month review, then use the extra days to become the person the firm cannot spare.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

Note: the question reads as if you lean toward leaving or pushing back. I judged it as if you hoped to stay quiet and accept. The evidence below holds either way.

**What the evidence says**

- Four office days is not unusual now. Surveys say more firms are raising office days than cutting them. One survey found [17% of firms globally plan four-day office schedules](https://hrreview.co.uk/hr-news/strategy-news/office-mandates-more-about-appearance-than-purpose/377999). Treat that as one survey, not a UK rule.
- Workers dislike it. Owl Labs' 2026 report says [almost three-quarters of UK workers see RTO as about visibility, not output](https://remoteworkeurope.eu/news/2026/owl-labs-2026-uk-workers-rto-visibility-not-output/). That is opinion, not proof.
- Best trial evidence: Nick Bloom's Trip.com study of 1,612 staff. Two days at home had [no measurable hit to productivity or promotions, and attrition was 4.8% versus 7.2%](https://fortune.com/2024/06/18/stanford-nick-bloom-hybrid-work-cuts-attrition-no-negative-impact-promoted). It is one firm, and it tests two days at home, not one.
- Bloom also calls mandates ["a cheap way to reduce head count"](https://www.personneltoday.com/?p=322150). Good staff with options tend to leave first.

**Claims to treat as false or weak**

- "RTO mandates raise turnover by 14%": I found no primary source. Unverified.
- "Mandates boost productivity": I found no solid evidence. A Pittsburgh study found [no gain in S&P 500 financial results, and lower satisfaction](https://www.personneltoday.com/?p=322150).
- "A four-day week" and "four office days" get mixed up in many articles. They are different things.

**Your legal position (UK)**

- Check your contract first. If it names an office as your place of work and has no hybrid terms, the employer can often change location or days under a mobility or flexibility clause. If hybrid is written in as a contractual term, a unilateral change may be a breach. I have not seen your contract.
- You can make a statutory flexible working request from day one. The Employment Rights Act 2025 will make employers refuse only where [reasonable, with a tribunal route](https://www.mondaq.com/uk/employee-rights-labour-relations/1751220/employment-rights-act-2025-consultation-on-changes-to-flexible-working-requests). Sources place this in 2027, and the detail is still under consultation. Do not count on it yet.
- Tribunal compensation for a flexible working breach is capped at eight weeks' pay.
- If you have under two years' service, unfair dismissal protection is weak. Check your start date.

**What I am unsure of**

- Whether your firm will enforce it strictly. Many mandates are applied loosely.
- Your commute cost and time. Four days is roughly double your current office days, so cost and hours rise sharply. Work out your own figure before you decide.

**Sensible order of action**

1. Read your contract and the policy's start date.
2. Push back with a specific written request: for example three days, or four with named flexibility. Cite your output, not your preference.
3. Quietly check the market. A third of workers [would consider rejecting a job needing three or more office days](https://hrreview.co.uk/hr-news/strategy-news/one-in-three-would-consider-rejecting-job-if-three-or-more-office-days-required/380375), but many employers are tightening too, so hybrid roles may be harder to find than you expect.

Bottom line: Do not accept silently and do not quit yet; check your contract, make a written flexible working request, and test the job market in parallel.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Push back formally and keep looking quietly, but do not quit and do not simply accept. Read your contract this week. Put in a written flexible working request for 3 office days, or 4 with a fixed set of days. If you have to start doing 4 days before you get an answer, confirm in writing that you are complying "under protest" while you take advice. Leave only if a signed offer gives you better written terms.

## Why
- **Saying nothing is the hidden trap.** If you just start working 4 days without objecting, that can count as agreeing to the change. Any contractual right to hybrid working could then be lost.
- **Asking costs little.** You have had a legal right to request flexible working from day one since 6 April 2024. The employer must decide within 2 months. You can make 2 requests in any 12 months.
- **Quitting early is costly.** Many employers are adding office days, so a new job may have the same rule. Moving also resets your length of service.
- **The cost is real.** At £25 a day, 2 extra days over 46 weeks is about £2,300 a year after tax. That is about £3,200 of gross salary for a basic-rate taxpayer, or about £4,000 at higher rate. A 1-hour commute each way also adds about 184 hours a year.
- **The evidence does not favour mandates.** The best trial (Trip.com, in China) found that 2 days at home made no difference to productivity or promotion. Treat promises of "visibility" as unproven.

## Main risks
- **Contract terms decide your leverage.** If hybrid working is in your contract, or has become an implied term through years of practice, the employer cannot simply change it. If it is only a policy, they probably can. We have not seen your contract.
- **A request is not a right.** Employers can refuse on 8 legal grounds, and the mandate can start while your request is pending. If they breach the process, tribunal awards are capped at 8 weeks' pay.
- **Length of service matters.** You need 2 years today for unfair dismissal protection. That is reportedly falling to 6 months from 1 January 2027. We are fairly confident but not certain, so check gov.uk.
- **Being seen as resistant.** Simply not turning up can become a conduct issue. A job search can also leak through recruiters or LinkedIn.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You framed this as three separate choices: accept, push back or leave. The strongest plan combines the last two. You also gave none of the facts that decide this: commute, costs, caring duties, length of service and start date. Work those out before you choose.

## Next steps
1. **By 9 Nov:** read your contract for a named workplace, a hybrid clause, a variation or mobility clause, your notice period and your start date. Get the policy and its start date in writing. Call Acas (free) if anything is unclear.
2. **By 16 Nov:** set a walk-away line, for example £3,200 gross a year or 5 extra travel hours a week. Then file the written request. Base it on your output and your costs, and mention any care or health needs, since the Equality Act may apply.
3. **By 31 Jan 2027:** review your answer and your search. If the request is refused and you are past your line, apply in earnest. Do not resign without a signed offer, written hybrid terms and 3 months' expenses saved.
