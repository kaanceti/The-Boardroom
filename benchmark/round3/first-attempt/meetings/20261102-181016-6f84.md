# Boardroom minutes: My manager has offered me a team lead role for £3,000 more, but I would stop…

Date: 2026-11-02 18:10  
Mode: full

## Agenda

My manager has offered me a team lead role for £3,000 more, but I would stop doing the hands-on work I enjoy. Should I take it?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic: statement to the board**

The usual answer: take it, because promotions compound and you can always go back.

The owner's wording leans towards declining ("hands-on work I enjoy"). I will judge it as if you wanted to say yes. The cautious case still holds up fairly well.

**What £3,000 is actually worth**
- Basic rate taxpayer: 20% tax plus 8% NI leaves about £2,160 a year, roughly £180 a month.
- Higher rate taxpayer: 40% plus 2% NI leaves about £1,740 a year, roughly £145 a month.
- With a Plan 2 student loan, take off a further 9% (£270).
- Between £100,000 and £125,140, the personal allowance taper makes the effective rate about 62%. You would keep around £1,140.

At 40 hours a week, £180 a month is about £1 an hour. One extra unpaid hour a week cancels most of it.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Hidden hours (high likelihood, high impact).** Leads often lose overtime eligibility and absorb evenings. This is the most likely way the deal turns bad.
2. **The player-coach trap (high, medium).** You may be expected to keep a full hands-on load and manage people as well. That gives you two jobs for £180 a month.
3. **Losing the work you enjoy (certain, impact unknown).** Only you can price this. People rarely value job satisfaction correctly in advance.
4. **Skill atrophy and no easy way back (medium, high).** After 18 to 24 months away from hands-on work, returning can mean a pay cut or looking "failed". I am unsure how true this is in your field.
5. **People problems land on you (medium, medium).** Performance management, grievances and redundancies become your job, with personal and reputational exposure.

**The honest case against caution**
It is not all one-sided. If you decline, someone else may get the role and become your manager. Your manager may read it as low ambition. Future pay rises may cap out sooner on the individual track. I cannot tell how strong these risks are without knowing your employer.

**What would have to be true for "yes" to be right**
- You want management as a career, not just this pay rise.
- Hours are capped, or overtime and the workload split are agreed in writing.
- There is an agreed route back, such as a 6-month trial with the right to return to your current role.
- The £3,000 is a step on a band with a visible next rise, not a one-off.
- Ideally, a senior individual contributor track exists, so declining does not freeze your pay.

**Before deciding, ask your manager three things**
1. What would my week actually look like?
2. Can I return to my current role after 6 months if it does not work?
3. Is there a pay path for me if I stay hands-on?

Bottom line: At about £150 to £180 a month after tax, the money alone does not justify giving up work you enjoy. Decline unless you get written answers on hours, a right to return, and a real pay path.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: weigh the pay rise against the work you enjoy, and stay hands-on if the craft matters more to you.

I will argue for the bold move: take the role.

Note: you seem to lean towards declining. I have judged this as if you hoped to hear the opposite.

**The upside**
- Team leads usually shape hiring, budgets and direction. That leverage is hard to get as an individual contributor.
- Leading people is a skill many senior roles in the UK ask for. It can open doors that technical skill alone may not.
- A £3,000 rise is real money. It may also be the first step to more.
- You may keep some hands-on work. Many leads still review code, design or do client work. Ask your manager to write that into the role.

**The limits**
- £3,000 is small if the role brings no other gains. If your current salary is £40,000, that is 7.5%. After tax and National Insurance, you will take home less than £3,000 a year, depending on your tax band.
- Stepping away from hands-on work is hard to reverse. Returning to a technical role after a year or two can be harder.
- I do not know whether this is a real promotion path or mainly a title change.

**What would change my view**
1. What is your current salary, and what is the new one?
2. Is the scope written down, and how much hands-on work stays?
3. Would your manager agree to a six-month trial with a review date?

Bottom line: Take the role if the scope and trial are in writing and the rise leads to a clear next step. If the work you love disappears and the pay is the only gain, the case is weak.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Don't accept the role on the current terms, but don't turn it down outright yet. Go back to your manager with a counter-offer: more pay (around £5,000 to £6,000), a written share of hands-on time, paid management training, and a 6-month trial with a written right to return. If they refuse all of these, and you don't actually want a career in management, decline.

## Why
- **The money is small.** £3,000 gross is about £2,160 a year after tax and NI at basic rate, which is £180 a month. At higher rate it is about £1,740, or £145 a month. A Plan 2 student loan takes roughly £270 a year more. These figures assume rest-of-UK rates for 2026/27. Scottish rates differ.
- **Extra hours could cancel it.** On a £40,000 salary, about 3 extra unpaid hours a week leaves your hourly pay no higher than now. Team leads often work longer hours, so this is a real risk, not a theoretical one.
- **The loss is certain but the gain isn't.** You will definitely lose work you enjoy. The career benefit is unproven: nobody knows if this employer promotes leads further or pays them above market.
- **Saying "no" on day one has a cost too.** Your manager may see it as low ambition, and the offer may not come back. Negotiating keeps the option open.

## Main risks
- **Two jobs for one rise.** You may keep a full hands-on workload and manage people on top of it. A written split helps, but when deadlines hit, leads drop their own work first.
- **No legal way back.** There is no statutory right to return to your old role. It exists only if your employer agrees to it in writing. Even then, your old post may have been filled.
- **Little support.** One study found almost 60% of first-time managers got no training. First-level managers also had the highest rate of leaving in one large dataset.
- **Tax traps.** If the rise takes your adjusted net income over £60,000 and you claim Child Benefit, the High Income Child Benefit Charge applies. Between £100,000 and £125,140 you would keep only about £1,140.
- **The counter-offer could backfire.** It could annoy your manager, or the offer could be withdrawn.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You have framed this as £3,000 against enjoyment. That is the wrong question. The real one is whether you want to manage people over the next 5 years. If you do, the £3,000 hardly matters. If you don't, no rise this size will fix it. You also say you "would stop" hands-on work, but you haven't confirmed that. Find out what the role actually involves before you decide.
