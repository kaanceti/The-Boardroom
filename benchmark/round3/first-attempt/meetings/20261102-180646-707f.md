### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

# Skeptic's Statement: Should You Incorporate on £38,000 Profit?

The usual answer: "Incorporate once profit passes about £30,000 to £40,000, because it saves tax."

That rule of thumb is now out of date. The case for staying a sole trader is strong, not weak.

**The core numbers (2026/27, my calculation; please have Scout check them)**

- **Sole trader:** income tax is 20% of £25,430, which is £5,086. Class 4 National Insurance is 6% of the same amount, which is £1,526. **Total: about £6,610.**
- **Limited company:** I assumed a £12,570 salary and the rest paid as dividends.
  - Employer National Insurance: £1,136. This is 15% above the £5,000 threshold. You get no Employment Allowance if you are the only employee.
  - Corporation tax: £4,616 at 19%.
  - Dividend tax: £2,062 at 10.75%, after the £500 allowance.
  - **Total: about £7,810.**
- A £5,000 salary instead comes out worse, at about £8,280.

So if you take all the profit out, incorporating likely costs you **about £1,200 more in tax each year**. The cuts since 2023 caused this: corporation tax rose to 19%, employer NI rose to 15%, the dividend allowance fell to £500, and the dividend rate rose in April 2026.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood × impact**

1. **Higher running costs (near certain, medium).** A company accountant typically costs £1,000 to £2,000 a year, against roughly £300 to £800 for a sole trader. You also add payroll, a confirmation statement and statutory accounts. I am not certain of the current Companies House fees. Combined with the tax gap, you could be £2,000 to £3,500 a year worse off.
2. **Misreading why people incorporate (likely, high).** The savings people talk about mostly come from leaving profit inside the company. If you need all £38,000 to live on, that benefit does not apply to you.
3. **Admin penalties (moderate, medium).** Late filing at Companies House starts at £150. Directors' duties and the director's loan account rules add traps a sole trader never faces.
4. **IR35 (only if you contract through agencies or for large clients; potentially high).** HMRC could treat your income as employment, which would wipe out any advantage.
5. **Hard to reverse (low, medium).** Closing a company costs money and paperwork. You cannot simply switch back.

**Fair points on the other side**

- Making Tax Digital will require you to file quarterly as a sole trader from April 2027, because your income is over £30,000. That shrinks, but does not close, the admin gap.
- Limited liability protects you only if your work carries real liability risk. Banks often ask for personal guarantees anyway.
- Some clients prefer suppliers who trade as a company.

**What would have to be true for incorporating to work**

- You keep £10,000 or more of profit in the company each year, or your profit is clearly heading towards £50,000 or more, or you face real liability risk.
- Your accountant runs a personal comparison that shows a net gain after their fees.

**What I am unsure of**

- The exact 2026/27 Companies House fees.
- Whether any rates change at the next Budget.

Bottom line: on £38,000 profit, all taken as income, incorporating probably costs you £1,200 to £3,000 a year more. Stay a sole trader unless you will leave profit in the business or face real liability risk.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

The usual answer: stay self-employed until profit is clearly higher or you need to keep money in the business.

I want to make the bold case, but the numbers only partly support it at £38,000.

**Rough 2026/27 comparison (my estimates, not checked):**

On these figures the company costs you about £1,200 a year more before accounting fees, which are often £1,000 to £2,000 a year.

**The strongest case for incorporating:**
- You can keep profit in the company at 19% corporation tax, instead of paying income tax on all of it now. This helps if you reinvest, hold cash for a planned purchase, or build a buffer.
- Company pension contributions are a business cost. They cut corporation tax and avoid employer NI. For example, a £10,000 contribution saves about £1,900 in corporation tax. Check the rules for your situation.
- A company can be easier to sell, bring in a partner or raise investment later.

**Where the upside is limited:**
- If you take everything out as salary and dividends each year, you gain nothing and lose money on fees.
- Limited liability matters more for risky work than for a £38,000 consultant or tradesperson.
- You must file accounts and confirmation statements, and deadlines are strict.

**What I'm unsure of:**
- Current rates and thresholds. Scout should verify them against HMRC before you act.
- Whether you would keep profit in the company or pay it all out.
- Your pension plans and whether you have other income.

**What I would check first:** whether you will keep at least £10,000 or so in the business each year. If yes, get a quote from an accountant for both options and run your actual figures.

Bottom line: At £38,000 profit with full extraction, staying self-employed is slightly better. Incorporating only pays if you retain profit or make pension contributions, so decide that before you move.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Stay a sole trader. At £38,000 profit, a limited company would most likely leave you about £1,200 a year worse off in tax. Higher accountancy fees would add roughly another £500 to £1,500 a year. Look at this again only if you plan to leave real money in the business or a client or liability risk requires a company.

## Why
- **The tax sums favour staying as you are (2026/27 rates, England, Wales and NI, no other income assumed).** As a sole trader you pay about £6,612: £5,086 income tax plus £1,526 Class 4 NI. Through a company paying a £12,570 salary and the rest as dividends, you pay about £7,813: £1,136 employer NI, £4,616 corporation tax and £2,062 dividend tax at 10.75%. Four members checked these figures separately and got the same answer.
- **What changed.** The basic dividend rate rose to 10.75% in April 2026. The dividend allowance is now £500. A one-person company gets no Employment Allowance, so you would pay 15% employer NI.
- **Keeping profit in a company helps less than people think.** Profit left in the company is taxed at 19%, against 26% for you now. That saves about £700 on £10,000. But you pay dividend tax when you take it out later, so it mostly delays tax rather than saving it.
- **Pensions don't change the answer.** You already get 20% tax relief on pension contributions as a sole trader. A company only saves the extra 6% NI, about £600 on a £10,000 contribution.

## Main risks
- **If you incorporate anyway:** a company has traps you don't face now. If you take money out before dividends are declared, the company can owe a 33.75% charge on it (called s455). Companies House fines for late accounts start at £150. You may also face IR35 if most of your work comes from one client. And limited liability won't protect you on loans or leases where you sign a personal guarantee.
- **If you stay a sole trader:** Making Tax Digital (quarterly digital updates to HMRC) is coming. It is based on turnover, not profit. If your 2025/26 turnover was over £30,000, quarterly digital reporting starts in April 2027. If your 2024/25 turnover was over £50,000, you may already be in it. Missing this brings penalties.
- **Uncertainty:** rates can change at the next Budget. The fee ranges quoted to the board are estimates, not checked against real quotes.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You asked using profit alone. The answer also depends on facts you didn't give: your turnover, any other income, whether you live in Scotland, student loan repayments, mortgage plans, and whether you need all £38,000 to live on. Several of these could change the details. None is likely to reverse the decision at this profit level.
