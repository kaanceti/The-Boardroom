# Boardroom minutes: Should I pay £400 for an evening SQL course or learn it from free online resourc

Date: 2026-10-18 10:24  
Mode: full

## Agenda

Should I pay £400 for an evening SQL course or learn it from free online resources?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement: £400 SQL course vs free resources**

I do not have the course details, so some of this is conditional.

**Risks of paying £400, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **You pay for content that is free.** SQL is one of the best documented skills online. Free options include SQLBolt, Mode's SQL tutorial, W3Schools, PostgreSQL docs, and SQLZoo. The core syntax (SELECT, WHERE, JOIN, GROUP BY) takes roughly 15 to 30 hours to learn. Likelihood: high. Impact: £400 lost.

2. **You attend but do not practise.** Evening classes feel like progress. Skill comes from writing queries on messy data. If the course gives 2 hours a week for 6 weeks, that is 12 hours. It is not enough alone. Likelihood: medium to high. Impact: money and time lost.

3. **The certificate does not matter.** Most employers test SQL with a short exercise. They rarely care about a private course certificate. Likelihood: high. Impact: low to medium.

4. **Wrong SQL dialect.** The course may teach MySQL when your target job uses PostgreSQL, BigQuery or SQL Server. The basics transfer, but the details differ. Likelihood: medium. Impact: low.

5. **Free learning stalls.** This is the real risk on the other side. Many people quit free courses because nothing is at stake. Likelihood: medium to high, depending on your track record. Impact: months lost.

**Hidden costs of the paid route**
- Travel and evening time, perhaps 20 to 30 hours in total.
- Possible fees for software or exams.
- A fixed schedule you may miss.

**Most likely reason this fails either way**
You learn the syntax but never build anything with real data. You then cannot show the skill to anyone.

**What would have to be true for the £400 to be worth it**
- You have tried free resources before and quit.
- The course ends with a project or has an instructor who reviews your queries.
- The syllabus covers joins, subqueries, window functions and your target dialect.
- A refund or trial lesson is offered.
- Your employer might reimburse some or all of the fee.

**What would have to be true for free to work**
- You can commit 3 to 5 hours a week for 6 to 8 weeks.
- You set a project goal, such as analysing a public dataset (UK government open data, for example).
- You can find a person to check your work, such as a forum or a friend who knows SQL.

**What I am unsure of**
- Your goal: a career change, a current job need, or curiosity.
- Your history with self-study.
- The course's reputation and syllabus.
- Whether your employer values paid training.

**A cheap test:** Spend two weeks on free material first, about 10 hours. If you finish and want more structure, the course is still available. If you cannot hold the habit for two weeks, that tells you something about the paid course too.

Bottom line: Start with free resources for two weeks and pay £400 only if you stall, or if the course includes reviewed project work and your target dialect.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Advocate's statement: SQL course or free resources**

I'm excited about this one. SQL is one of the best skills per hour spent. Most of the basics take 20 to 40 hours. It shows up in analyst, operations, finance, product and marketing jobs. Either route can get you there.

**The case for the £400 course**

- A fixed evening slot forces you to show up. If it runs 8 weeks at 2.5 hours, that is 20 hours at £20 an hour. Money already spent is a strong push to finish.
- A teacher can unstick you in two minutes when you hit an error. Alone, that can cost an hour.
- Classmates and feedback on your queries help. Free tools only tell you if the answer was right, not if it was good.
- A good course can include a real project. That gives you something to show an employer.

**The case for free**

The content is genuinely good and costs £0. SQLBolt takes about 3 to 5 hours. Kaggle Learn's Intro to SQL takes about 3 hours. Mode's SQL tutorial and SQLZoo cover joins and aggregates. For practice, DataLemur and LeetCode have hundreds of interview-style problems. A course cannot teach you much that these do not.

**Where the upside is limited**

- A certificate from an evening course carries little weight with employers. Your portfolio and your ability to answer questions matter more.
- The course is not what raises your pay. Using SQL in a role does that.
- I do not know which course this is, who teaches it, or what it covers. A weak £400 course is worse than a strong free path.

**Your likely advantage**

You are asking the question before spending, which is a good sign. If you have a real use for SQL, such as data at work or a target job, you will learn faster than most.

**What I am unsure of**

- Whether you have tried free resources before and dropped off. That is the biggest factor.
- Whether your employer would pay or reimburse the fee. Worth asking.
- How much a course would shorten your path to a job. I cannot put a number on that.

**A cheap test**

Spend 2 weeks on free material before paying. Aim for 8 hours total, finishing by 1 November 2026. Use Kaggle Learn, then SQLBolt, then 10 problems on DataLemur. If you hit the 8 hours and want more structure, the course is then a well-informed buy. If you stalled, that tells you accountability is your real problem, and £400 may be worth it. Also ask the provider for a syllabus, a refund policy and a sample session first.

Bottom line: Start free for 2 weeks, since it costs nothing and tests your follow-through; buy the £400 course only if you stall or if the syllabus and refund terms check out.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Scout, evidence statement**

The content of an SQL course is not worth £400. Free material covers the same ground. What the £400 might buy is structure and a deadline, and that is worth less than people assume.

**What free gets you**
- Free SQL options include [SQLBolt, Khan Academy's Intro to SQL, Kaggle and Scrimba](https://scrimba.com/articles/best-free-sql-courses/). Several run queries in your browser with no setup.
- One roundup notes that free courses rarely fail on content. [They fail on practice.](https://scrimba.com/articles/best-free-sql-courses/) So pick ones where you write queries yourself.
- Paid self-paced is cheap too. [LearnSQL.com lists a 20-hour course at £31 and a lifetime plan at £119.](https://learnsql.com/blog/sql-courses-uk/)

**What £400 is up against**
- £400 is around the going rate for a short live course. [London Academy of IT lists a 12-hour beginners course at £420.](https://learnsql.com/blog/sql-courses-uk/) That is about £33 per hour.
- It is cheap next to other providers. [Wise Owl charges £1,425 plus VAT for two days.](https://www.wiseowl.co.uk/sql/London/) Those are daytime classes, so they don't fit your evening plan.
- I found no confirmed evening SQL course at exactly £400. Check what yours includes: hours, instructor access, a certificate and project feedback.

**Claims to treat with care**
- **"Instructor-led courses finish at 60 to 96%, self-paced at 2.5%."** These numbers come from vendor blogs with no clear method. The direction is plausible. The size is not reliable. [Rework's review](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/edtech-growth/cohort-based-vs-self-paced-learning) says the gap is partly selection bias. People who pay, or are told to attend, are more motivated to start with.
- **A controlled study found a smaller effect.** In one MITx course, self-paced cut completion by 6% and pass rate by 10%. That is one course, and it wasn't SQL.
- **"A certificate will get you hired."** I found no evidence for this. Free courses also offer certificates. Employers usually test SQL in interviews. I did not search for hiring data, so I'm unsure here.

**What I'm unsure of**
- I don't know your starting level, your goal or your employer's attitude. A career switch and a small skills top-up are different cases.
- If your employer reimburses the fee, the maths changes.
- I haven't checked the quality of the specific course you are considering.

**A cheap test**
Spend two weeks on a free course, such as SQLBolt, then Kaggle. Aim for 3 evenings a week. If you finish that and want more, you have lost nothing. If you keep dropping off, the £400 may buy accountability. But a fixed weekly study slot costs nothing and may do the same job.

Bottom line: Start with free resources for two weeks, and pay the £400 only if you stall or need live feedback that you can't get any other way.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Do not pay the £400 yet. Spend 14 days on free material, with a hard target of 10 logged hours by 1 November 2026. Buy a course only if you miss that target, or if the course has reviewed project work, a refund window and your target SQL dialect.

## Where the board agreed
- Free content is as good as most paid syllabi. You pay for structure and feedback.
- A 14-day free test costs nothing and shows whether you follow through.
- Ask for the syllabus, refund terms and a sample session before paying.
- Ask your employer about funding first.
- A certificate from a small evening course carries little weight.

## Where the board disagreed
- Hours to learn core SQL: 15 to 30 (Skeptic) against 20 to 40 (Advocate). Neither is sourced.
- Value of structure. Advocate and Skeptic see real value in a live teacher. Scout says it is worth less than people assume.
- What counts as a stall. Operator says one missed fortnight. Skeptic says that is too quick.

## Where I overrule the board
- Scout opened with "not worth £400" before anyone has seen the course. That is a verdict without evidence. I treat it as unknown.
- Scout's £33 an hour is wrong. £420 for 12 hours is £35 an hour.
- Advocate's "£20 an hour" rests on an invented 20-hour course. Use the real hours.
- Operator's "10 of 10 hours" does not match its own plan, which adds up to 13. I set one number: 10 hours.
- Operator's "pay after one missed fortnight" is too harsh. One bad fortnight is weak evidence. If you miss, restart for one more week before paying.
- Advocate's "money spent pushes you to finish" is sunk-cost thinking, and Scout's selection-bias point weakens it.
- Skeptic's "20 to 30 hours of travel" double counts class time.
- The 3, 6 and 12 month skill levels and "junior analyst at 240 hours" are guesses. Ignore them.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You have not said why you want SQL. That is the biggest gap. For a data job or a raise, £400 is small and speed matters. For curiosity, free wins. You also gave no weekly hours, no course details and no word on employer funding. Without these, the board is guessing. Be careful too that "researching the choice" does not replace learning. Ten hours of queries teaches more than another hour of comparing courses.
