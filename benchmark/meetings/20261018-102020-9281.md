## Agenda

I own a one-bed buy-to-let flat in Manchester and the service charge has risen sharply two years running. Should I sell or keep renting it out?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement: sell or keep the Manchester flat**

I do not have your numbers. So I rank the risks and then say what must be true.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **The service charge keeps rising (high likelihood, high impact).** Two sharp rises in a row usually mean a cause. It could be insurance, a new managing agent, or a backlog of repairs. Repairs often lead to a Section 20 major works bill. That can be thousands of pounds per flat, billed in one go. Many Manchester blocks built after 2000 also face cladding or fire safety work. I do not know which applies to your block.

2. **Your yield is squeezed (high likelihood, medium to high impact).** A rise of £500 a year cuts your net income by £500. It does not cut your rent. Check your net yield after service charge, ground rent, agent fees, insurance and void weeks. If it is under about 4% of today's value, you are being paid little for the risk.

3. **Regulation and tax (high likelihood, medium impact).** The Renters' Rights Act 2025 ends fixed terms and no-fault evictions. Check the dates in force and what applies to you. Mortgage interest relief is only a 20% tax credit. Capital gains tax on a sale is lower than it was, but still real. I am unsure of your gain and your tax band.

4. **Mortgage reset (medium likelihood, high impact).** If your fixed rate ends soon, a higher rate may turn the flat cash negative.

5. **Weak resale (medium likelihood, high impact).** Buyers and their lenders now ask about service charge levels, cladding and the EWS1 form. A rising charge cuts the pool of buyers. Waiting could lower your sale price, not just your income.

6. **Rent falls or long voids (low to medium likelihood).** Manchester has had strong rent demand. Still, new flat supply in the city centre is a risk for a one-bed.

**What I would check this week**

- The last three years of service charge accounts, line by line.
- Any notice of major works. Ask the managing agent in writing.
- Your sale value, mortgage balance and early repayment charge.
- Your capital gains tax estimate from an accountant.

**What would have to be true to keep it**

- The rise has a one-off cause and is not a trend.
- No big works bill is coming.
- Net yield stays above about 4% and cash flow is not negative.
- Your mortgage rate is fixed for a while yet.
- You do not need the cash for something better.

**What would make selling right**

- Major works are pending or the agent will not explain the rises.
- Cash flow is negative or will be at remix.
- The sale price is falling as buyers walk away.

I am unsure about all of this until I see your figures. The worst case is a surprise works bill landing just after you decide to hold.

Bottom line: Do not decide yet. Get the accounts and any major works notice within a few weeks. If a large bill is pending or net yield is under about 4%, sell before buyers see it. Otherwise keep it.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Statement from Advocate**

My case is for keeping the flat, but only if a quick check on your numbers passes. Selling is the better move if it fails.

**Why keeping has real upside**

- Manchester rental demand is strong. Gross yields on one-beds have been around 5% to 7% in recent years. I haven't checked current figures, so treat that range as unverified.
- A service charge rise is a cost problem, not a demand problem. Costs can be attacked. Rents can be reset upward.
- Under the Renters' Rights Act, you can raise rent once a year to market level through a formal notice. Check the exact process with a current landlord guide.
- You may be able to cut the charge. Ask the managing agent for a full cost breakdown. If costs look unreasonable, you can challenge them at the First-tier Tribunal. Leaseholders win partial reductions more often than people expect, though I can't give you a success rate.
- If other owners are angry too, you could pursue Right to Manage. That can take control of the budget.
- Selling costs real money. Expect agent and legal fees of roughly 1.5% to 3% of the price, plus Capital Gains Tax on any gain. The rate is 18% or 24%, and the annual allowance is only £3,000.

**A quick test, with illustrative numbers**

Say rent is £1,100 a month, so £13,200 a year. Say the charge went from £1,800 to £2,600 a year. That is about 20% of your rent, up from about 14%. It hurts, but the flat can still work. If the charge is nearer 30% of rent, it probably doesn't.

**Where the upside is limited**

- A one-bed flat in a managed block rarely delivers big capital growth.
- Rising charges can be capitalised by buyers. They may push down what you can sell for, so waiting could cost you.
- If the rises come from building safety or major works, more may follow. A big bill under a Section 20 consultation could be a one-off hit of thousands of pounds.
- Mortgage interest tax relief is limited to a 20% credit, which squeezes higher-rate taxpayers.

**What I'm unsure of**

I don't know your rent, mortgage, purchase price, tax band, or why the charge rose. Those facts decide this.

**Do this first, within 30 days**

1. Request the service charge accounts and reserve fund details.
2. Ask for upcoming major works.
3. Get one agent's sale valuation and one rent valuation.
4. Compare your net yield after charges to what the sale proceeds would earn elsewhere.

Bottom line: Keep it for now and fight the charge, because a rent rise and a cost challenge can fix this, but sell if net yield after charges falls below what your sale proceeds could earn elsewhere or major works are coming.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Operator's statement**

**Steps, in order**
1. **By 25 Oct (2 hours):** Pull your figures. You need rent, service charge for the last 3 years, ground rent, mortgage balance and rate, purchase price and date, and agent fee.
2. **By 1 Nov (1 hour):** Email the managing agent. Ask for the last 3 years of accounts, the budget, the sinking fund balance and any planned major works. Works over £250 per flat trigger Section 20 consultation. Ask about those first.

4. **By 15 Nov:** Decide using the test below.

**The test**
Net yield = (annual rent minus service charge, ground rent, agent fees, insurance, voids, repairs) divided by what you would have after selling costs and tax.

Illustrative only, these are my assumptions, not your figures: value £150,000, rent £950 a month (£11,400 a year), service charge £2,500, agent £1,370, voids £950, repairs and insurance £600. That leaves about £6,000 a year before income tax. That is 4.0% on £150,000. After tax and selling costs, the real yield is lower.

If your yield after tax is below what cash or a tracker fund would pay you, sell. I believe cash pays around 4%, but check current rates. If big works are coming, sell sooner.

**Selling costs, rough:** Agent 1% to 1.5% plus VAT. Conveyancing £1,500 to £2,000. Capital gains tax at 18% or 24% on any gain, with only a £3,000 allowance. Verify these rates with your accountant.

**Realistic numbers**
- **Sell path:** Instruct an agent in January 2027. Expect 3 to 6 months to complete on a leasehold flat. At 3 months you are under offer. At 6 months you are likely complete. At 12 months the cash is working elsewhere.
- **Keep path:** At 3 months you have challenged unclear charges. At 6 months you have raised rent to market. At 12 months you know the actual charge. If it rose again, you have lost another year.

**What does not fit**
- Chasing the managing agent and a tribunal challenge under Section 27A takes months. It only works if the charges are unreasonable. Most rises are not.
- Renters' Rights Act changes to tenancies have, I believe, started in 2026. Check the current rules. They make an unwanted tenant harder to remove, so sell vacant if you can.

**What I am unsure of**
- Your rent, equity, mortgage terms and tax band.
- Whether the rises are one-off works or a permanent new level.
- Local sale prices. Manchester one-beds have had heavy supply, so valuations matter.

Do not sit on this. Two more rises will cost you more than any fee above.

Bottom line: Send me the numbers by 25 October. If your after-tax yield is below about 4% or major works are coming, sell and list in January 2027. Otherwise keep it and review in 6 months.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

I can't tell you sell or keep yet. I don't have your numbers. Here is what the evidence says.

**Rent**
- The [ONS page](https://www.ons.gov.uk/visualisations/housingpriceslocal/E08000003) gives a Manchester one-bed average of about £993 a month (June 2026). A [secondary site](https://payprecision.co.uk/where-to-live/manchester/) quotes £998 for July 2026, and I could not check that against ONS.
- A third-party page says rents rose about 3% in the year to April 2026. I have not verified that.
- So a service charge rise of even £50 a month would take a visible bite out of about £1,000 of rent.

**Law changes that matter**
- Section 21 "no-fault" evictions ended on 1 May 2026 under the Renters' Rights Act, according to [law firm and agent summaries](https://www.lawyer-monthly.com/2026/04/sell-buy-to-let-after-section-21-ends/). I did not check GOV.UK itself.
- To sell with a tenant in place, you must now use a Section 8 ground (Ground 1A). One source says four months' notice, and that you can't use it in the early months of a tenancy. Another says the flat must stay off the rental market for 12 months afterwards. The sources differ on details.
- The outdated claim is that you can quickly clear a tenant for a sale. That is no longer safe to assume. Selling to another landlord with the tenant in place may be easier. That is my inference, not something I found.

**Tax on selling**
- Capital gains tax on residential property is 18% or 24%. The annual exempt amount is £3,000 for 2026/27, per [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/capital-gains-tax-rates-and-allowances). Sources disagree on where the band threshold sits, so check it.
- You must report and pay within 60 days of completion.
- Selling costs, which I did not price, come on top of this.

**The service charge itself**
- A rise is not final just because it was billed. A leaseholder can ask the First-tier Tribunal to rule on whether a charge is reasonable and payable, under section 27A. Recent Manchester decisions, such as [Malvern Grove, M20 (May 2026)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a0f0ff82ff6e7a6e4d59db0/malvern_grove_decision.pdf), found charges not payable. Others, such as [Old Market Street, M9](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6a3a970630b491f55b3c47c2/Apartment_4_73_Old_Market_Street._Manchester_SC.Decision.pdf), found only part payable. [Egerton Street, Oldham](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/695f9be8714f11cb497765b6/110_Egerton_Street_Oldham_OL1_3UE.pdf), upheld the charges.
- Outcomes are mixed. Do not assume you will win.

**What I could not find**
- Your building's charge history, lease length and ground rent.
- Local sale prices for your block. Large rises can hurt resale, because buyers and lenders look hard at service charges. I did not find a source for how much.

**Numbers you need**
1. Net yield: (annual rent minus service charge, ground rent, insurance, agent fees and repairs) divided by what you would net after selling.
2. Your gain and the tax on it.
3. What the sale money would earn elsewhere.

Bottom line: Don't sell in a panic. First get the service charge breakdown, check whether it can be challenged, and work out your net yield after tax and sale costs. If that yield is below what you'd earn elsewhere, plan the sale with the new Section 8 rules in mind.

## Decision
Do not sell yet, and do not commit to keeping it either. Spend about four weeks getting the real figures. Then sell if net yield after all costs and tax is below about 4%, or if a major works bill is pending. Otherwise keep it and challenge the charge.

## Where the board agreed

- Get three years of service charge accounts and any notice of major works first.

- **Gap:** Nobody asked whether the flat is let now, or when the tenancy began.
