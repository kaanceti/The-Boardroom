## Agenda

Should I spend £1,500 on a new laptop now to work on side projects in the evenings?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement: £1,500 laptop for evening side projects**

I do not know what your side projects are, what you own now, or what you earn. Those three gaps decide most of this. My view is conditional.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **The purchase replaces the work (high likelihood, medium impact).** A new machine feels like progress. Many people buy the tool, then do little with it. The key question is whether you have shipped anything in the last 3 months on your current machine. If not, hardware is not the bottleneck.

2. **Your current machine is probably good enough (high likelihood, medium impact).** Most side projects, such as web apps, scripts, writing and light data work, run fine on a laptop that is 4 to 5 years old. A £1,500 spec is only justified by specific workloads: video editing, local AI models, large builds, 3D or games. I am unsure whether you have one.

3. **Spec mismatch (medium likelihood, medium impact).** Buying on a vague need often means overpaying for the wrong thing. For example, a laptop with a strong GPU is poor value if your work is mostly CPU and RAM bound.

4. **Opportunity cost (medium likelihood, medium impact).** £1,500 could cover about 6 months of a modest hosting, tools and course budget. It could also sit as a buffer. If this money is not clearly spare, the risk rises sharply.

5. **Depreciation (certain, low to medium impact).** Laptops typically lose around 30 to 40 percent of value in the first 2 years. That is roughly £450 to £600 gone. This is a real cost of buying early.

6. **Timing (medium likelihood, low impact).** It is 18 October 2026. Black Friday is 27 November, about 6 weeks away. Waiting could save perhaps 10 to 15 percent, so £150 to £225. I am unsure of the exact discounts on the model you want. Check price history before trusting that.

7. **Financing or tax errors (low likelihood, medium impact).** If you put it on a credit card and carry a balance, the interest erodes any benefit. If any project earns income, you may be able to claim part of the cost, but I cannot confirm your position. Check HMRC rules or ask an accountant.

**What would have to be true for this to work**

- You have a named project with a clear next step, and your current laptop demonstrably blocks it. For example, builds take over 5 minutes, or it crashes.
- The £1,500 is spare cash, not buffer money, and no debt is involved.
- You have worked on projects at least 3 evenings a week for the past 2 months. That shows the habit exists.
- You have checked the price of a cheaper option, around £700 to £900, and can say what it fails to do.

**Most likely reason this dies:** the laptop arrives, the novelty fades within 4 to 6 weeks, and the real constraint, which is evening energy and time, is unchanged.

**A cheap test:** for 30 days, log each evening you work and each time the current laptop stopped you. If the laptop blocked you more than a few times, buy. If not, do not.

Bottom line: Do not buy yet. Run the 30-day test, then buy during Black Friday if the old laptop is clearly the blocker.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Advocate's statement**

I like this idea, with one condition. The laptop has to remove a real obstacle.

**The upside**

- The cost per use is low. Say you work 3 evenings a week for 48 weeks a year. Over 3 years that is about 430 sessions. At £1,500, that is about £3.50 per session. At 2 hours each, it is under £1.75 an hour.
- Good tools reduce friction. If your current machine is slow, noisy, or hot, you lose minutes at the start of each evening. Small delays kill side-project habits. A machine that opens fast and never gets in your way helps you keep going.
- A dedicated evening machine can mark a boundary between work and play. Some people find that this alone makes them start more often. I'm not sure it applies to you.
- It can pay back. If a side project earns even £50 a month, that is £600 a year. A new laptop could also help you learn skills that raise your day-job pay. Neither is guaranteed.
- Hardware holds some value. A good laptop may resell for 30 to 40 percent of its price after 3 years. That is my estimate, not a checked figure.

**Where the upside is limited**

- A laptop does not make a project succeed. Ideas, time, and follow-through matter far more.
- Most side projects (web apps, writing, light design, general coding) run fine on a machine costing £700 to £1,000. The extra £500 to £800 buys speed and build quality, not new ability.
- If your projects need heavy video, 3D, or local AI work, then £1,500 makes more sense. I don't know what yours need.

**What I don't know**

- What laptop you have now, and what is actually wrong with it.
- Whether the £1,500 is spare cash or comes from savings you need.
- Whether you have a record of finishing side projects, or whether this would be your first push.

**How to get the best version of this**

1. Name the exact problem with your current laptop. Write it down. If you can't, wait.
2. Pick one project and work on it for 4 weeks on your current machine. If you hit the hardware limit, buy with confidence.
3. If the problem is clear, set a budget based on needs. Check refurbished models from the maker's own store. These often cost 15 to 20 percent less with a warranty.
4. Buy only with cash you won't miss.

My strongest argument is that you will use it for years, so the daily cost is small. My honest caveat is that this only holds if you actually work on projects in the evenings. A laptop bought to inspire motivation usually doesn't.

Bottom line: Yes, if your current machine is a proven bottleneck and the £1,500 is spare cash. If not, prove the habit for 4 weeks first, or buy a cheaper model.
