## Agenda

Should I move my investments from index funds into individual shares?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement: moving from index funds to individual shares**

I would not make this move as framed. I lack key facts, so treat my numbers as general, not personal.

**What I don't know.** I don't know your amount, age, tax situation, account type, or why you want to switch. Those change the answer a lot.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **You probably underperform the index.** This is the most likely failure. SPIVA reports from S&P Dow Jones Indices have shown for years that roughly 85-90% of active US large-cap funds trail the S&P 500 over 15 years. Those are professionals. Individual investors tend to do worse. I'm quoting these figures from memory, so Scout should check the latest report.

2. **A few stocks drive most returns.** Research by Hendrik Bessembinder found that a small share of stocks produced most of the market's long-run gains. Many stocks lose money. If you hold 10 to 20 names, you may miss the winners entirely.

3. **Concentration risk.** An index fund like a total-market fund holds thousands of companies. A 15-stock portfolio can fall 50% on two bad names. One company can go to zero. An index cannot.

4. **Behavior.** Selling in a panic, chasing recent winners, and trading too often cost more than fees do. This risk grows when the stock is one you picked and feel attached to.

5. **Tax cost.** In a taxable account, selling index fund holdings triggers capital gains tax. In the US, long-term rates are 0%, 15% or 20% depending on income, plus possibly 3.8% on top. A large gain could cost you thousands of dollars before you buy anything. In a retirement account this risk mostly vanishes.

6. **Costs and time.** Trading is cheap now, but spreads, tax drag, and research time are real. Doing it properly may take 5 to 10 hours a month.

7. **Timing.** Selling everything at once means you pick one entry day. Today is 18 October 2026. I can't say whether markets are high or low, and nobody reliably can.

**What would have to be true for this to work**

- You have a real edge, such as deep knowledge of one industry, and you can name it in one sentence.
- You can afford to lose the money you move. A common cap is 5% to 10% of the portfolio.
- You hold at least 20 to 30 stocks across sectors, or accept that you are gambling.
- You keep a written rule for when to sell, and you follow it.
- You track your results against the index for at least 3 to 5 years, honestly.
- The tax bill is small or zero.

**A safer version.** Keep 90% to 95% in the index fund. Use the rest as a "satellite" for stocks. If you beat the index after 5 years, raise the share slowly.

**Where I may be wrong.** If your account is tax-sheltered and you love researching companies, the cost is lower. Some people also stay invested better when they enjoy the process.

Bottom line: Do not move everything; cap individual shares at 5% to 10% of your money, and only after checking the tax bill.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Statement from Advocate**

I will make the best case I honestly can. Then I will say where it runs out.

**The upside is real, but it is narrow.** Index funds give you the market return, minus a fee of about 0.03% to 0.20% a year. They can never beat the market. Individual shares can. Winners can also be huge. A 5% slice of your money in a stock that rises 10 times becomes 50% of your original portfolio value. That adds 45 points to your total. No index fund can do that.

**The best version of this idea is not a full switch.** I would back a "core and satellite" plan:

- Keep 85% to 95% in a broad index fund.
- Put 5% to 15% into 5 to 10 individual shares.
- Use a tax-sheltered account for the shares if you have one (an ISA, IRA or 401(k), depending on your country).
- Write down why you bought each share and what would make you sell.

**Your possible advantages.** I do not know them yet, so these are questions:

- Do you work in an industry where you understand companies better than most investors?
- Do you have a long time horizon, such as 15 years or more?
- Do you have steady income, so you will not be forced to sell in a crash?
- Can you sit through a 40% fall in one share without panic selling?

If you answer yes to most of these, a small satellite is reasonable. It also teaches you a lot at low cost.

**Where the upside is limited.** This is the honest part:

- Hendrik Bessembinder's research on US stocks (1926 to 2016) found that about 4% of shares created all the net gain above Treasury bills. Most shares did worse than that. Picking the few winners is hard.
- The S&P SPIVA reports show that roughly 85% to 90% of active US large-cap funds trail the index over 15 years. Professionals with teams struggle here.
- A full switch removes the one thing index funds do well, which is spreading your risk.
- Selling funds you hold in a taxable account may trigger capital gains tax, which cuts your starting amount.

**What I am unsure of.** I do not know your age, the amount, your country, your tax setup, or your experience. Those change the answer a lot. I also cannot say whether you have any real edge. Most people think they do, and most do not.

Bottom line: Do not move everything. Keep at least 85% in index funds and try a 5% to 15% satellite of 5 to 10 shares, because that keeps most of the upside you want with a loss you can survive.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Operator's statement**

I do not have your portfolio size, tax situation, or weekly free hours. Without those I cannot size this. Send them. Until then I use an example: a $100,000 portfolio.

**What a full switch costs you**

- Selling index funds may trigger capital gains tax. On $50,000 of gains at 15%, that is $7,500 gone on day one. I am unsure of your rate and your account type. In a tax-sheltered account this cost is zero.
- Time: 10 stocks at about 5 hours of research each is 50 hours upfront. Then about 2 hours per stock per quarter, so 80 hours a year. If you cannot spare roughly 7 hours a month, this will not get done properly.
- Running costs: many brokers charge $0 per trade. An index fund costs about 0.03% to 0.20% a year, so $30 to $200 on $100,000. Individual shares cost less in fees but more in time and mistakes.

**What the numbers look like**

I cannot forecast returns. I can tell you the spread.

- 3 months: nothing is knowable. Results will be noise.
- 6 months: a 10-stock portfolio can easily trail or beat the index by 5 points. That tells you nothing about skill.
- 12 months: a gap of 10 to 15 points either way is common. That is my rough estimate, not a measured figure.
- Research by Hendrik Bessembinder found that about 4% of US stocks produced all the net gain of the market since 1926. Miss those few and you trail the index. Scout should check this.

**If you insist, do it in this order**

1. Week 1: write down your goal, your time limit, and the maximum loss you accept. Example: 5% of the portfolio, $5,000, no more.
2. Weeks 2 to 4: open or use a broker with $0 commissions. Check the tax cost of every sale before you place it.
3. Month 2: buy 5 to 8 stocks, no more than $1,000 each, from your sell proceeds or new savings. Do not sell index funds to fund it.
4. Every quarter: 2 hours per stock. Write down why you own it and what would make you sell.
5. Month 12 review, on a fixed date: compare to the index fund over the same dates. If you trail it, stop.

**What does not fit**

- A full switch with no written rules is not a plan. It is a mood.
- Checking prices daily does not count as research.
- If you have under 7 hours a month, skip the whole thing.

**What I am unsure of**

Your tax rate, your account type, your experience, and whether this is about returns or about wanting control. Those change the answer.

Bottom line: Do not move your investments. Keep the index funds as the core, cap individual shares at 5% ($5,000 on a $100,000 portfolio) funded with new money, set a review date of 18 October 2027, and drop it if you trail the index.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Scout, evidence statement**

The evidence leans against a full switch. It does not say stock picking is impossible.

**What the data shows**

- **Pros mostly lose to the index.** In the S&P SPIVA report for year-end 2024, no U.S. fund category had a majority of active managers beating their benchmark over 15 years. One-year, 65% of large-cap active funds trailed the S&P 500. ([SPIVA US Year-End 2024](https://spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-us-year-end-2024)) If full-time professionals mostly lose, a part-time individual faces the same odds or worse. The same report found that only 30% of small-cap funds trailed in 2024, which is an exception.
- **A few stocks do most of the work.** Bessembinder's 2018 study covers U.S. stocks since 1926. About 4% of companies (1,092 of roughly 26,000) account for the market's net gains over Treasury bills. The rest, taken together, only matched T-bills. Only about 43% of stocks beat T-bills over their lifetimes. ([ASU summary](https://news.wpcarey.asu.edu/20180917-do-stocks-outperform-treasury-bills)) A small portfolio of 10 to 20 stocks may miss every one of the winners. An index fund owns all of them.
- **Individuals trade badly.** Barber and Odean studied 66,465 households from 1991 to 1996. The most active traders earned about 11.4% a year against 17.9% for the market. The average household earned 16.4%. ([summary](https://finance.martinsewell.com/traders)) That sample is old, and costs have since fallen. Many brokers now charge $0 commission. But the main causes were behavior and overconfidence, and those have not gone away.

**Claims that are false or outdated**

- "Stock picking is a lost cause." Too strong. Some people do beat the market. The data says most do not, and you cannot know in advance which group you are in.
- "Trading costs will eat you alive." Outdated for commissions. Spreads, taxes and mistakes still cost money.
- "Index funds are boring and low-return." The index is the benchmark that most pros fail to beat.

**What I could not confirm**

- I did not find the year-end 2025 SPIVA report. The 2024 report is the latest I verified.
- I could not confirm the exact 15-year large-cap percentage for 2024. One source says about 90%, but it was undated.
- I read summaries, not the full papers. Check the originals for exact figures.
- I know nothing about your age, tax situation, account type, or how much you hold. These change the answer, especially taxes. Selling index funds in a taxable account can trigger capital gains tax.

**If you still want to pick stocks**

The evidence supports a limited version. Keep about 90% to 95% in the index funds. Use the remaining 5% to 10% for individual shares. Then track that slice against the index for at least 5 years. If it lags, you have your answer at a small cost.

Bottom line: The evidence does not support moving your investments from index funds into individual shares. If you want to try, limit it to 5% to 10% and measure it against the index.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Do not move your investments from index funds into individual shares. Keep the index funds as your core. If you want to pick stocks, use a satellite of at most 5% to 10% of your portfolio, funded with new money, and measure it against the index for 5 years.

## Where the board agreed
- A full switch is not supported by the evidence.
- Most professional active managers trail the index over long periods (SPIVA).
- A few stocks drive most long-run gains (Bessembinder), and a small portfolio can miss them.
- Selling in a taxable account can trigger capital gains tax. Check this first.
- A written reason to buy and a written sell rule are needed for each share.
- Nobody knows your amount, age, country or account type.

## Where the board disagreed
- Satellite size: Skeptic and Scout said 5% to 10%, Operator said 5%, Advocate said up to 15%.
- Review length: Operator chose 12 months. Others said 3 to 5 years.
- Funding: Operator said both "sell proceeds" and "do not sell index funds."
- Time needed: estimates ranged from 3 to 5 hours a month up to 10 hours. None is sourced.

## Where I overrule the board
- Advocate's 15% cap: rejected. No one justified it, and no edge has been shown.
- Operator's 12-month stop rule: overruled. Operator itself says 12 months is mostly noise. Review yearly, but decide at 5 years.
- Operator's plan breaks its own $5,000 cap (8 stocks at $1,000 each is $8,000). Use a fixed dollar cap.
- Advocate's 10x example is a rare tail outcome, not a base case. Ignore it for planning.
- Skeptic said SPIVA shows individuals do worse. SPIVA measures funds, not individuals. Barber and Odean is the better support, but it covers 1991 to 1996 and heavy traders.
- Scout's 2024 small-cap line ("only 30% trailed") looks possibly reversed. I could not verify it, so I give it no weight.
- Scout's corrected Bessembinder point stands: the 4% is gains above Treasury bills, not of the whole market.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You have given no reason for wanting this. Is it for returns, control or interest? That matters. Most people who think they have a stock-picking edge do not. The index already holds the winners, and you get them without finding them. Also, a small sleeve cannot change your outcome much: a 5% sleeve that doubles adds only 5% to the whole. If it is for fun and learning, say so and treat it as a hobby budget. Also check whether you mean individual shares or just a different fund.
