## Agenda

I run a small café. Should I join a delivery app that takes 30% commission per order?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement**

The main risk is simple. A 30% commission can wipe out your profit on every delivered order. I do not know your numbers, so treat my figures as examples.

**Ranked risks**

1. **Margin collapse (very likely, high impact).** Say your café has 65% gross margin after food and packaging. A 30% fee leaves 35%. Labor, rent and card fees come out of that. Many cafés end up at break-even or a loss on each delivery order. This is the most likely reason it dies.

2. **Cannibalization (likely, high impact).** Regulars who now pay $4 for a coffee in your shop may order it through the app. You then pay $1.20 on a sale you already had. Net new customers matter. Repeat ones do not help.

3. **Price inflation backfires (likely, medium impact).** If you raise app prices 15 to 20% to cover the fee, orders drop. If you keep prices equal, you eat the fee. Some markets or apps limit price differences. Check the contract.

4. **Operational strain (likely, medium impact).** Orders arrive during your rush. Staff split attention. Drinks sit waiting for drivers and arrive cold or spilled. The customer blames you, not the driver.

5. **Reputation and refunds (medium likelihood, medium impact).** Bad ratings on the app are public. Refunds for missing items are often charged to you. I am unsure how each app handles disputes.

6. **Platform dependence (medium, high over time).** The app owns the customer data. Fees can rise later. Once 25% of your sales come through it, leaving hurts.

7. **Legal and contract (lower, variable).** Look for exclusivity clauses, minimum terms, chargeback rules and fee changes with short notice. I have not seen your contract.

**What I am unsure of**

- Your actual margin, order volume and location.
- Whether this app offers a lower tier. Some offer around 15% for pickup or for self-delivery. Ask.
- Whether delivery fits your product. Hot coffee and pastries travel poorly.

**What would have to be true for this to work**

- Your gross margin after packaging is above about 60%, or you can set app prices higher without losing orders.
- At least half of app orders come from people who would not have visited you.
- Your kitchen can handle extra orders without slowing in-store service.
- The contract has no lock-in, so you can quit within 30 days.
- A trial of 60 to 90 days shows a positive profit per order, after refunds and extra labor.

**Before you sign:** calculate profit on a typical $12 order after the fee. If it is under $1.50, decline or negotiate.

Bottom line: Do not join at a flat 30% unless a short trial, with higher app prices and no lock-in, shows each order still earns a profit.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Advocate statement: delivery app**

The 30% fee sounds painful. But the right question is not "is 30% too much?" It is "what do I earn on orders I would not get otherwise?"

**Where the upside is**

1. **New customers.** People who search an app for "coffee near me" may never have walked past your door. Each one is a chance to win a regular.
2. **Dead hours.** If your café is quiet from 2pm to 5pm, an extra 8 orders a day at an average of 15 dollars is 120 dollars a day. That is about 3,600 dollars a month in sales. After the 30% fee, you keep 2,520 dollars before ingredients and packaging.
3. **Spare capacity.** Your rent, power and staff are mostly fixed. If you have idle labor, the extra margin on those orders is mostly real.
4. **Low commitment.** Most apps let you pause or leave. A 60 to 90 day trial costs little to run.
5. **Data.** You learn which items travel well, and who orders in your area.

**The best version**

- Make a separate, shorter delivery menu. Pick items with high margin and good travel quality, like cold brew, baked goods and sandwiches.
- Raise delivery menu prices by 10 to 15%. Many cafés do this. It recovers part of the fee.
- Put a flyer in every bag with a direct order code or loyalty offer. Be careful, because some apps ban this. Check the terms.
- Turn it on only in the quiet hours at first.
- Look at the lower commission tier. Many apps offer a pickup-only or reduced-service plan at around 15%, though I have not checked current rates.

**Your advantages as a small café**

You can move fast. You can change the menu this week. You can add a handwritten note that a chain cannot. That personal touch is what turns an app customer into a regular.

**Where the upside is limited**

- Margins are thin. If your food cost is 30% and the app takes 30%, you have 40% left for labor and overhead. Without a price rise, some items will lose money.
- Apps own the customer relationship. Your repeat rate may be low.
- Delivery takes counter time. If it slows your in-person service, you can lose more than you gain.
- This will not transform a café. Expect a modest boost, perhaps 5 to 15% of sales. That range is my estimate, not data.

**What I am unsure of**

I do not know your food cost, your quiet hours, your area's app competition, or the exact fee plans in your market today. The Scout and Operator should check those.

**Suggested test:** 60 days, quiet hours only, a delivery menu with higher prices, and a clear stop rule. If the profit per order after fees and packaging is under 2 dollars, leave.

Bottom line: Yes, try it as a small, time-limited test in your slow hours with higher delivery prices, because the downside is small and you could win new regulars.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Operator's statement**

I don't have your numbers, so I used a test case. Swap in your own. Assumptions: 20 average order, 30% food cost, 0.80 packaging, 15/hour labor. Currency doesn't matter.

**The math per order**
- Sale: 20.00
- Commission at 30%: 6.00
- Food: 6.00
- Packaging: 0.80
- Left before labor: 7.20

The same 20 sale in your shop leaves about 14. So delivery earns you about half. If the order is new business, you gain 7.20. If a regular would have walked in, you lose about 6.80.

**Plan, in order**
1. Week of Oct 19 (3 hours): pull 90 days of till data. Get your real average order, food cost % and quiet hours.
2. Oct 26 (3 hours): read the contract. Check commission tiers, ad fees, refund rules, exit terms and any price parity clause. Do not sign what you haven't read.
3. Oct 26 to Nov 1 (6 hours): pick 10 to 12 items that travel well. Raise delivery prices 15 to 20%. Order packaging and a tablet.
4. Nov 2: go live. Set delivery hours only for your quiet times.
5. Jan 4, 2027: review. Keep it only if net profit per week is above zero after labor.

**Upfront cost:** about 600 to 800. That covers a tablet (150), packaging (400) and photos (0 to 200).
**Running cost:** 30% commission, packaging, and 2 to 4 hours of your time per week.

**Realistic case, orders are new business**
- Month 3: 5 orders/day. Sales 3,000/month. Contribution about 1,080. Extra labor about 450. Net about 630.
- Month 6: 8 orders/day. Sales 4,800. Contribution about 1,730. Net about 1,280 after labor.
- Month 12: 10 orders/day. Sales 6,000. Contribution about 2,160. Net about 1,700.

**If half the orders are your own regulars switching,** month 3 net drops to roughly 120. That is not worth your hours.

**What does not fit**
- If you are alone on shift, a delivery rider arriving in your rush will hurt dine-in service. Restrict hours.
- Payback on the 700 setup is about 5 to 6 weeks in the good case. In the bad case it never comes.
- Do not count on the app "bringing you customers" in month one. New listings often get few orders until reviews build.

**What I am unsure of**
- Your real margins and ticket size.
- Which app, and its actual terms in your city. Some offer 15 to 25% tiers with a lower service level.
- How many of your customers already order delivery from somewhere else.
- Whether your kitchen can take 5 to 10 extra orders a day without extra staff.

**Owner decision needed:** set a hard stop. If net after labor is under 400/month by Jan 4, 2027, you quit. Write that number down now.

Bottom line: Try it as an 8-week pilot with delivery-only prices 15 to 20% higher, limited hours, and a written quit rule, because at 30% commission you only profit on orders you would not have got anyway.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Scout, evidence statement**

I don't know your country or your margins, so I used U.S. figures. Check them against your app's own pricing page.

**What the evidence says**

- **30% is the top tier, not the only one.** [Rezku](https://rezku.com/blog/third-party-delivery-fees-in-2026-what-doordash-uber-eats-grubhub-really-cost-restaurants/) and [Duck Hub](https://www.duck-hub.com/blog/food-delivery-commission-rates) list DoorDash at 15%, 25% and 30% (Basic, Plus, Premier). Uber Eats is reported at 20%, 25% and 30% after a March 2026 change. Sources disagree on the exact Uber range.
- **Grubhub starts lower.** [Sauce](https://getsauce.com/post/grubhub-fees-for-restaurants) reports 5%, 15% and 20% plans. It adds a separate 10% if Grubhub drivers deliver.
- **The 30% is rarely the whole cost.** [Orders.co](https://orders.co/blog/delivery-app-commissions/) and others add 2% to 4% for payment processing. Promotions and ads come on top. Several sources put the all-in take at 30% to 40%.
- **The tier you choose changes visibility.** Lower tiers get a basic listing. Higher tiers buy better placement. So 15% may bring few orders.

**The maths**

- A 30% commission needs about a 43% price rise to keep the same income per order (1 ÷ 0.70). A 20% commission needs about 25%.
- Independent restaurants often net 3% to 5%, according to the vendor blogs above. One analysis says a meal earning about 15% in-house can lose about 7.6% through an app.
- Example: a $6 latte with a 30% commission leaves $4.20 before cup, milk and labour. That is workable only if your ingredient cost is under about 20% of the price.

**Claims that are false or outdated**

- "It's a flat 30%." False. It depends on the plan and the platform.
- "Apps bring new customers for free." Unproven for cafés. I found no independent study showing that.
- "Raising app prices fixes it." Partly true. Some platforms limit or penalise menu price differences, and I didn't verify the current rules for each. Check your contract.

**Weak spots in this evidence**

- Nearly every source is a vendor that sells ordering or POS software. They gain if apps look bad.
- The margin figures are for restaurants in general. I found no data specific to cafés.
- Café drinks are cheap to make but travel badly. Hot coffee and delicate pastries may suffer, and that leads to refunds.

**What would settle it**

1. Work out your food and packaging cost per top five items.
2. Get your quote in writing, including the tier, processing fee and any promotion requirements.
3. Run a 60-day trial on the lowest-commission tier with app prices raised. Count orders that are new customers versus existing regulars.

Bottom line: Don't accept 30% as a permanent plan. A trial on the lowest tier, with higher app prices and a hard check on whether the customers are new, is defensible. Joining at 30% only makes sense if your ingredient cost is under about 20% and the orders really are new.
