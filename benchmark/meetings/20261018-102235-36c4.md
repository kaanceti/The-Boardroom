# Boardroom minutes: My employer matches pension contributions up to 5%. Should I put in more than 5%

Date: 2026-10-18 10:22  
Mode: full

## Agenda

My employer matches pension contributions up to 5%. Should I put in more than 5% while I'm saving for a house deposit?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Statement from Skeptic**

I assume you are in the UK. If not, parts of this change. Please confirm.

**Short answer:** Take the full 5% match first. Beyond that, extra pension money is risky while the deposit is the goal.

**Risks of paying more than 5% into the pension, ranked**

1. **Locked money (very likely, high impact).** You cannot use pension money for a deposit. The minimum access age is 55 now and rises to 57 on 6 April 2028. Every extra pound is a pound not in the deposit.
2. **Delayed purchase (likely, high impact).** If house prices or rents rise while you save more slowly, the delay can cost more than the extra tax relief. I do not know your local market.
3. **Thin cash cushion (moderate, high impact).** Without 3 to 6 months of spending in easy-access cash, a job loss could force you to stop saving or borrow.
4. **Rule changes (moderate, moderate impact).** Pension and tax rules change often. Check the current salary sacrifice and tax relief rules before you commit.

**Risks of paying only 5%**

- You give up tax relief on the extra amount. Relief is 20% for basic rate taxpayers and 40% for higher rate taxpayers.
- If you earn just above the 40% threshold, extra pension money is more attractive. It can also bring your income back under the threshold.

**Better places for the extra money**

- A **Lifetime ISA**, if you are under 40 and the home costs £450,000 or less. The government adds 25%, up to £1,000 a year, on a £4,000 deposit. You can open one only before age 40. Using it for anything other than a first home or retirement at 60 costs a 25% penalty.
- A **cash ISA or easy-access account** for the rest. It is less generous but fully flexible.

**What I am unsure of**

- Your age, salary, tax band and target price.
- Whether your employer's match counts only your own contributions or also salary sacrifice.
- How soon you plan to buy.

**What would have to be true for more than 5% to be right**

- You are a higher rate taxpayer, or just above that line.
- You already have a cash cushion.
- Your deposit target is met or close on a firm date.
- You will not use the Lifetime ISA, or you have already filled it.
- You accept the money is untouchable until at least 55.

If most of these are false, extra pension saving is the wrong move now.

Bottom line: Contribute 5% to get the full match, then put extra savings toward the deposit, ideally in a Lifetime ISA if you qualify, and revisit extra pension contributions after you buy.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Statement from Advocate**

I'm assuming you are in the UK and have not used a Lifetime ISA yet. Please correct me if not.

**First, the part that is a clear win.** Take the full 5% match. That is free money. On a £40,000 salary, you put in £2,000 and your employer adds £2,000. That is an instant 100% return. Do not drop below 5% for any reason.

**Now the question: should you go above 5%?** The best case for yes is tax relief. Extra pension money costs you less than it looks.
- Basic-rate taxpayer: £800 from your pay becomes £1,000 in the pension. That is a 25% boost.
- Higher-rate taxpayer (over about £50,270): the same £1,000 costs you about £600. That is a 67% boost.
- Salary sacrifice, if your employer offers it, can also save National Insurance. That could take the cost of £1,000 down to roughly £720 for a basic-rate taxpayer.

**Why it is weaker for your goal.** Pension money is locked until age 55, rising to 57 from 2028. You cannot use it for a deposit. So the upside only helps your deposit indirectly.

**The better-fit tool is a Lifetime ISA.** You can put in up to £4,000 a year. The government adds 25%, which is £1,000. That is the same 25% boost as basic-rate pension relief, but you can use it for a first home up to £450,000. For a basic-rate taxpayer saving for a deposit, the LISA beats extra pension.

**A sensible split:**
1. Pension at 5% to get the full match.
2. First £4,000 a year into a LISA.
3. Anything beyond that into a cash ISA or similar, so it stays easy to reach.

**Where extra pension does win:**
- You pay higher-rate tax. The 40% relief can beat the 25% LISA bonus. Then a mixed approach makes sense.
- Your income is near £100,000. Extra contributions can reduce the loss of your personal allowance, which makes the effective relief about 60%.
- Your deposit target is already nearly covered.

**What I am unsure of:**
- Your salary, tax band and house price. These change the answer.
- Whether your employer offers salary sacrifice.
- Whether your employer's match rises if you pay in more. Some do. Check your scheme rules.
- Planned limits on National Insurance savings from salary sacrifice, which I believe start in April 2029. Scout should verify this.
- Whether your buying timeline is short. A purchase within about 2 years makes locked money a bigger cost.

**Limits on the upside:** this is a modest gain, not a big one. On an extra 3% of a £40,000 salary, the tax advantage is a few hundred pounds a year. The real value is long-term pension growth, not your house.

Bottom line: Take the full 5% match, put your next £4,000 a year into a Lifetime ISA for the deposit, and only go above 5% in the pension if you pay higher-rate tax or your deposit is already covered.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Operator's statement**

I assume you are in the UK and pay income tax. Correct me if not.

**Step 1: Take the full match. Do not skip this.**
Your employer's match is an instant 100% return. On a £40,000 salary, 5% is £2,000 a year. Your employer adds another £2,000. Nothing else you can do with that money beats it. If you are below 5% today, fix that this week.

**Step 2: Do not go above 5% yet.**
Pension money is locked until age 55, rising to 57 from 2028. You cannot use it for a deposit. Every extra pound you put in is a pound that cannot go towards the house.

**Step 3: Put the deposit money in a Lifetime ISA, if you are eligible.**
You must be aged 18 to 39 and have had the account open at least 12 months before buying. The limit is £4,000 a year. The government adds 25%, so up to £1,000 a year. The house must cost £450,000 or less. Withdrawing for anything else costs a 25% penalty, which loses you some of your own money.

**Numbers on an extra 3% (£1,200 a year gross):**
- Basic-rate taxpayer: it costs you £960 after tax relief. A LISA gives the same 25% uplift and you can reach it. LISA wins.
- Higher-rate taxpayer: it costs you £720 after relief. That is a better deal on paper, but the money is locked for decades. Only worth it if your deposit is already covered.

**Realistic numbers if you save £500 a month into a LISA (cap £4,000 a year, so £333 a month fits):**
- 3 months: about £1,000 plus £250 bonus.
- 6 months: about £2,000 plus £500 bonus.
- 12 months: £4,000 plus £1,000 bonus. That is £5,000.

The rest of your saving goes in a separate easy-access account. Check the current rate yourself.

**What does not fit:**
I do not know your salary, your deposit target or your purchase date. If you want to buy within 12 months, a LISA opened today will not meet the 12-month rule until October 2027. Check this first.

**What I am unsure about:**
- Whether the LISA rules change. The government has talked about replacing it. Check gov.uk before you commit.
- Whether your employer offers salary sacrifice. It can save National Insurance too and shifts the maths slightly.

**Actions, with dates:**
1. By 25 October: confirm your contribution is at least 5% on your payslip.
2. By 31 October: open a LISA if you are eligible.
3. By 30 November: set a standing order for the first month.
4. Review in April 2027, at the start of the new tax year.

Bottom line: Contribute exactly 5% to get the full match, then send deposit money to a Lifetime ISA, not extra pension, unless your deposit is already covered or you are a higher-rate taxpayer with spare cash.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

I assume you are in the UK. Please correct me if not. I also don't know your age, pay, or deposit target.

**Where the evidence is clear**
- Take the full match first. Missing it means turning down free money. This is the one point where I see no real dispute.
- Pension money is locked for years. The normal minimum access age is 55. I believe it rises to 57 on 6 April 2028. I did not verify that date in this session, so check it.
- You can't use pension money for a deposit. Taking it early usually means heavy tax charges.

**Common claims that are false or out of date**
- "Help to Buy ISA is an option." It has been closed to new savers since 2019. This is from my own background knowledge. I did not re-check it today.
- "Salary sacrifice will lose its tax perk soon." This is only partly true. From 6 April 2029, National Insurance relief on sacrificed pension contributions is capped at £2,000 a year. Income tax relief stays. The government's own estimate is an average extra cost of £84 in 2029/30 ([Blake Morgan](https://www.blakemorgan.co.uk/pensions-salary-sacrifice-cap-of-2000-will-be-introduced-6-april-2029/), [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/changes-to-salary-sacrifice-for-pensions-from-april-2029/changes-to-salary-sacrifice-for-pensions-from-april-2029)). This matters little for a deposit decision.

**The real alternative: Lifetime ISA (LISA)**
- You can pay in £4,000 a year if you are aged 18 to 39. The government adds 25%, which is up to £1,000 a year.
- The home must cost £450,000 or less.
- Withdrawing for anything else costs a 25% penalty ([EQ Investors](https://eqinvestors.co.uk/individuals/hub/article/lifetime-isa-reform)).
- That penalty means you get back less than you paid in. Treat it as a house-only pot.
- A reform is proposed. One source says changes could come from April 2028, and that the bonus might only be paid at purchase ([Pie.tax](https://develop.pie.tax/tax-pible/government-reform-lifetime-isa-scheme)). The £450,000 cap and the penalty looked likely to stay ([Mortgage Solutions](https://www.mortgagesolutions.co.uk/news/2025/04/24/isa-reform-on-cards-but-lifetime-isa-withdrawal-penalty-and-house-price-cap-unlikely-to-change-economic-secretary-says/)). Those sources are mostly from 2025 and mid-2026. I found no final rules.

**How the two compare, in rough terms**
- Pension above the match: basic-rate tax relief is 20%. Higher-rate payers also get 40% relief, with the extra 20% claimed back. Your employer adds nothing beyond 5%. The money is out of reach for years.
- LISA: 25% bonus on up to £4,000. It is usable for a deposit, but only for a qualifying home.
- For a basic-rate taxpayer, the two are close on the headline rate. The LISA wins on access.
- A higher-rate taxpayer gets more from extra pension. Even so, the cash is locked, and that may cost you the house.

**What I'm unsure of**
- Whether your employer offers salary sacrifice.
- Whether your target home is under £450,000.
- Whether a higher-rate taxpayer could pay into a pension, then use the tax saved for the deposit. That is a legitimate approach, but I did not research it.

Bottom line: Take the full 5% match, put deposit savings above that into a Lifetime ISA (up to £4,000 a year) if you are under 40 and the home costs £450,000 or less, and don't pay extra into your pension while the deposit is the goal.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Take the full 5% match, and do not pay more than 5% into the pension while the house deposit is the goal. Put extra savings into a Lifetime ISA (LISA) if you qualify, and keep a cash cushion alongside it. Revisit extra pension payments after you buy, or sooner if you pay 40% tax.

## Where the board agreed
- The match is the first priority. Never drop below 5%.
- Pension money is locked until age 55, rising to 57 on 6 April 2028. It cannot fund a deposit.
- For most basic-rate taxpayers, a LISA beats extra pension for a deposit. Both give a 25% boost, but the LISA is reachable.
- Higher-rate taxpayers and people near £100,000 income have a stronger case for extra pension.

## Where the board disagreed
- Scout said "don't pay extra into your pension" outright. Advocate and Skeptic allowed exceptions.
- Operator put the LISA first. Operator's review put a cash cushion first. Skeptic stressed the cushion.
- Whether the higher-rate "claim relief, then save the refund" idea works. Nobody researched it.

## Where I overrule the board
- **Operator's numbers:** "£500 a month" into a LISA is wrong. The £4,000 cap means £333 a month. The 3, 6 and 12 month figures do not fit £500.
- **Operator's 12-month claim:** it is right that a LISA opened today qualifies from October 2027. But Operator's list of dates is invented scheduling, not fact. Treat it as a suggestion.
- **"Free money" and "100% return":** this assumes your employer matches pound for pound. The owner never said so. Check your scheme.
- **Scout's absolute rule:** too strong. It conflicts with the higher-rate point.
- **Skeptic's "delay costs more than tax relief":** a guess with no numbers.
- **Scout's `develop.` source** on bonus timing is unreliable. I treat LISA reform as unconfirmed.
- **Missed by everyone:** the LISA is for first-time buyers only. It also fails if the home costs over £450,000 or you are 40 or older. Scottish tax bands differ.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You gave no age, salary, house price, location or purchase date. Those decide this answer, and I am guessing without them. Also, a cash cushion comes before either pot, and I do not know if you have one.
