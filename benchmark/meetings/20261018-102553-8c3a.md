## Agenda

I want to post on LinkedIn about my side projects. Could it hurt me at my current job?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement**

Yes, it could hurt you. Most of the risk is in your paperwork, not on LinkedIn. I don't know your country, your employer, your contract or what the projects are. That is the biggest gap in my view.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Your employer may claim the project.** Many contracts say the company owns anything "related to the business." Some say anything made during employment. Public posting creates a dated record of what you built and when. Likelihood: moderate. Impact: severe, because you could lose the project or face a legal fight. In California, Labor Code 2870 limits these claims. Many other places give you less protection. I am not certain how your location handles this.

2. **You may have broken an outside-work rule.** Many employers require you to disclose or get approval for side work. Posting publicly ends any chance of quietly asking later. Likelihood: moderate. Impact: a warning at best, dismissal at worst.

3. **The project may look like competition.** If it is in the same field as your employer, even loosely, a non-compete or non-solicit clause may apply. Those clauses often run 6 to 12 months after you leave, but some apply during employment too. Likelihood: low to moderate. Impact: high.

4. **You may leak confidential information.** Screenshots, metrics, client names, tool choices or lessons learned can reveal employer details by accident. Likelihood: moderate, since it takes one careless sentence. Impact: high if it is traced back to you.

5. **The evidence may show you used work time or gear.** Post timestamps between 9:00 and 17:00 on weekdays invite questions. So does any use of a work laptop or work accounts. Likelihood: moderate. Impact: moderate, but it weakens you in any ownership dispute.

6. **Your manager may read it badly.** They might think you are distracted or planning to leave. That can affect raises, projects or who gets cut in a layoff. Likelihood: moderate. Impact: low to moderate, and hard to prove either way.

7. **Your profile may blur the line between you and the employer.** If your headline links your employer's name to the project, it can look like they endorse it. Likelihood: low. Impact: low, and easy to fix.

**Most likely way this goes wrong:** the project sits near your employer's field, your contract has a broad ownership clause, and you never checked it before posting.

**What would have to be true for this to be safe**

- Your contract has no broad claim over your side work, or your projects clearly fall outside it.
- You built everything on your own time, devices and accounts.
- The projects do not overlap with your employer's products, clients or market.
- Nothing you post contains employer information.
- You have disclosed the work, or got written approval, if your policy requires it.
- Your profile makes clear these are personal projects.

Before you post, read three sections of your contract and handbook: invention assignment, outside activities, and confidentiality. If any is unclear, a one-hour consult with an employment lawyer is cheap next to losing a project or a job.

Bottom line: It can hurt you, mainly through ownership clauses, outside-work rules and accidental leaks, so check your contract and keep the projects clearly separate from your employer before you post.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Advocate's statement**

I see real upside here, and the downside is mostly manageable.

**The upside**
- Side projects are proof of skill. A resume says you can build things. A post with a demo shows it.
- Inbound interest is the big prize. Recruiters, clients, and peers can find you without you applying. LinkedIn says it has over 1 billion members, though I have not checked the current figure.
- You have a job, so you have leverage. You can be choosy about what comes in.
- It can help inside your company too. Managers like people who learn on their own, especially if the projects touch skills the team uses.
- The habit compounds. Six posts over 3 months builds a visible track record. One post does not.

**Your advantages**
- You already have real work to show. Most people who want to post have nothing to post about.
- You do not need this to pay off fast. That lets you play a long game.

**Limits on the upside**
- Most posts reach few people. A small network might give you a few hundred views per post. Do not expect job offers from one post.
- Results depend on your field. Developers, designers, and founders gain more than people in tightly regulated or conservative industries.
- It will not replace a strong network or a good portfolio site. It supports them.

**Could it hurt?**
Yes, but mainly in a few specific ways. Skeptic will go deeper. Here is how I would shrink them:
1. Read your employment contract. Look for clauses on outside work, conflicts of interest, and who owns what you create. I have not seen yours, so I do not know what it says.
2. Build on your own time and your own equipment. Never use employer code, data, or tools.
3. Do not post projects that compete with your employer's business.
4. Never mention clients, internal numbers, or colleagues.
5. Consider telling your manager before you post. A short heads-up often turns a possible worry into a non-issue. It is not always needed, and it depends on your workplace culture.

**What I am unsure of**
- Your contract terms.
- Your employer's social media policy.
- How your manager feels about outside projects.
- Whether any project is close to your day job.

**Best version**
Post once every 2 weeks for 3 months. Each post shows one thing you built, what you learned, and one result with a number, such as "cut load time from 4 seconds to 1.2 seconds." Keep the tone about learning, not about leaving. Then check what happened: profile views, messages, and connection requests from people you want to meet.

Bottom line: Posting is more likely to help than hurt, as long as you first check your contract, keep the work separate from your employer, and avoid anything that competes with it. Expect slow, modest gains.

- Vague plans die. Put the Oct 25 deadline in your calendar today.

Bottom line: Posting is low cost and mostly safe if you check your contract by Oct 25, keep employer details out, and post only about clearly separate projects.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

Yes, it can hurt you. But the risk is narrow and you can check it in advance.

**Risk 1: your contract, not LinkedIn.**
- Many employees sign an invention assignment clause. It can claim work you do on your own time if it relates to the employer's business. [Promise Legal](https://blog.promise.legal/moonlighting-ip-assignment-clause-austin-semiconductor/) describes a 2006 federal case where a social-network slideshow side project was held to be covered.
- California is friendlier. [Labor Code 2870](https://california.public.law/codes/labor_code_section_2870) protects inventions made on your own time, with no employer equipment or trade secrets. That protection does not apply if the work relates to the employer's business or R&D.
- Texas has no such floor, per the same Promise Legal post.
- A public post is dated proof of what you built and when. That helps you if it is clearly unrelated. It hurts you if it is close to your employer's field.

**Risk 2: moonlighting or approval rules.**
- Some employers require prior approval for outside work. A post can make a breach visible. I found only anecdotes on how approval affects ownership. See the [Game Developer piece](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/-i-have-been-hunted-down-by-hr-reps-lawyers-and-comms-people-developers-discuss-the-pain-and-prevalence-of-side-work-clauses). That is not settled law.

**Risk 3: perception.**
- In a 2018 CareerBuilder survey, 48% of employers said they monitor current employees' social media. 34% had found content that led them to reprimand or fire someone. See [HR Reporter](https://www.hrreporter.com/chrr-plus/employment-law/9-in-10-employers-will-fire-employees-for-inappropriate-posts/372824) and [HCA Mag](https://www.hcamag.com/ca/specialization/recruitment/9-in-10-employers-will-fire-employee-for-inappropriate-posts/432856).
- A 2023 Harris Poll found 88% of US hiring managers would consider firing someone over posts. That measures willingness, not actual firings. See [Bend Chamber](https://bendchamber.org/business/88-of-hiring-managers-would-consider-firing-workers-for-content-in-personal-social-media-posts).
- These surveys are about inappropriate content. Nothing I found says ordinary project posts get people fired.

**Outdated or false claims**
- "Own time and own laptop means it's yours." False in many states if the work relates to the employer's business.
- "California law always protects me." Overstated. It has a relatedness exception.
- "Employers fire people for side project posts." I found no data on that. The numbers above are about inappropriate posts.

**What I'm unsure of**
- Your state, employer, industry and contract. These decide almost everything.
- A secondary site says California requires written notice of 2870 rights in the clause. I did not verify it against the statute.
- The sources are mostly law firm blogs and forums, not court rulings. This is not legal advice.

**Checks before you post**
1. Read your offer letter, invention assignment and outside-activity policy.
2. Compare your project's subject to your employer's products and clients.
3. Post nothing confidential, and nothing built on work time or work equipment.
4. If the overlap is unclear, ask a lawyer or get written approval first.

Bottom line: Posting is usually low risk if your project is unrelated to your employer's business and you used none of their resources, but read your contract first, because the real danger is an ownership or approval clause, not the post.

Yes, posting could hurt you, but mainly through your contract, not through LinkedIn. Read your contract first. Post only about projects that are clearly unrelated to your employer, starting after the check is done. If any clause is broad or unclear, hold the post and get legal advice.
