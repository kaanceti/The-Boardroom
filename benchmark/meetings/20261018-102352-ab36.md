# Boardroom minutes: I'm one year into a stable corporate job. A startup has offered me £4,000 more. 

Date: 2026-10-18 10:23  
Mode: full

## Agenda

I'm one year into a stable corporate job. A startup has offered me £4,000 more. Should I move?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement**

£4,000 is a weak reason to move. I would not take this offer on salary alone.

**The numbers first.** £4,000 gross is roughly £2,800 to £3,000 a year after tax and National Insurance for a basic or higher rate payer. That is about £235 to £250 a month. I am unsure of your exact tax band, so treat this as a range.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **The startup runs out of money or cuts staff.** Most startups fail or restructure. Likelihood is moderate to high. Impact is high. If you lose the job in year one, you lose far more than £4,000. Job searches often take 3 to 6 months. That is £15,000 or more in lost pay.

2. **The pay gap is not real.** Check what you give up. Pension match, bonus, private health, sick pay, holiday days, and training budget all count. A corporate employer may add 5% to 10% of salary in benefits. That can wipe out the £4,000 gap. Likelihood is high. Impact is moderate.

3. **Equity is worth nothing.** If options are part of the pitch, assume zero until proven. Ask for the strike price, vesting schedule, and latest valuation. Likelihood of a low payout is high.

4. **Short tenure on your CV.** You are one year in. A second short stay in a row starts to look like a pattern. Likelihood is low to moderate. Impact is moderate.

5. **Lost employment rights.** In the UK, many protections build with service. Unfair dismissal protection has needed two years of service. Rules have been changing, so I am unsure of the current position. Check it. A move resets your clock.

6. **Role creep and long hours.** Startups often ask for more scope and more hours. If hours rise 10%, your hourly pay may fall.

7. **Weak management or unclear role.** Likelihood is moderate. It is hard to see from outside.

**Hidden costs.** Possible loss of unvested pension contributions, a clawback of any bonus or relocation payment, and a notice period gap with no pay.

**What I do not know.** Your career goals, your savings, your dependants, the startup's funding stage, and what the corporate job offers in growth.

**What would have to be true for this to work**
- The startup has at least 18 to 24 months of cash runway, confirmed in writing or by a funding announcement.
- Total package, not base pay, is higher after benefits.
- The role gives you skills or a title you cannot get in your current job within 2 years.
- You hold 6 months of expenses in savings.
- Your contract has a fair notice period and clear terms.

**Questions to ask the startup:** last funding date and amount, monthly burn, runway, headcount change in the past year, and why the role is open.

Bottom line: Do not move for £4,000. Move only if the startup has proven runway and the role clearly builds your career faster than staying.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Advocate's statement**

I'll be honest first. £4,000 alone is a weak reason to move. It is the smallest part of the upside.

**The money.** £4,000 gross is about £2,900 to £3,100 take-home a year in the UK, depending on your tax band and pension. That is roughly £250 a month. Nice, but not life-changing. If you lose a pension match or bonus at your current job, it could shrink to near zero.

**Where the real upside is.** A startup can give you things a stable firm often won't:

- **Scope.** You may do work in year two that would take five years at a big employer.
- **Speed of learning.** One year in, you have a stable base. That is a good time to take a bigger swing.
- **Equity.** If they offer options, ask for the number of shares, the total shares outstanding, the strike price and the vesting schedule. Most options end up worth little. A few pay off hugely. Treat it as a lottery ticket, not salary.
- **Title and visibility.** Early staff often become heads of teams if the company grows. That can lift your pay by £15,000 to £30,000 within two to three years. That is my estimate, not a certain number.
- **Your advantage.** You are early in your career with a clean record and a year of solid experience. If you are single or have low fixed costs, you can afford the risk better than most people will ever be able to.

**The best version of this move.** The startup is funded with at least 18 months of runway. You get a real role with clear growth. You get equity in writing. You learn skills your current job can't teach. If it fails, you come back to the market with more varied experience and a better story, and a year-two hire with startup experience is easy to place.

**Where the upside is limited.** If the pay is the main draw and the equity is vague, you are taking extra risk for £250 a month. Also, leaving after only one year is not a problem once. Doing it repeatedly starts to look odd.

**What I don't know.**
- Your current benefits, pension and bonus.
- The startup's funding stage and runway.
- Whether the role is a step up or a sideways move.
- Your personal costs, such as a mortgage or dependants.

**Before deciding, ask the startup:** when was the last funding round, how many months of cash remain, and what is the equity offer in writing.

Bottom line: The £4,000 is not the reason to go, but if the startup has 18 months or more of runway and offers real growth plus written equity, this is a good time in your career to take the swing.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

**The money is smaller than it looks.** I don't know your current salary, so I'm using £4,000 gross as the example.
- At the 40% higher-rate band, you keep about £2,400 a year. That's about £200 a month.
- At the 20% basic rate plus 8% National Insurance, you keep about £2,880. That's about £240 a month.
- You may lose pension match, bonus, or private health cover. Any one of these could wipe out the gain. I haven't seen the offer, so I can't check.

**Common claims that are false or outdated**
- "You need two years in a job before you have any protection." This is outdated. Under the Employment Rights Act 2025, the unfair dismissal qualifying period drops from two years to six months for dismissals on or after 1 January 2027. See [BCLP](https://www.bclplaw.com/en-US/events-insights-news/employment-rights-act-2025-key-changes-to-unfair-dismissal-from-1-january-2027.html) and [NatWest Mentor](https://www.natwestmentor.co.uk/news/unfair-dismissal-changes-2027).
  - This helps you if you move. You'd have protection about six months into the new job, not after two years.
  - The government first planned day-one protection, but the final Act did not include it.
  - I did not confirm whether the two-year rule for statutory redundancy pay is changing. Check it before you rely on it.
- "Startups fail, so don't go." This is too blunt. Only about 38% of UK businesses survive five years, according to a [secondary analysis](https://whito.co.uk/research/where-uk-businesses-close-fastest/) of the 2019 cohort. Older ONS-based figures are 41% to 43% ([ONS 2013](https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/business/activitysizeandlocation/bulletins/businessdemography/2014-11-27)).
  - Those figures cover all new businesses, including sole traders. They are not funded, staffed startups, so they overstate the risk for a company that has already hired people.
  - I found no good survival figure for funded startups. Ask this one directly.
- "One year is too soon to leave." I found no hard evidence for this. Leaving after one year is common and employers rarely treat it as a problem. That is my judgement, not a sourced finding.

**What the evidence cannot tell you**
- Whether this particular startup is healthy. That is the main question.
- Whether you would be happier or learn more there.

**Facts to get before deciding**
1. Runway in months. Ask for it plainly. Under 18 months is a warning sign.
2. When it last raised money, how much, and from whom. You can check Companies House filings for free.
3. Whether any equity is on offer. If so, ask about the vesting schedule and the strike price. Most startup equity ends up worth nothing.
4. The notice period and redundancy terms in the new contract, compared with your current ones.
5. Your current employer's pension match, bonus, and benefits, set against the offer.

**Reading the evidence**
Roughly £200 to £240 a month extra does not justify much risk on its own. If the startup's finances check out and the role gives you a clear step up in scope or skills, the move can make sense. If the only reason is the pay rise, the evidence favours staying.

Bottom line: £4,000 gross is only about £2,400 to £2,900 a year after tax, so don't move for the money alone. Move only if the startup shows 18 or more months of runway and the role clearly develops you.

## Chair's resolution

## Decision
Do not move for £4,000 alone. Only move if the startup proves at least 18 months of cash runway in writing and the role gives you scope or skills you cannot get where you are. Otherwise stay.

## Where the board agreed
- £4,000 gross is a weak reason. Take-home is about £2,320 to £2,880 a year, or roughly £190 to £240 a month.
- Compare the whole package: pension match, bonus, health cover, holiday, sick pay, notice.
- Treat equity as worth zero until you see share count, strike price and vesting in writing.
- Runway is the key unknown.

## Where the board disagreed
- Runway floor: Operator said 12 months, the others 18. Nobody had evidence.
- Risk of failure: Skeptic called it moderate to high. Scout said the survival data covers all new firms, not funded startups. Scout is fairer.
- Upside: Advocate leaned on a £15,000 to £30,000 uplift. Others called it a guess.
- Employment law: Skeptic and Operator used the old two-year rule. Scout cited a change to six months.

## Where I overrule the board
- Advocate's take-home of £2,900 to £3,100 is too high. Use £2,320 (higher rate) to £2,880 (basic rate). Scout's 40% figure of £2,400 also ignored the 2% NI.
- Advocate's £15,000 to £30,000 uplift and "easy to place" are unsupported. I give them no weight.
- Advocate's assumption that you are single with low costs is invented.
- Skeptic's "unvested pension contributions" is wrong. Employer pension contributions in the UK normally belong to you at once. Scout is right.
- Law: Scout's six-month change is sourced, but I have not verified it. Skeptic is right that you would have no unfair dismissal protection for about six months after moving. Staying keeps your one year of service. Statutory redundancy pay still needs two years, as far as I know. Check gov.uk.
- Operator's January 2027 start date and £40,000 salary are assumptions. I treat them as illustrations.
- I set the runway floor at 18 months, because fundraising can take about 6 months. It is a rule of thumb, not proven.

## Honest criticism for the owner
You gave the board only a salary number. That suggests you are weighing money, and the money is small. You have not said whether the role is bigger, what your current job offers for growth, or what savings you hold. Without those, you cannot decide well. Also, one year in is not a problem, but a second short stay would start to look like a pattern.

## Next steps
1. By 25 October, ask the startup in writing: last raise date and amount, months of cash, equity terms, pension, probation and notice. Check their Companies House filings yourself. If they dodge, walk away.
2. By 1 November, compare the written offer line by line with your current package. Confirm you hold 3 to 6 months of expenses in savings. If not, do not move.
3. By 15 November, ask your current employer about a raise or a bigger role. Do it carefully, as it can mark you as a leaver. Then decide.
