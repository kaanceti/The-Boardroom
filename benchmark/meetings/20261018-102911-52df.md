# Boardroom minutes: Should I buy a used car outright for £6,000 or lease a new one for £250 a month?

## Agenda

Should I buy a used car outright for £6,000 or lease a new one for £250 a month?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement: used car at £6,000 vs new lease at £250 a month**

I am comparing two options that are not like for like. I will flag what I do not know.

**What the £250 hides.** A lease headline is rarely the full cost. Check for these:
- An initial rental, often 3 to 9 times the monthly payment. That is £750 to £2,250 up front, and sometimes more.
- A mileage cap, commonly 6,000 to 10,000 miles a year. Excess charges run about 5p to 15p a mile. Going 3,000 miles over at 10p costs £300 a year.
- Damage charges at hand-back. Scratches and worn tyres get billed.
- Insurance, tax and servicing, which are often not included.
- Early exit penalties. Ending a lease early can cost most of the remaining payments.

A 3-year lease at £250 with a £1,500 initial rental costs about £10,500 total. At the end you own nothing.

**What the £6,000 hides.**
- Repairs. A used car around £6,000 is often 6 to 9 years old. Budget £500 to £1,200 a year, and a single failure (clutch, gearbox, timing chain) can cost £800 to £2,000.
- A bad purchase. Hidden finance, clocked mileage or accident history. A history check costs about £10 to £20. Do it.
- Tying up £6,000 of cash. If it is your whole buffer, one bill can sink you.
- Depreciation. Not zero, but usually £600 to £1,000 a year at that price. That is far lower than a new car loses.

**Rough 3-year comparison (my estimates, not quotes):**
- Used: £6,000 minus resale of about £3,500 = £2,500 lost value. Add £2,000 to £3,000 repairs and servicing. Total about £4,500 to £5,500.
- Lease: about £10,500, with servicing and warranty cover often lower, plus risk of excess charges.

On these assumptions, buying costs roughly half as much. The lease buys certainty and a warranty. It does not buy savings.

**Ranked risks**
1. Lease: lock-in and surprise fees. High likelihood, medium impact.
2. Used: a big repair in year one or two. Medium likelihood, medium impact.
3. Used: buying a dud with no history check. Low to medium likelihood, high impact.
4. Buying: draining your emergency cash. Depends on your savings, which I do not know.
5. Lease: your job or mileage changes mid-term. Medium likelihood, high impact.

**What I am unsure of.** Your annual mileage, your savings, your credit score, the lease terms, and the exact used car. Also whether lease prices include VAT. Personal leases usually do, but check.

**What would have to be true for the lease to win:** your mileage is low and predictable, you want zero repair risk, you have no spare £6,000, and the quote has a low initial rental and includes maintenance.

**What would have to be true for buying to win:** you have the £6,000 and still keep at least 3 months of expenses in cash, you buy a well-documented car with a full history check and an independent inspection (about £100 to £200), and you hold it for 4 or more years.

Bottom line: Buy the used car, but only if it leaves you a cash buffer and passes a history check and inspection; otherwise neither option is safe.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Advocate's statement**

I will make the case for buying the used car. It has the bigger upside. I will also say where the lease wins.

**The money**

A £250 lease over 36 months costs £9,000. Most deals also ask for an upfront payment of about 3 to 9 months of rent. That is £750 to £2,250. So the real cost is roughly £9,750 to £11,250. At the end you own nothing.

The £6,000 used car is different. After three years it will probably still sell for £3,000 to £4,000. That is my estimate, not a quote. It depends on the model and mileage. So your real depreciation cost is about £2,000 to £3,000.

Add repairs. A sensible budget is £500 to £1,000 a year. Over three years that is £1,500 to £3,000. You will also pay for the MOT, which is capped at £54.85 for a car.

Total for buying: about £3,500 to £6,000 over three years. Total for leasing: about £9,750 to £11,250. The gap is around £4,000 to £6,000 in your favour.

**Other advantages of buying**

- No mileage cap. Leases often allow 6,000 to 10,000 miles a year. Excess miles cost roughly 6p to 15p each.
- No end-of-lease damage bills.
- You can sell at any time. A lease usually locks you in, and early exit fees can be most of the remaining payments.
- Insurance is often cheaper on an older, lower-value car.
- Once the car is yours, running costs fall. Year four onward could cost only fuel, insurance, tax and repairs.

**Where the lease wins**

- It needs almost no cash today. You keep £6,000 free.
- A new car comes with a warranty, usually 3 years. Repair bills are far less likely.
- Costs are predictable. A used car can throw up one £1,500 bill (gearbox, clutch) in a bad month.
- Some leases include road tax.

**Where the upside is limited**

Buying is not free money. If the used car has a major fault, the gap can shrink to near zero. Tying up £6,000 also costs you. At about 4% savings interest, that is roughly £240 a year in lost interest. That is small next to the £4,000 to £6,000 gap.

**What I am unsure of**

- Your yearly mileage. High mileage hurts the lease badly.
- Whether £6,000 is spare cash or your whole safety net.
- The exact car and its history. Check its service record and run a history check before paying.
- Real lease terms. Ask for the full cost, including upfront payment, fees and excess mileage rate.

**Bottom line:** Buy the used car if the £6,000 is not your emergency fund. Expect to save roughly £4,000 to £6,000 over three years, and lease only if you value a warranty and zero cash outlay above that saving.

The lease is the right call only if you need zero repair risk, cannot spare the cash, or want a new car regardless of cost. Pay the premium knowingly. Don't call it a saving.

Bottom line: Buy the used car, but only if £6,000 leaves you at least three months of living costs in savings and you keep £1,000 aside for repairs. Otherwise the lease is the fallback, at about £4,500 more over three years.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

The two options are not like for like, so the headline numbers mislead.

**What £250 a month really costs**
- The usual personal lease runs 3 years. At £250 a month that is £9,000 over 36 months.
- Most deals also ask for an upfront "initial rental". On a common 9+35 profile, one [broker guide](https://www.firstvehicleleasing.co.uk/guides/costs-and-payments/how-to-budget-for-a-lease) says this adds roughly a quarter to the advertised monthly figure once spread out. Check whether your £250 includes it. The cost could be nearer £300 a month in effect, or about £10,800 in total.
- At the end you own nothing. You hand the car back.
- Insurance, fuel and charging are extra. Mileage caps apply, and excess-mileage and damage charges can hurt.
- Advertised deals vary. [Parkers](https://www.parkers.co.uk/car-news/28-april-2026/) lists examples like £280 a month for a Cupra Formentor. A [2026 EV guide](https://www.bestchargers.co.uk/electric-cars/electric-car-leasing-uk/) gives £200 to £400 a month as typical for compact and mid-range EVs. Your £250 is plausible but not unusually cheap.

**What £6,000 really costs**
- Depreciation is steepest early. [WeBuyAnyCar's calculator](https://www.webuyanycar.com/car-valuation/car-value-after-3-years) puts cumulative loss at 42% by year 3 and 60% by year 5. That averages about 12% a year.
- A used car already past its worst drop might lose around 10% of £6,000 a year, so about £600 a year. That is roughly £50 a month. This is my estimate from the general figures, not a quoted number.
- You must budget for repairs, MOT and servicing. A £6,000 car is often 7 to 10 years old. Repair bills are the main risk.
- Insurance, fuel and tax are paid either way. Lease prices usually include road tax. Tax on an older car is not included.

**Common claims that are weak**
- "Leasing is cheaper because the monthly payment is lower." It is only lower because you never build equity.
- "Used cars are always the cheaper choice." Not if the car needs big repairs. Cheap used cars have no warranty.
- "Depreciation is a steady 15% a year." It is front-loaded, and it varies by model. The [AA found](https://www.theaa.com/car-leasing/leasing-guides/crash-landing) a 41% to 46% fall between years 1 and 3 for popular models.

**Rough three-year comparison**
- Lease: about £9,000 to £10,800, with no asset left.
- Buy: £6,000 minus resale value of perhaps £4,000 to £4,500, so £1,500 to £2,000 lost. Add maybe £500 to £1,500 a year for repairs and servicing. That is a total of roughly £3,000 to £6,500.
- On these numbers, buying costs less. It also ties up £6,000 in cash upfront.

**What I'm unsure of**
- I found no authoritative UK average lease price. Sources are mostly brokers with a sales interest.
- I don't know the car, your mileage, your credit, or whether the £6,000 is savings or borrowed. Financing it would change the result.
- I could not check real resale values for a specific model.

Bottom line: On the evidence, buying the used car is usually cheaper over three years, probably by £3,000 to £6,000, unless the £250 lease is fully inclusive of upfront costs and the used car is unreliable.
