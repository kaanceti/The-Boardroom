# Boardroom minutes: I'm 24 and have £5,000 in savings. Should I pay off my £4,000 graduate overdraft

Date: 2026-10-18 10:18  
Mode: full

## Agenda

I'm 24 and have £5,000 in savings. Should I pay off my £4,000 graduate overdraft now or invest the money?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement**

**The short case:** Investing this money is the riskier choice. Paying the overdraft is the safer one. But the right answer depends on one number I don't have: your overdraft interest rate after any 0% period ends.

**Risks of investing the £5,000 while holding a £4,000 overdraft, ranked by likelihood and impact:**

1. **Rate cliff (likely, high impact).** Graduate overdrafts are often 0% for a set time, then switch to a standard rate. Many UK banks charge roughly 35% to 40% EAR on arranged overdrafts. I'm unsure of your bank's exact terms. No sensible investment reliably earns that. Even 40% of £4,000 is about £1,600 a year in interest.
2. **Market loss (likely, medium impact).** A global equity fund has historically returned maybe 5% to 8% a year on average. That is not guaranteed. A 20% fall in one year is normal. On £5,000 that is £1,000.
3. **Overdraft pulled (possible, high impact).** Overdrafts are repayable on demand. The bank can cut your limit. If your money is tied up in investments, you could be forced to sell at a bad time.
4. **No cash buffer (likely, medium impact).** If you pay off the whole £4,000, you have £1,000 left. One emergency, such as a laptop, a rent deposit or a job gap, could put you back in the overdraft.
5. **Behaviour (possible, medium impact).** People who invest while in debt often panic-sell after a dip. That locks in the loss.
6. **Lifetime ISA trap (possible, medium impact).** At 24 you may be tempted by the 25% government bonus, up to £1,000 a year. But early withdrawal for non-qualifying reasons carries a 25% charge. That takes back the bonus and slightly more. Only use it if you are sure about a first home or retirement at 60.

**What would have to be true for investing to win:**
- Your overdraft stays at 0% for the whole period you hold the investment.
- You keep at least 3 months of living costs in cash. That is probably £2,000 to £3,000 for you, though I don't know your spending.
- You will not need the invested money for 5 years or more.
- You have a firm plan to clear the overdraft before the 0% ends.

If all four hold, a middle path is reasonable. Keep the cash in an easy-access savings account and clear the overdraft before the 0% ends.

**What I don't know:** your interest rate, your 0% end date, your monthly spending, your job security, and whether you have a student loan or other debts. Check the first two today in your banking app.

**Bottom line:** Unless your overdraft is 0% for well over a year, pay off the £4,000 now, keep the remaining £1,000 as a buffer, and start investing from monthly income afterwards.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Advocate's statement**

The answer turns on one number I don't have: your overdraft interest rate. Please check it today.

**Case 1: the overdraft costs you interest.**
Many standard UK arranged overdrafts charge around 30% to 40% EAR. Clearing £4,000 at 35% saves about £1,400 a year. That is a guaranteed, tax-free return. No investment can match it. Pay it off now. This is the easy win.

**Case 2: it is still 0%.**
Many graduate accounts charge 0% for one to three years. Then the rate can jump, or the limit can shrink. In that case you have more options, and this is where the upside is.

- **Employer pension match.** If your employer matches contributions, that is often an instant 100% return on what you put in. Take it first.
- **Lifetime ISA.** You are 24, so you can open one. Put in £4,000 and the government adds a 25% bonus, which is £1,000. The catch is a 25% withdrawal charge if you take the money out for anything except a first home or after age 60. You would lose about 6% of your own money on a bad exit. Only use it if buying a first home is a real goal.
- **Stocks and shares ISA.** Long-run global shares have returned roughly 5% to 7% a year after inflation. On £4,000 that is about £200 to £280 in a typical year. Some years will be negative.

**What I like about your position.**
You are 24 and you have £5,000 saved. That is a real head start. Time is your biggest advantage. £4,000 invested at 6% for 35 years grows to roughly £30,000, though that is an estimate and not a promise.

**Where the upside is limited.**
Investing £4,000 in a normal year earns a few hundred pounds. That is small next to the 35% case. It is also uncertain. Paying off the overdraft is smaller in the 0% case, but it is certain, and it removes the risk of a rate jump.

**A middle path.**
Pay off most of the overdraft. Keep about £1,000 as an emergency fund. If you clear everything and have no buffer, you may fall back into the overdraft the next month.

**What I'm unsure of.**
- Your actual rate and when any 0% period ends.
- Your income and whether your job has a pension match.
- Current Lifetime ISA rules. They have been under government review, so check gov.uk.
- Whether you plan to buy a home in the next 10 years.

Bottom line: If the overdraft charges interest, clear it now and keep about £1,000 spare. If it is truly 0% until a known date, take any pension match first, then consider investing, but only with money you can leave alone for 5+ years and only if you can clear the overdraft before the 0% ends.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

The answer turns on one fact: is your overdraft interest-free right now? I don't know, and you haven't said.

**What the evidence says**

- Graduate overdrafts are usually 0% for 1 to 3 years after you finish. Some banks shrink the free limit each year. [NatWest](https://www.natwest.com/mobile-app-content/responsive/current-accounts/etb/manage/graduate-account.html) steps down from £3,250 to £2,250 to £1,250. Then it stops being interest-free in year four. [Nationwide](https://www.nationwide.co.uk/current-accounts/flexstudent/student-guides/how-student-overdrafts-work) also tapers its limit.
- Borrowing above the free limit costs about 39% to 40% EAR. NatWest's example is 39.49%. [Santander's graduate account](https://www.santander.co.uk/assets/s3fs-public/AD_documents/Santander_Edge_Graduate_Current_Account_Key_Facts_Document_BANK0549SEP26PUBCDT.pdf) quotes 39.94% above £1,000 in year two.
- The Bank of England base rate is 3.75%, per [DepositScout](https://depositscout.com/guides/best-uk-savings-rates-october-2026) (6 Oct 2026). Top easy-access rates are around 4.5% to 5% AER. But some of that is a short bonus. Starling's 5% drops to 2.5% after 6 months, per [Money.co.uk](https://money.co.uk/savings-accounts/the-top-easy-access-savings-accounts).

**What this means in pounds**

- If your overdraft is 0% and the limit isn't about to fall, keep the cash in savings. At roughly 4.5%, £4,000 earns about £180 a year. This is only worth it if you can't be tempted to spend it.
- If part of the overdraft will soon cost 39%, pay that part off first. On £4,000, 39% is about £1,560 a year. No investment reliably beats that.
- Stocks are not a good use of this money. Long-run returns are uncertain. They can fall 20% or more in a year. A guaranteed 39% saving beats an uncertain gain.

**Claims that are false or outdated**

- "Overdrafts are always cheap debt": false once the 0% period ends.
- "Investing beats debt repayment": only true against low-rate debt, and even then it's a gamble.
- "5% savings rates": often bonus-inclusive and temporary.

**What I'm unsure of**

- Your bank, your 0% end date and your limit schedule. These decide everything.
- Current rates. The sources I found were dated differently and some are comparison sites. Check each provider directly.
- Whether you have an emergency fund beyond this £5,000.

**What to do**

1. Find your bank's graduate terms and the date the free limit drops.
2. Work out how much of the £4,000 will be charged interest in the next 12 months. Repay that amount.
3. Keep the rest, at least 3 months of spending, in easy-access savings.
4. Consider investing only after that, and only money you won't need for 5+ years.

Bottom line: Don't invest this money yet. Repay any part of the overdraft that will soon cost about 39%. If it stays 0%, hold the cash in savings at around 4.5% and clear the overdraft before the free period ends.
