# Boardroom minutes: A friend wants to start a business with me, split 50/50. Should I agree?

Date: 2026-10-18 10:30  
Mode: full

## Agenda

A friend wants to start a business with me, split 50/50. Should I agree?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement: a 50/50 business with a friend**

I do not have the key facts. I don't know the business, the money involved, or how long you have known this friend. So I can only rank the risks that usually kill this kind of deal.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Deadlock (high likelihood, high impact).** At 50/50, neither of you can break a tie. A fight over pricing, hiring or direction can freeze the company. Many partnerships that split evenly end this way.

2. **Unequal effort (high likelihood, high impact).** An even split assumes even work, money and risk. Within 12 months, one person is usually carrying more. Resentment follows, and it is hard to fix once the shares are fixed.

3. **No exit plan (medium likelihood, very high impact).** If one of you wants out, quits, divorces, gets sick or dies, what happens to that 50%? Without a written buy-sell clause, you may end up in business with a stranger, a spouse or an heir.

4. **Personal liability (medium likelihood, high impact).** If you skip forming an LLC or similar company, you may be personally liable for the business's debts. In a general partnership, you can also be liable for your partner's actions. Rules vary by country and state, and I am not a lawyer.

5. **Friendship damage (medium to high likelihood, high impact).** Money disputes between friends tend to end both the business and the friendship. You lose twice.

6. **Weak market assumptions (unknown likelihood).** A friend's enthusiasm is not evidence of demand. I have seen no customers, prices or costs.

7. **Hidden costs (medium likelihood).** Legal setup, accounting, insurance and taxes often cost USD 2,000 to 10,000 in year one, even for a small firm. Treat that as a rough estimate. Also count your own unpaid time.

**Most likely way it dies:** a dispute over effort or money, with no tie-break rule and no exit clause. The market is a close second.

**What would have to be true for a yes**

- You can state in one sentence what the business sells, to whom, and why they will pay.
- The friend brings a skill or asset you lack, not just company.
- Roles, hours and capital from each side are written down.
- A lawyer drafts an operating agreement with a buy-sell clause, a deadlock method (such as mediation, then a named arbitrator) and a vesting schedule, for example 4 years with a 1-year cliff.
- You can lose everything you put in and still pay your bills.
- You have seen how this friend behaves under money stress, not only socially.

**Ask yourself:** why 50/50? If the answer is "it feels fair" and not "we contribute equally," that is a warning sign. A 51/49 split, or a split tied to milestones, can remove deadlock.

I am unsure whether the friend has proposed terms at all. That matters more than the split itself.

Bottom line: Do not agree to 50/50 yet. Agree to explore it, and say yes only after the business case, roles, vesting and exit terms are in writing and a lawyer has reviewed them.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Advocate's statement**

I do not know the business, the friend, or the money involved. So I will make the case for the version of this deal that is worth saying yes to.

**The upside**

A good co-founder is one of the biggest edges a new business can have. Two people can cover more ground than one. One can sell while the other builds. One can take a bad month without the whole thing stopping.

A friend also starts with trust. You already know how they act under stress, how they handle money, and whether they show up. A stranger co-founder takes a year to learn that about.

Splitting costs helps too. If startup cost is $20,000, each of you risks $10,000, not all of it. That lowers your personal risk and makes it easier to try.

Many strong companies began as two-person teams. The pattern is common enough that investors often prefer a pair to a solo founder. I can't give you an exact figure, and I would not trust anyone who does.

**Your advantages**

You are being asked. That means your friend sees something in you: skills, reliability, or contacts they lack. Find out exactly what they think you bring. That is your bargaining power.

**The best version of this**

- You each bring a different skill, not the same one.
- You both put in similar money and time.
- You sign a written agreement before any work starts.
- The agreement covers vesting, roles, exits, and deadlock.

Vesting matters most. A common setup is 4 years with a 1 year cliff. If your friend leaves after 6 months, they leave with nothing, not 50%.

**Where the upside is limited**

A 50/50 split is the weak point. If you disagree, nobody has the final say. Deadlock can freeze the business. A tie-break rule fixes much of this, such as a named decision owner for each area, or an agreed mediator.

Also, 50% of a small business is still small. If this is a side project that earns $30,000 a year, your half is $15,000 before tax. Be honest about how big the idea can really get.

**What I am unsure of**

- How much time each of you will put in.
- Whether your skills overlap or complement.
- Whether the friendship can survive a money fight. Some do not.

**What I would do**

Do not say yes or no yet. Say: "I'm interested. Let's write down roles, money in, time per week, vesting, and what happens if one of us quits." Then see how your friend reacts. A good partner will welcome that talk. If they dodge it, you have learned something cheaply.

Bottom line: Say yes to the idea of partnering, but not to a plain 50/50 handshake; agree only after you both sign a written deal with vesting, defined roles, and a deadlock rule.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Operator's statement**

I can't tell you yes or no yet. You gave me no business, no budget, no hours, and no friend's name. That is vague, and I won't plan around vague. Here is what I need, and what I'd do.

**What I don't know**
- What the business is.
- How much cash each of you puts in.
- How many hours per week each of you works.
- Whether you keep your day job.

**Why 50/50 worries me**
A 50/50 split has no tiebreaker. If you disagree on a big call, nothing moves. That is the usual way these partnerships stall. I'm not certain of the exact failure rate, so treat that as a pattern, not a statistic.

**Order of steps (do not skip ahead)**
1. **Week 1:** Write a one-page description. Product, customer, price, who does what. Owner: both of you.
2. **Week 2:** Test the idea cheaply. Talk to 10 potential customers. Cost: about $0 to $100. Time: 5 to 8 hours.
3. **Weeks 3 to 4:** Agree on terms in writing. Roles, cash in, hours in, what happens if one quits, how a buyout works. A lawyer review costs roughly $500 to $2,000 depending on where you live. I'm unsure of your local rates.
4. **Week 5:** Form the entity and open a business bank account. Cost: often $50 to $500 in filing fees, varies by place.
5. **Weeks 6 to 12:** Sell something. Get a first paying customer by day 90.

**Terms I'd insist on**
- Vesting: each of you earns your 50% over 4 years, with a 1-year cliff. If your friend leaves at month 6, they don't keep half.
- A deadlock rule, such as a named mediator or a buy-sell clause.
- Clear roles. One person decides in each area.

**Realistic numbers (assuming a small service or online business, which is a guess)**
- **3 months:** Spent $1,000 to $3,500 upfront. First customers, maybe $0 to $2,000 revenue. Not profitable.
- **6 months:** Revenue $1,000 to $6,000 per month if it's working. Many break even or lose money.
- **12 months:** Either $3,000 to $10,000 per month with a clear path, or a decision to stop. Neither of you pays yourself much, if anything.

These ranges are wide because I don't know the business. Treat them as placeholders.

**What doesn't fit**
If you work full time and can give under 10 hours per week, this will likely take 18 months or more to reach the numbers above. If your friend works 40 hours and you work 10, a 50/50 split is unfair to them and will cause resentment. Equity should match contribution.

**Deadline**
Don't answer your friend today. Give them a reply by **November 1, 2026**, after you finish steps 1 and 2.

Bottom line: Don't agree to 50/50 yet; agree to a two-week test and a written deal with vesting and a deadlock rule, and say yes only if the hours, cash and roles are clearly matched.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (Evidence)**

I can't tell you yes or no yet. The evidence says the 50/50 split is not the main risk. The missing paperwork is.

**What the evidence shows**

- **Even splits are common.** Techstars cites about 42% of two-founder teams splitting evenly ([Techstars](https://www.techstars.com/blog/founder-advice/how-to-split-co-founder-equity-the-right-way)). I didn't check the original data behind that figure. So 50/50 isn't strange.
- **Deadlock is the real danger.** Law-firm guides call a 50/50 split with no tiebreaker a design flaw. They suggest a casting vote, a neutral director, mediation, or a buy-sell clause ([Orrick](https://www.orrick.com/fr-FR/Insights/2026/04/Founder-Equity-Splits-Getting-it-right-from-day-one), [Echai Ventures](https://echai.ventures/startingup/equity-splits/how-do-we-handle-deadlock-when-two-co-founders-own-50-50-and-cannot-agree-on-a-major-decision)).
- **Courts are a poor exit.** In Delaware, a deadlocked 50/50 LLC has no dedicated dissolution statute. Courts borrow the corporate test, as in *Achaian v. Leemon Family LLC*, 25 A.3d 800 (Del. Ch. 2011). Dissolution is discretionary and granted sparingly ([Fox Rothschild](https://delawarechancery.foxrothschild.com/dissolution-2/dissolution-of-an-llc-with-two-5050-deadlocked-shareholders/)). Your state's rules may differ. I did not check them.
- **Vesting is standard.** Sources recommend four years with a one-year cliff, so 25% vests after year one ([Techstars](https://www.techstars.com/blog/founder-advice/how-to-split-co-founder-equity-the-right-way)). Without it, a partner who quits in month 3 keeps 50% for good.

**Claims that are false or shaky**

- "50/50 is always fair." Fair depends on who puts in the money, time, and skills. Equal shares don't make equal contributions.
- "We can fix it later." One blog claims about 73% of teams lock in their split within a month. I couldn't verify that source. Even so, changing a split later is hard, because the person giving up shares has to agree.
- "A handshake is enough." In a deadlock, you'd rely on a court to sort it out.

**What I could not find**

I did not find a primary study, such as Noam Wasserman's research, that ties 50/50 splits to higher failure rates. Most of what I found is practitioner advice, not hard data. Treat the deadlock warnings as sound reasoning, not proven statistics.

**What I don't know**

I don't know your friend, the business type, who funds it, who works full-time, or your state or country. Those facts matter more than the split.

**Questions to put to your friend**

1. Who works full-time, and who keeps a day job?
2. Who puts in how much money?
3. Will you both accept vesting?
4. Will you both accept a written tiebreaker and a buy-sell clause?

If your friend refuses any of the last two, that tells you something.

Bottom line: Agree to 50/50 only if you both sign a written agreement with four-year vesting, a deadlock tiebreaker, and a buy-out clause. Without those, say no for now.
