# Boardroom minutes: Our five-person events team runs on spreadsheets. Should we move to a CRM like H

Date: 2026-10-18 10:33

## Agenda

Our five-person events team runs on spreadsheets. Should we move to a CRM like HubSpot?

## Statements

### Skeptic (Risk and failure analysis)

**Skeptic's statement: spreadsheets to HubSpot**

I don't know why you want to move. That is my biggest gap. A CRM fixes specific pain. It does not fix a vague sense of being behind.

**Risks, ranked by likelihood and impact**

1. **Nobody uses it (high likelihood, high impact).** Five people with spreadsheet habits will keep the spreadsheets "just for now." You then pay for two systems and trust neither. This is the most likely way it dies.

2. **Wrong tool for events (high, high).** HubSpot is built for sales and marketing pipelines. Events work is different. You track venues, run-of-show, vendors, budgets, and attendee lists. If most of your spreadsheets do that, a CRM may not replace them. You might still need a project or event tool.

3. **Cost creep (high, medium).** The entry price looks small. The real bill comes from paid seats, add-ons, onboarding fees, and the tier that has the features you need. I don't have current 2026 pricing, so check it. Ask for the full annual cost for five users with the features you need, not the headline rate.

4. **Messy migration (medium, high).** Spreadsheets hold duplicates, inconsistent names, and free-text notes. Cleaning this can take weeks of work from people who also run live events. Importing dirty data gives you a dirty CRM.

5. **Bad timing (medium, high).** Rolling out during a peak event month risks a failed event. A failed event costs far more than the software.

6. **Data and legal exposure (medium, medium).** Attendee data falls under GDPR, CCPA, or similar rules, depending on where your guests live. You need consent records and a clear process for deletion requests. A CRM can help here, but only if it is set up properly. I'm not a lawyer, so confirm with one.

7. **Lock-in (low to medium, medium).** Exporting out later is possible but painful. Workflows you build inside the tool do not travel.

**Competition and alternatives**
Other tools exist for events and for lighter CRM needs, such as Airtable, Notion, or Pipedrive. I haven't compared them. The board should not treat HubSpot as the only option.

**What would have to be true for this to work**

- You can name two or three concrete problems the spreadsheets cause today. Examples are lost leads, double-booked contacts, or no view of sponsor follow-up.
- Most of your pain is about contacts and follow-up, not logistics.
- One person owns the rollout and has protected time. I'd guess 5 to 10 hours a week for the first 6 weeks, but that is an estimate.
- You agree a hard cutoff date after which the old spreadsheets go read-only.
- You run a trial on one real event or one pipeline first, outside peak season.
- The full annual cost is approved before you sign, with an exit option.

**What I'm unsure of**
Your team's actual pain, your event volume, your contact count, and today's HubSpot pricing and tiers.

Bottom line: Don't commit yet. Run a 30 to 60 day trial on one pipeline with a named owner and a cutoff date, and only buy if it clearly beats the spreadsheets on the specific problems you can name.

### Advocate (Opportunity and upside)

**Advocate statement: Move the events team to a CRM**

I think this is worth doing. A five-person team on spreadsheets is at the size where the pain is real but the switch is still cheap.

**Where the upside is**

1. **One record per contact.** Spreadsheets split people across tabs and files. A CRM gives one record for each sponsor, speaker or attendee, with every email, call and past event attached. A new teammate can read the history in minutes.

2. **Fewer dropped follow-ups.** Tasks and reminders replace "I thought you were emailing them." For events, this matters most with sponsors and VIPs. One missed renewal can cost more than a year of software.

3. **Reuse across events.** Last year's attendees become this year's warm list. Past sponsors get a ready-made renewal sequence. This is where the value compounds.

4. **Reporting without rebuilding.** Pipeline by event, sponsor revenue by quarter, and registration-to-attendance rates come from a dashboard, not a Friday of copy and paste.

5. **Room to grow.** HubSpot ties in email, forms, landing pages and event registration. If the team grows from 5 to 10, the system does not have to be rebuilt.

**Your advantages**

- Five people is small. You can train everyone in a week and agree on one process.
- Your spreadsheets are a ready map of what you track. That makes data migration and field setup easier.
- HubSpot has a free CRM tier and paid Starter plans. You can test before committing real money.

**Where the upside is limited**

- A CRM will not fix a messy process. It will only make the mess more visible.
- If you run two or three events a year with a few hundred contacts, the gain is smaller.
- Event logistics, such as run-of-show, vendor budgets and seating, often stay in spreadsheets or other tools. HubSpot is strong at relationships, weaker at logistics.
- Time savings are real but modest at first. Expect a dip in the first 4 to 8 weeks while people learn it.

**What I am unsure of**

- Your contact volume and how many events you run per year.
- Your budget. Paid HubSpot tiers with automation and event features can cost much more than the entry price. Check current pricing before deciding. I have not verified it today.
- Whether HubSpot is the best fit. Salesforce, Pipedrive or an event-specific tool may suit you better. I have not compared them.

**How I would do it**

Run a 60-day pilot on the free or Starter tier. Pick one upcoming event. Import only active sponsors and key contacts. Name one owner. Set a clear test: fewer missed follow-ups and less time spent on weekly reporting. If it fails, you lose a couple of weeks, not a year.

Bottom line: Yes, try it, but as a 60-day pilot on one event with one owner, and keep logistics in spreadsheets.

### Operator (Execution, numbers and timelines)

**Operator's statement**

Do not decide this on a feeling. Decide it on numbers. I don't have yours yet, so everything below is an estimate.

**First, the test.** Move only if the team loses more than 8 hours a week combined to spreadsheet problems. That means duplicate entries, lost version history, chasing who contacted which sponsor, and rebuilding attendee lists. Under 8 hours, fix the spreadsheets instead. Someone must log this for two weeks. That is 15 minutes a day per person.

**Plan, in order**
1. Weeks 1 to 2: Log the pain. Owner: you. About 3 hours of your time.
2. Week 3: Trial HubSpot's free CRM with one past event. Owner: one team lead. About 10 hours. Cost: $0.
3. Weeks 4 to 6: Clean the data. Merge duplicates and standardize columns. This is the real work. Budget 20 to 40 hours.
4. Weeks 7 to 10: Pilot on one live event with all 5 people. Keep the spreadsheet as backup.
5. Weeks 11 to 12: Cut over or stop. Set the decision date now.

**Costs (my figures may be out of date, so check HubSpot's pricing page)**
- Starter tier: roughly $20 per seat per month. For 5 seats that is about $100 a month, or $1,200 a year.
- Professional tier: roughly $90 to $100 per seat per month. That is about $500 a month, plus a one-time onboarding fee I recall at $1,500 or more.
- Your time: 60 to 80 hours in the first quarter.

**Realistic outcomes**
- 3 months: Net negative. Expect a productivity dip and about 40 to 60 hours spent. Savings are close to zero.
- 6 months: Roughly break-even if people actually use it.
- 12 months: If you save 4 hours a week, that is about 200 hours a year. At $40 an hour that is $8,000 of time against $1,200 to $7,500 in costs. The range is wide because the tier matters a lot.

**What does not fit**
- HubSpot is built for contacts and deals. It is not built for run-of-show, vendor logistics or event budgets. Expect to keep some spreadsheets. If your pain is mostly logistics, HubSpot will disappoint.
- If nobody on the team has 5 spare hours a week for two months, this will not get done. Do not start it in your busiest event season.
- If you have no named owner, it will stall by week 6. That is the usual failure.

**What I am unsure of**
- Current HubSpot prices and onboarding fees.
- Your event volume, your data quality and your team's habits.
- Whether an events-specific tool such as Cvent or Eventbrite fits better than a general CRM. I haven't compared them.

Dates: if you start this week (October 2026), the go or no-go falls in mid-January 2027. Watch for holiday slowdowns.

Bottom line: Not yet a yes. Spend 2 weeks logging lost hours and run the free trial on one event. Commit to a paid plan only if the team loses 8 or more hours a week, and name one owner and a January decision date first.

### Scout (Evidence and fact-checking)

**Statement from Scout (evidence)**

I could not reach HubSpot's own price page. The prices below come from third-party blogs, and they disagree with each other. Check them with HubSpot before you rely on them.

**What the evidence says**

- HubSpot's free CRM exists, so you can test it at no cost. I did not verify its current limits.
- Sales Hub Starter is reported at about $15 to $20 per seat per month. Sources differ ([Zeeg](https://zeeg.me/en/blog/post/hubspot-pricing), [Meetergo](https://meetergo.com/en/magazine/hubspot-pricing)).
- Sales Hub Professional is reported at about $100 per seat per month. It also carries a one-time onboarding fee of about $1,500 ([Chatarmin](https://chatarmin.com/en/blog/hubspot-pricing)).
- Marketing Hub Professional is reported at about $890 per month for three seats, plus a $3,000 onboarding fee. Contacts are metered, and you pay extra above the included amount ([Meetergo](https://meetergo.com/en/magazine/hubspot-pricing)).
- Paid plans reportedly need an annual commitment. Monthly billing reportedly adds 20 to 30%.

**Rough cost for five people**

- Starter: about $75 to $100 per month, or $900 to $1,200 per year.
- Professional Sales Hub: about $500 per month plus the $1,500 fee. That is roughly $7,500 in year one.
- If you add Marketing Hub, expect a much larger bill.

These are my own estimates from the reported prices. They are not quotes.

**Claims that are false or shaky**

- "A CRM is an events tool." It is not. A CRM manages relationships and sales pipeline. Registration and ticketing tools handle event logistics. One consultant says one does not automatically replace the other ([Insidea](https://insidea.com/blog/hubspot/crms-for-event-management-companies/)). Vendor guides support this view, but vendors have a stake in it.
- "HubSpot is cheap." The free and Starter tiers are cheap. The tiers with automation and reporting cost much more once you add onboarding fees and contact limits.
- "Zapier fixes the gap." Event platforms such as RSVPify sync attendees to HubSpot through Zapier ([RSVPify](https://rsvpify.com/event-management-crm/)). That works, but it adds another tool and another cost.

**A test worth running**

One source suggests checking that a CRM keeps a separate record for each event an attendee joins. If one person attends three events, you want three records, not one overwritten field. Ask HubSpot to show this with your own data. HubSpot can do it with custom objects, but I have not confirmed which tier includes them.

**What I'm unsure of**

- Exact current HubSpot prices and tier limits.
- Whether your pain is contact tracking, registration, budgets, or task handoffs. A CRM only fixes the first one well.
- Real switching costs for a five-person team. I found no solid data on this.

**Suggested next step**

Run the free CRM for 30 days. Import one past event's data and see if it holds up. Compare that against an event-specific tool.

Bottom line: A CRM is worth a cheap trial, but do not assume HubSpot replaces your event spreadsheets. Test the free tier with real data before paying for onboarding.
